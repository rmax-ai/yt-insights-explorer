Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Sait Izmit
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Deploying Go-To-Market (GTM) AI agents at scale and managing user trust, change management, and architectural iteration.
  • Purpose: To share practical engineering and product lessons, best practices, and pitfalls in deploying enterprise-grade AI assistants to sales and Go-To-Market teams. Sait Izmit, Principal Product Manager at Snowflake, shares lessons from deploying Raven (an internal GTM AI assistant powered by Snowflake CoWork) to 6,000 users, handling over 1.2 million questions. He discusses why agentic GTM matters, the critical importance of quality over coverage, change management lifecycles, and continuously re-architecting systems with evolving technology while leveraging data feedback loops.

Topic Map

Why Agentic GTM?

  • Explanation: Outlining why sales and GTM teams need AI agents: up to 50% of organization headcount is in sales/revenue generation, but teams suffer from siloed data, fragmented UI tools, and endless repetitive workflows.
  • Key claims:
    • GTM teams account for nearly half of enterprise headcount and are responsible for revenue generation.
    • Siloed first-party and third-party data across 15+ different tools creates massive operational drag.
    • AI agents offer data democratization, automation, and tool consolidation.
  • Examples:
    • Reps using 15 different tools and stitching data together in spreadsheets.
    • Reps managing 1,000 customer accounts and needing to stay on top of recent news, support tickets, and earnings results.
  • Terminology:
    • Go-To-Market (GTM)
    • Revenue Generation Accountability
    • Tool Consolidation
    • Data Democratization
  • Why it matters: Understanding the pain points of GTM organizations explains why agentic AI deployment can deliver significant productivity savings, better book coverage, increased win rates, shorter deal cycles, and incremental revenue.

Quality Over Coverage and User Trust

  • Explanation: Discussing the non-deterministic nature of LLM systems and why user trust is extremely hard to earn and lost overnight. Emphasizes the rule: 'Quality is P-1'.
  • Key claims:
    • User trust is extremely hard to earn and can be lost overnight.
    • A free-form chatbot where users can ask anything without quality checks will fail if users encounter poor answers early on.
    • It is better to answer fewer questions with 95% accuracy than many questions with 50% accuracy.
  • Examples:
    • An initial launch where engineering tried to answer 150 questions right away without data being ready, resulting in 50% accuracy and frustrated users.
  • Terminology:
    • Non-deterministic systems
    • Quality over coverage
    • Quality is P-1
  • Why it matters: First impressions matter immensely in enterprise AI adoption; burning user trust in the first five interactions makes winning them back nearly impossible.

Phased Launch Strategy: Pilot, Beta, GA

  • Explanation: The phased rollout approach used to manage user expectations, prove accuracy, test MVP feature depth, and drive activation.
  • Key claims:
    • Pilot phase (~50 users) focuses on quality and user trust, targeting >70% weekly active user retention.
    • Beta phase (~600 users) focuses on MVP feature depth, stickiness, questions per WAU, and retention rate.
    • GA phase (~6,000 users) focuses on awareness, activation, and weekly questions.
  • Examples:
    • Starting with 50 AI-native power users in the organization to iron out rough edges before broader rollout.
  • Terminology:
    • Pilot
    • Beta
    • GA (General Availability)
    • Weekly Active Users (WAU)
    • Retention Rate
  • Why it matters: Controlled rollouts prevent catastrophic system failures and build an initial base of advocates who validate the product before scaling.

Change Management in AI Deployments

  • Explanation: Highlighting that change management accounts for half of AI success, addressing low initial adoption rates and the 'collapse of the wow factor'.
  • Key claims:
    • Only 20% of an organization will naturally try a new product without active change management.
    • Users experience the 'collapse of the wow factor' a few months after launch as excitement fades into standard habits.
    • Organizations must continuously raise the bar and drive adoption through executive sponsorship, sales meetings, and demos.
  • Examples:
    • Spending 60-70% of time in sales meetings, giving demos, and shaming managers whose teams aren't adopting the tool.
  • Terminology:
    • Change Management
    • Innovation Adoption Lifecycle
    • Collapse of the Wow Factor
  • Why it matters: Even the best AI product will fail if management and users do not take the time to adopt and integrate it into their daily workflows.

Continuously Re-Architecting with Evolving Technology

  • Explanation: Explaining how the agent architecture evolved post-GA as new technology matured (skills library, MCP servers, user memory, progressive disclosures).
  • Key claims:
    • 80% of the initial 9-page agent instructions built in a Google Doc did not match the final architecture.
    • Teams must build fast with today's tech and be comfortable continuously re-architecting as new capabilities emerge.
    • Over 34-40% of ongoing engineering work is re-architecting with new technology.
  • Examples:
    • Moving from raw agent instruction docs to a structured skills library, Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers, and progressive disclosures.
  • Terminology:
    • Model Context Protocol (MCP)
    • Skills Library
    • Progressive Disclosures
    • Task Scheduler
    • User Memory
  • Why it matters: Rigid architectures become obsolete quickly; teams must be willing to get their hands dirty and pivot as AI technology evolves rapidly.

Creating Feedback Loops for Exponential Impact

  • Explanation: Using large and small LLMs to analyze 1.2M+ user logs and 40k weekly questions for topic modeling, surfacing feature gaps in real-time.
  • Key claims:
    • Logging user interactions creates a golden feedback loop for sales enablement and product roadmaps.
    • Using large compute LLMs to generate topic clusters and rules/regex, and small LLMs to label all data at scale.
    • Real-time visibility into feature gaps and user struggles cannot be achieved manually with humans.
  • Examples:
    • Analyzing 40,000 questions a week to instantly generate battle cards, sales enablement documents, and roadmap improvements.
  • Terminology:
    • Few-Shot Topic Modeling
    • Feedback Loops
    • Topic Clustering
  • Why it matters: Automated feedback loops allow product and engineering teams to identify user struggles and quality issues instantly, far outpacing manual user interviews.

Key Points

Quality over Coverage

  • Explanation: Prioritizing high accuracy on fewer questions over low accuracy across a broad question set.
  • Evidence: Focusing on answering 50 questions with 95% accuracy rather than 100 questions with 70% accuracy during pilot phases.
  • Practical implication: Protects early user trust and prevents permanent churn from bad initial experiences.

Change Management is Critical

  • Explanation: Overcoming the initial hurdle where only 20% of an organization naturally tries new internal AI tools.
  • Evidence: Requirement of active management, executive sponsorship, and sales leadership alignment during rollout.
  • Practical implication: Engineering teams must partner closely with go-to-market leaders to drive active adoption.

Continuous Re-architecting

  • Explanation: AI engineering requires constantly adapting architectures as new primitives (like MCP, skills, memory) emerge.
  • Evidence: 80% of original architecture changed within months post-GA as new tech was integrated.
  • Practical implication: Do not over-engineer the initial release; build fast and expect to iterate constantly.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Phased AI Rollout Framework

  • How it works: Gradually scaling user access while tightening accuracy, feature depth, and retention metrics.
  • Components:
    • Pilot (~50 users, focus on accuracy & user trust)
    • Beta (~600 users, focus on MVP feature depth & WAU)
    • GA (~6,000 users, focus on awareness & activation)
  • When to use: When deploying high-stakes enterprise AI assistants to business-critical teams.

Automated Feedback Loop Architecture

  • How it works: Capturing all user interaction logs and running topic modeling to extract feature gaps and product insights.
  • Components:
    • Large compute LLM for topic clustering & rules
    • Small LLM for labeling all data at scale
    • Integration into product roadmaps and sales enablement
  • When to use: When managing high-volume enterprise AI assistants with tens of thousands of weekly interactions.

[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltystrong evidence

Deliberately narrowing the agent's answerable question set to reach high accuracy at launch (rather than allowing free-form questions) is treated as the primary mechanism for earning user trust, and the initial failure mode was the inverse: answering broadly before the data layer was ready.

Why it matters

It reframes agent scope as a reliability parameter rather than a product aspiration: the set of questions the agent will answer (and implicitly route away or refuse) is a launch-critical design decision, and a first-launch accuracy miss appears to be effectively unrecoverable with enterprise users.

Generalization

For any non-deterministic agent facing cold-start users, constrain the capability surface to the subset with verified data/quality, and expand only after retention signals (e.g., weekly active retention) hold. Coverage is a later-phase metric, not a launch metric.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Agent behavior definition migrated from monolithic prose instructions (a 9-page Google Doc) to composable runtime artifacts: a skills library, MCP servers for tools/data, progressive disclosure of context, user memory, and a task scheduler.

Why it matters

It relocates the unit of agent capability from 'the prompt' to versioned, individually replaceable modules (skills, tool servers), which changes how teams test, review, and evolve behavior; progressive disclosure and user memory further imply explicit context-budget and per-user state management rather than a single global instruction blob.

Generalization

Treat agent instructions as disposable code with a high expected rewrite rate: plan for capability registries (skills), standardized tool interfaces (MCP), on-demand context loading, and per-user memory as separate architectural concerns.

[M]Mechanismmedium noveltymoderate evidence

A tiered LLM pipeline is used to turn production interaction logs into structured insight: large (expensive) models generate topic clusters and rules/regex once, and small models apply those labels to all data at scale.

Why it matters

It is a concrete cost-optimization pattern for high-volume log analysis (1.2M questions, ~40k/week): pay for frontier capability only on cluster/rule induction, then amortize labeling across cheap models. It also creates a machine-readable taxonomy that can drive both evaluation and content generation.

Generalization

Whenever an analysis must run over unbounded production traffic, split the work into a frontier-model 'induction' stage that produces a reusable schema (clusters, rules, regex, few-shot exemplars) and a small-model 'application' stage that executes it at scale.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Adoption is bounded by change management rather than product quality: only ~20% of an organization will self-serve a new tool, and enthusiasm decays ('collapse of the wow factor'), requiring continuous adoption pressure from leadership and demos.

Why it matters

Generalization

Treat adoption as a maintained service with a decay rate: measure baseline self-adoption, schedule recurring reinforcement (executive sponsorship, demos, per-team accountability), and expect usage to regress without it.

Scope-gating the answerable question set as the primary launch reliability mechanism

Research question

Does restricting an agent to a verified question set at launch produce higher weekly retention and long-run usage than a broad best-effort scope over identical data, and what procedure derives that scope boundary?

Why

If early low-accuracy answers burn trust irrecoverably, then the set of questions the agent is allowed to answer is the highest-leverage launch parameter — above model choice, UI, or tooling — and it requires a repeatable derivation method plus designed refusal/routing/fallback behavior on out-of-scope questions.

Source video ↗

Label fidelity and drift in the frontier-induction / small-model-application pipeline

Research question

When small models apply frontier-induced clusters, rules and regex to production logs, how much label agreement with frontier and human labels is retained, and how quickly does that agreement drift across successive log windows?

Why

The tiered pipeline is the cost structure that makes analysis of ~40k questions per week viable, but every downstream decision — roadmap prioritization, battle cards, enablement docs — inherits its label error. Without an agreement metric and a drift audit, the feedback loop can silently feed bad structure back into the product.

The standing re-architecture budget and designing components for replacement

Research question

Is a ~35% ongoing re-architecture allocation a stable operating cost for agent platforms or a transitional artifact of immature primitives, and does component-level replacement (skills, MCP servers, memory) deliver measurable task-success, latency or cost deltas?

Why

If a third or more of engineering capacity goes to replacing working architecture, then throwaway-ability, migration paths and per-component interfaces become primary design constraints rather than hygiene. The 80% mismatch between the original instruction document and final architecture becomes an expected planning outcome, not a failure.

Adoption decay as a maintained service rather than a launch campaign

Research question

Do recurring change-management interventions (executive sponsorship, demos, per-manager accountability) produce durable cohort retention, or only a promotional spike that decays back to the same baseline?

Why

If only ~20% of an organization self-adopts and enthusiasm collapses months after launch, the deployment has no natural steady state. The reinforcement effort then competes directly with build and evaluation time, so it must be modeled as an operating cost with a measured decay rate rather than treated as post-launch marketing.

Phase-specific metric sets as rollout gates for non-deterministic systems

Research question

Does gating rollout phases on different primary metrics (quality/retention, then depth/stickiness, then activation/awareness) prevent the failure mode of optimizing top-of-funnel activation while quality defects remain?

Why

It converts rollout staging from a user-count schedule into an evaluation design problem: the criteria themselves change with scale, and a phase cannot be exited on metrics that only matter later. Without this, teams import GA-style activation metrics into a pilot that is still failing on quality.

Project ideas

4

Scope Gate

gatehouse

An agent gated to a verified question set at launch achieves equal or higher 4-week weekly-active retention than an equally-resourced broad best-effort agent, without reducing total questions answered per active user.

Proof of concept

Run two 50-user pilots on identical data with an instrumented routing/refusal layer: arm A answers broadly and best-effort, arm B answers only classes whose underlying data passes a readiness check and explicitly refuses/routes the rest. Track per-question-class accuracy, refusal rate, and WAU retention weekly.

Measurement

Weekly active user retention, per-cluster answer accuracy, questions per WAU, and out-of-scope refusal behavior (do users rephrase, retry, or churn).

Source video ↗

Label Drift Watch

beyond-evals

Frontier-induced clusters/rules/regex applied by small models retain at least 90% label agreement with frontier labels only for a bounded number of log windows, and drift becomes detectable before it corrupts downstream roadmap and content outputs.

Proof of concept

Build the two-stage pipeline: a frontier model induces clusters and rules/regex once per window; small models label the full corpus. Each window, sample a held-out slice and re-label it with the frontier model and human annotators; plot agreement, cluster mass shift, and downstream artifact churn over successive windows.

Measurement

Agreement rate between small-model labels and frontier/human labels per window, cost per labeled query versus full frontier labeling, and the number of windows until agreement falls below threshold.

Source video ↗

Adoption Decay Tracker

movement-lab

Post-launch usage decays toward a stable baseline within roughly 90 days without reinforcement, and recurring per-manager reinforcement produces durable cohort retention rather than a promotional spike that decays to the same baseline.

Proof of concept

Instrument cohort retention curves segmented by whether a cohort's manager received reinforcement interventions (exec sponsorship, scheduled demos, accountability reviews). Compare cohorts that received a one-time launch push versus recurring reinforcement over matched windows.

Measurement

Cohort-level weekly retention curves before/during/after campaigns, time-to-baseline decay rate, fraction of cohorts whose usage persists above baseline after reinforcement stops, and reinforcement hours spent per retained weekly active user.

Source video ↗

Re-architecture ROI Ledger

new

Component-level architectural swaps (instruction doc to skills library, tool integration to MCP servers, added memory, progressive disclosure) each produce a measurable delta in task success, latency or cost, and the swap rate decays as primitives stabilize rather than remaining a permanent ~35% tax.

Proof of concept

Maintain a frozen evaluation suite run before and after each architectural swap, logging task success, latency, token/cost per task, and engineering hours consumed by the migration. Plot deltas and swap frequency over quarters.

Measurement

Pre/post deltas in task success, latency and cost per swap; engineering hours per swap; swap count per quarter and whether the trend decays or holds.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Coverage versus answer accuracy at launch

Benefit

Broad question coverage maximizes apparent utility and perceived agent capability.

Cost or risk

Low accuracy on unfunded question classes burns user trust early, which the speaker frames as effectively unrecoverable; the observed result was 50% accuracy and frustrated users.

Source video ↗

Continuous re-architecture versus architectural stability

Benefit

Captures newly matured capabilities (MCP, skills, memory, progressive disclosure) instead of shipping on obsolete patterns.

Cost or risk

A large share of engineering capacity (34-40%) is spent replacing architecture, and substantial prior work is discarded (80% of initial instructions unmatched by the final design), creating recurring migration and revalidation cost.

Source video ↗

Executive-driven change management versus product engineering time

Benefit

Sustains adoption beyond the ~20% of users who self-adopt and counters post-launch enthusiasm decay.

Cost or risk

Consumes the majority of the team's working time (60-70% in meetings, demos, and per-manager accountability), time not spent on building or evaluating the system.

Source video ↗

Frontier-model induction versus small-model labeling at scale

Benefit

Frontier-quality taxonomy (topic clusters, rules/regex) at a cost structure viable for tens of thousands of queries per week.

Cost or risk

Label fidelity at scale depends on the small model applying induced rules correctly; the summary provides no measured label accuracy for this stage, so downstream roadmap and enablement decisions inherit any drift.
