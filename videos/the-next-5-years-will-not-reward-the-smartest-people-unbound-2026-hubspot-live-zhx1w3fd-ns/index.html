Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Jay Schwedelson
  • Channel: HubSpot Live
  • Main topic: How consistency, accountability, and action outperform pure intelligence in the next 5 years.
  • Purpose: To inspire professionals to stop waiting, hold themselves accountable, be consistent, and leverage AI differently to achieve success. Jay Schwedelson argues that in a world where AI and information are easily accessible, the next five years will not reward the smartest people, but rather those who are consistent, accountable, and take action. He shares personal anecdotes about his own college struggles, overcoming negative self-talk, and leveraging AI in unconventional ways, emphasizing that accountability and doing things consistently are the keys to career and personal success.

Topic Map

The Myth of Intelligence

  • Explanation: Intelligence and academic credentials (like GPAs or degrees) don't matter as much as people think because information is widely available and AI can handle complex reasoning.
  • Key claims:
    • The next five years will not reward the smartest people.
    • Degrees and GPAs don't matter in the real world.
    • AI has already won the intelligence contest.
  • Examples:
    • Jay's college transcript showing multiple attempts at statistics and a C in advertising strategy.
  • Terminology:
    • GPA
    • AI
    • Credentials
  • Why it matters: It shifts focus away from feeling inadequate due to lack of traditional credentials toward taking action.

Accountability and Learning by Doing

  • Explanation: Learning happens by doing, and accountability forces learning to be non-optional. You don't need motivation; you need something due.
  • Key claims:
    • Accountability makes learning non-optional.
    • You don't need more motivation; you need something due.
    • People learn by doing, not just by doing their job.
  • Examples:
    • Running a podcast or a weekly newsletter to force weekly learning.
  • Terminology:
    • Accountability
    • Motivation
    • Homework
  • Why it matters: Without forced deadlines and accountability, professionals repeat the same tasks without actual growth.

Consistency Beats Intelligence

  • Explanation: Being consistent beats 95% of people because the smartest people often stop when things get hard or busy.
  • Key claims:
    • You will beat out 95% of people just by being consistent.
    • Consistency is the secret to winning.
  • Examples:
    • Podcast drop-off rates: 47% don't make it past episode 12.
  • Terminology:
    • Consistency
    • Dropout rate
  • Why it matters: Most people stop after a few weeks; consistency requires simply not stopping.

Ten Seconds of Bravery

  • Explanation: You don't need to be fearless forever; you just need to borrow bravery for ten seconds to take action.
  • Key claims:
    • You don't have to be fearless forever; you just need to push for ten seconds.
    • You need to temporarily borrow bravery.
  • Examples:
    • Ten seconds to hit send, introduce yourself, walk across a room, or post the thing.
  • Terminology:
    • Bravery
    • Fearlessness
  • Why it matters: Overcoming anxiety and taking small bold actions changes career trajectories.

Using AI Differently

  • Explanation: AI can be used in unconventional, highly specific ways to drive business and career growth, such as analyzing LinkedIn databases or creating a fictional board of directors.
  • Key claims:
    • We need to use AI differently.
    • Downloading LinkedIn databases and prompting AI to find underutilized connections.
    • Creating a board of directors of historical figures powered by AI.
  • Examples:
    • Asking AI to create a board of directors modeled after Steve Jobs, Jeff Bezos, Mark Cuban, etc.
  • Terminology:
    • AI prompts
    • LinkedIn database
  • Why it matters: Simple, 5-minute AI prompts can drastically accelerate professional networking and decision-making.

The Danger of Negative Self-Talk and Four Toxic Words

  • Explanation: Four tiny words—still, only, should, but—do a ridiculous amount of damage to a fantastic life.
  • Key claims:
    • Still, only, should, but are the worst words in human language.
    • Nobody feels like they're enough.
  • Examples:
    • 'I still make less money...', 'I only got promoted because...', 'I should be happier...', 'I am... but...'
  • Terminology:
    • Negative self-talk
    • Imposter syndrome
  • Why it matters: Recognizing and eliminating these words stops self-doubt and allows people to realize they are enough.

Key Points

AI won the intelligence contest

  • Explanation: Intelligence alone is no longer a competitive advantage because AI and information are universally accessible.
  • Evidence: AI can process vast amounts of data and answer complex queries instantly.
  • Practical implication: Focus on execution, consistency, and human accountability rather than accumulating credentials.

Accountability creates growth

  • Explanation: Forcing yourself to produce regular outputs (newsletters, podcasts, team updates) creates forced learning.
  • Evidence: Jay maintains a weekly podcast specifically because it forces him to research and learn new things every week.
  • Practical implication: Establish weekly public or team commitments that force you to learn and adapt.

Consistency beats talent

  • Explanation: Most people quit initiatives after a few weeks. Sticking with something long-term puts you ahead of 95% of people.
  • Evidence: Statistics show massive drop-off rates in podcasts, budgeting apps, and journaling within weeks or months.
  • Practical implication: Commit to long-term consistency instead of seeking instant gratification.

Ten seconds of bravery is enough

  • Explanation: Fear holds people back, but courage only needs to be sustained for brief moments.
  • Evidence: Actions like hitting send or introducing yourself only take ten seconds of courage.
  • Practical implication: Break intimidating tasks down into ten-second micro-actions of bravery.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Weekly Things (Accountability Framework)

  • How it works: Assigning regular weekly tasks to yourself and your team to ensure continuous learning and sharing.
  • Components:
    • 5 minutes: Share 1 industry article in team Slack
    • 10 minutes: Bring 1 competitor move to team meeting
    • 20 minutes: Send boss 3 things learned this week
    • 30 minutes: Ask to email entire department 3 things happening in industry
    • 45 minutes: Test 1 new tool, prompt, tactic
    • 60 minutes: 10 minutes of weekly department meeting sharing changes
    • The Big One: Publish something every single week
  • When to use: To maintain team alignment, continuous learning, and personal accountability.

AI Board of Directors Prompting

  • How it works: Feed an AI system the public philosophies and thinking styles of 6 historical or business icons, then pressure-test personal or business decisions against them.
  • Components:
    • Perfectionist (Steve Jobs)
    • Operator (Jeff Bezos)
    • Contrarian (Mark Cuban)
    • Brand Builder (Emma Grede)
    • Provocateur (Rory Sutherland)
    • Founder (Sara Blakely)
  • When to use: When making major personal, career, or business decisions that require diverse perspectives.

Examples & Case Studies

Jay struggled through college statistics three times, earning a C+, but went on to run an advertising agency.

  • Illustrates: Academic intelligence and grades do not dictate professional success.
  • Lesson: Your beginning doesn't get a vote on your ending.

Jay downloaded his LinkedIn connections database and prompted AI to find the 25 most underutilized connections.

  • Illustrates: Leveraging simple AI workflows on existing digital assets to drive career and business opportunities.
  • Lesson: Use AI to unlock dormant relationships and hidden value in existing tools.

Jay tried Wegovy (weight loss medication) expecting effortless results, ate everything in sight, and gained weight.

  • Illustrates: Tools and shortcuts don't replace active effort and trying.
  • Lesson: You have to do the work; there are no magic shortcuts.

A decision can be pressure-tested by prompting a model with fixed, differentiated expert personas (perfectionist, operator, contrarian, brand builder, provocateur, founder) that each supply the public philosophies and thinking style of a named figure, producing a panel rather than a single answer.

Why it matters

This is a concrete recipe for role-differentiated critique: the diversity lives in the persona specification layer, not in the model, so it can be implemented as an orchestration pattern over one base model and reused for red-teaming plans, prompts, or architecture decisions.

Generalization

Persona-differentiated panels are a cheap substitute for heterogeneous models when building critique, review, or red-team stages; the value depends on whether the disagreement is substantive or merely stylistic.

Persona Panel A/B Harness

beyond-evals

A fixed N-persona panel will surface decision-relevant objections that a single strong-model pass misses, measured by blind judge revision rate and substantive-objection classification, without collapsing into stylistic variation.

Proof of concept

Run the same decision memo through one baseline prompt and a six-persona panel (perfectionist, operator, contrarian, brand builder, provocateur, founder); classify each objection as substantive or stylistic and have blind raters select the more actionable critique.

Measurement

Decision revision rate, substantive-objection count per dollar, blind preference, and persona agreement entropy across 50 decisions.

Source video ↗

Survival-to-N Agent Reliability Metric

beyond-evals

Scheduled agent jobs exhibit a survival curve where most failures or abandonments happen before N cycles, and survival-to-N predicts downstream utility better than per-step accuracy.

Proof of concept

Instrument a repeated weekly research or summarization job; log cycle completions, aborts, and artifact quality; plot survival curves for 100 scheduled runs.

Measurement

Median cycles survived, survival-to-10 rate, and correlation between survival and cumulative artifact reuse.

Source video ↗

Forcing-Function vs On-Demand Knowledge Artifacts

movement-lab

A mandatory recurring-output workflow produces more cumulative reusable knowledge artifacts than an on-demand workflow over the same task period, even when total token budget is equal.

Proof of concept

Compare two agent workflows: one must produce a weekly synthesis artifact, one runs only when prompted; both start from the same corpus and token budget.

Measurement

Artifact count, retrieval reuse count, downstream task success on held-out queries, and operator-perceived value.

Source video ↗

Redacted Graph Grounding Pipeline

gatehouse

Field-level redaction and retention limits on an exported relational graph preserve most underutilized-connection signal while reducing privacy exposure.

Proof of concept

Prototype a local pipeline that ingests a synthetic LinkedIn-like connection export, applies field-level redaction, and prompts a model to surface dormant connections; compare against an unredacted baseline.

Measurement

Precision and recall of surfaced dormant connections, sensitive-field leakage count, and latency overhead.

Architectural implications

2

The speaker describes assembling a panel of named archetype personas (perfectionist, operator, contrarian, brand builder, provocateur, founder) to review decisions.

Before

A single model response is treated as the evaluation signal.

After

Evaluation responsibility moves into an orchestration layer that instantiates multiple fixed persona critics over the same artifact and aggregates or contrasts their outputs.

Consequence

Persona definitions, ordering, and aggregation become first-class, versioned configuration; cost and latency scale with panel size, so the trade-off between critique diversity and budget becomes an explicit design parameter.

After

A scheduling/commitment layer with required recurring output artifacts sits between the system and its data, forcing periodic ingestion and synthesis.

Consequence

Multi-persona review panels

Benefit

Cost or risk

The summary provides no outcome evidence that panel diversity improves decisions; the risk is additive cost, latency, and more opinions without added signal, plus persona outputs collapsing toward agreement.
