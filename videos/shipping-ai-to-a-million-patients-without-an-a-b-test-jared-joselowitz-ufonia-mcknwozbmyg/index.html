Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Jared Joselowitz
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Evaluating and testing AI agents in healthcare safely without traditional software deployment methods like A/B testing.
  • Purpose: To demonstrate how high-stakes autonomous AI systems in healthcare can be safely stress-tested, evaluated, and deployed using automated simulations and expert-level judges. Jared Joselowitz from Ufonia explains how to safely deploy clinical conversational AI agents like Dora to hundreds of thousands of patients without relying on traditional software practices like A/B testing on live patients, unrolling phone calls, or relying solely on static model cards. By presenting the MATRIX simulation framework and automated prompt optimization using GEPA and BehJudge, Ufonia proves product safety before real patients ever interact with the AI.

Key Points

Healthcare AI cannot use standard software testing playbooks

  • Explanation: Traditional methods like A/B testing on live patients, rolling back after patient harm, or relying on static model cards are unethical, illegal, or ineffective in medical contexts.
  • Evidence: You cannot unsend a phone call or randomize patients into a worse variant.
  • Practical implication: Teams building high-stakes AI must build rigorous offline safety stacks before deployment.

MATRIX replaces human actors with simulated patient agents

  • Explanation: To scale testing rapidly across thousands of hazardous scenarios, Ufonia uses an LLM-based simulated patient (PatBot) grounded in real clinical workflows.
  • Evidence: PPI studies showed simulated patient conversations scored similarly or more realistic than human-to-human clinical transcripts.
  • Practical implication: Use domain-conditioned AI agents to simulate user behavior in high-stakes testing.

BevJudge automates expert-level safety grading

  • Explanation: An LLM judge evaluates simulated dialogues against hazard criteria, achieving sensitivity on par with or exceeding human clinicians.
  • Evidence: BevJudge achieved 0.999 sensitivity and 0.96 F1 score across 240 clinician-evaluated examples.
  • Practical implication: Automated judges with tailored cost functions can replace manual review bottlenecks.

Automated Prompt Optimization replaces manual prompt engineering

  • Explanation: GEPA uses evaluation feedback and cost matrices to automatically optimize prompts, drastically outperforming manual trial-and-error.
  • Evidence: Formatting changes can swing benchmarks by 76 percentage points; manual tuning cannot keep pace.
  • Practical implication: Adopt automated prompt optimization tools driven by quantified domain-specific cost functions.

Simulation earns the right to test on real patients

  • Explanation: Simulation is the inner loop (fast, safe, thousands of runs), while real patients are the outer loop (slow, costly, the ultimate proof).
  • Evidence: Every call, dataset, pinned prompt, and judge verdict traces back to the hazard behind it.
  • Practical implication: You don't ship the model; you ship the evidence.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Write down your domain-specific hazards and define what 'harm' means for your product.
    • Build or adopt automated prompt optimization tools like GEPA instead of manual prompt tuning.
    • Establish cost matrices that weight different types of errors appropriately rather than relying on flat accuracy scores.
  • Strategic:
    • Treat simulation as the inner loop to earn the right to test on real users in the outer loop.
    • Ship the evidence (traceable test results, judge verdicts, audit trails) rather than just the model.
    • Scale safety frameworks across new modalities (like voice) by treating new hazards with the same simulation methodology.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • How do we construct comprehensive hazard corpora for our specific domain?
    • What is the optimal balance between automated judge sensitivity and false escalation rates?
    • How can we further diversify simulated patient personas to capture edge-case patient demographics?

Core insights

7
[A]Architecturehigh noveltystrong evidence

Ufonia replaces online experimentation with a closed-loop offline safety stack: an LLM simulated patient (MATRIX/PatBot) generates hazardous clinical conversations, an LLM expert judge (BevJudge) scores them against expected behaviours and hazard definitions, and failed outputs feed an automated prompt optimizer (GEPA) that rewrites the system prompt — so no real patient is ever exposed to an unvalidated variant. The deployment gate is a regression suite of simulated hazards rather than live traffic.

Why it matters

It is a concrete, shipping-at-scale counterexample to the default 'ship it and A/B test' playbook for irreversible-action agents. The loop (simulate → judge → optimize → re-simulate) is the replaceable unit of deployment governance, not the model card.

Generalization

Any agent whose actions are irreversible or unethical to randomize (outbound calls, payments, clinical advice, legal notices, destructive tool calls) needs an offline simulation-plus-judge gate as the primary release mechanism, and must treat 'can we roll back?' as a design question rather than a deployment afterthought.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

The optimization objective is an explicit asymmetric cost matrix, not a scalar accuracy metric: different mistakes carry different penalties (missing a red flag versus mildly annoying a patient), and GEPA optimizes prompts against those weighted penalties plus evaluation results. Harm taxonomy is therefore encoded into the optimizer's loss, not buried in prose in the prompt.

Why it matters

Most prompt-optimization work optimizes a uniform pass rate. Encoding domain harm weights changes which behaviours the optimizer is willing to trade away, and makes the trade-off reviewable by clinicians rather than implicit in a prompt author's taste.

Generalization

For any agent operating under asymmetric risk, the eval harness should expose a cost matrix as a first-class, versioned artifact that the optimizer optimizes against — the same artifact doubles as the documentation a regulator or incident reviewer reads.

Source video ↗
[E]Empirical Resultmedium noveltystrong evidence

The judge is deliberately tuned for sensitivity rather than balanced accuracy: 0.999 sensitivity with F1 0.96 means the judge over-calls hazards rather than risk under-calling them, and the judge's threshold is treated as a safety margin against downstream harm.

Why it matters

It makes explicit that an LLM judge's operating point is a policy decision tied to the cost of the two error types, not a neutral measurement. Teams copying LLM-as-judge patterns with default thresholds are implicitly choosing a false-negative rate they never reasoned about.

Generalization

Calibrate automated evaluators to the asymmetric error cost of the domain (favour recall when misses are catastrophic), and report sensitivity separately from F1 so the reviewer can see which direction the judge errs.

Source video ↗
[P]Practicehigh noveltymoderate evidence

The simulator is itself an object under test. Simulated patients are conditioned on specific clinical scenarios and workflows rather than abstract situations, and their realism is validated against real patient-doctor transcripts through Patient and Public Involvement studies before simulated evidence is trusted.

Why it matters

If the synthetic patient is not representative, every downstream evaluation number is fiction. Treating the generator as a validated component (with its own ground-truth comparison) is the discipline that separates a useful simulation harness from a demo.

Generalization

Any synthetic-data-driven evaluation pipeline needs a validation step for the data generator itself — a fidelity benchmark against captured real interactions — otherwise the harness optimizes against the simulator's quirks.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Changing modality rotates the hazard taxonomy but not the harness architecture: moving from text to voice introduces new failure modes (backchanneling, patient cutting in mid-safety-advice with out-of-scope questions) while the simulate → judge → optimize framework stays identical.

Why it matters

It suggests a stable seam: a portable scenario/hazard catalog plus a portable judge interface, with modality-specific adapters supplying new hazards and interaction traces. Ports are cheap when failures are data, not code.

Generalization

Design agent safety harnesses around a modality-agnostic hazard catalog and transcript/judgment contract; adding a new interface (voice, vision, GUI control) should mean adding hazard entries and trace adapters, not rebuilding evaluation.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltystrong evidence

Regulatory status is triggered by functional capability, not by model type: because Dora asks about symptoms and gives clinical advice, it is a medical device and must answer three questions — what the software does, what could go wrong, and how you ensure it does not. The shipping artifact becomes evidence rather than a model card.

Why it matters

Reframes what the engineering team must produce: a hazard enumeration plus per-hazard evidence, generated by the harness. Model cards and benchmark percentages are explicitly framed as non-defensible in a post-incident review.

Generalization

In any regulated or high-liability domain, the deliverable of the eval pipeline is an auditable hazard-to-evidence mapping; plan the telemetry and test artifacts to serve that document, not just internal quality dashboards.

[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Prompt engineering is moved out of the human editor and into the eval loop: instead of subjective, non-reproducible manual iteration, GEPA reflects on failed outputs and updates prompts automatically within minutes, keeping humans as reviewers of a gated deploy rather than authors of each change.

Why it matters

Prompts become build artifacts produced by an optimizer against measured criteria, so a failing production pattern can be converted into a scenario, then into a prompt change, without a human rewriting prose by intuition.

Generalization

Where an automated, scored evaluation exists, treat prompt authoring as an optimization problem inside CI; where it does not, the honest first investment is the evaluation, not the optimizer.

Project ideas

4

Judge Drift Sentinel

beyond-evals

A judge's measured sensitivity will drop materially after a judge-model or rubric upgrade unless it is re-certified on a frozen clinician-labelled set; a per-hazard drift monitor will detect the drop before the deployment gate does.

Proof of concept

Build a small labelled hazard set from clinician-reviewed examples, version the judge and rubric, simulate upgrades, and compute per-hazard sensitivity/F1 against the frozen labels.

Measurement

Sensitivity delta per hazard, false-positive rate, and detection latency after a seeded judge regression.

Source video ↗

Hazard-to-Evidence Gate

gatehouse

A gate driven by a single versioned hazard catalog shared by scenario generation and judging will catch more injected regressions per release than aggregate pass-rate testing and will produce an auditable coverage number.

Proof of concept

Implement 20-30 hazards for a reversible test agent, generate scenarios, judge outcomes, inject prompt regressions, and compare per-hazard detection against an aggregate eval.

Measurement

Regression catch rate, per-hazard coverage, false-alarm rate, and time to produce evidence for a release.

Source video ↗

Simulator Fidelity Bench

movement-lab

Simulated-patient transcripts diverge from real transcripts on distress signalling and topic derailment, and the divergence changes agent pass rates on the same hazard set.

Proof of concept

Compare synthetic and captured transcripts on behavioural dimensions, then run the same agent and judge on matched simulated and real subsets.

Measurement

Distribution distance on behavioural dimensions and pass-rate gap between simulated and real transcripts.

Source video ↗

Optimizer Exploit Test

beyond-evals

GEPA-style prompt optimization improves scores on the training hazard set but can degrade on withheld clinician-authored hazards; the train/hold-out gap predicts harness exploitation.

Proof of concept

Split scenarios into train and hold-out sets, run several optimization rounds against the cost matrix, and evaluate on hold-out and judge-blind hazards.

Measurement

Train-hold-out score gap, regression on withheld hazards, and judge-blind agreement.

Architectural implications

5

The release gate is a library of simulated hazardous scenarios scored by an automated judge rather than a canary or A/B split in live traffic.

Before

Ship behind a flag, expose a fraction of live patients, compare conversion or outcome metrics, roll back on regression; rely on model cards and benchmark percentages for prior assurance.

After

Every candidate prompt/model change runs through a versioned hazard-scenario suite with a simulation agent and a calibrated judge; the gate passes on per-hazard evidence with an asymmetric cost score.

Consequence

The eval harness becomes production infrastructure with its own versioning, scenario ownership, and clinician-labeling workflow; model changes are gated on harness results, and the harness's own regressions become a release risk.

Source video ↗

The hazard catalog is the shared contract between the generator and the judge — it seeds the simulated patient and defines the oracle's pass/fail criteria.

Before

Test data and expected results live in separate places; expectation drift is invisible and coverage is undeclared.

After

A single enumerated hazard list (missed red flags, hallucination, ignored distress, non-disclosure of AI status) drives both scenario synthesis and judge rubrics, making coverage measurably complete or incomplete.

Consequence

Coverage becomes an auditable number ('which hazards are unrepresented in the suite?') and adding a new hazard is a data change rather than a code change.

Source video ↗

The LLM judge is a separately versioned, separately calibrated component with its own ground-truth set, not an inline assertion.

Before

Assertions in test code, or aggregate pass rates, with no measured agreement against expert human judgment.

After

A judge model whose agreement (sensitivity, F1) against clinician-labelled examples is tracked and re-validated whenever the judge or rubric changes.

Consequence

Judge upgrades are releases in their own right; a judge regression can silently pass a bad agent, so judge invariance tests and frozen clinician-labelled sets are required.

Source video ↗

Prompt optimization runs inside the loop against the measured cost matrix, so prompt changes are generated artifacts.

Before

An engineer edits prompt text by intuition, reviews the diff with a colleague, and redeploys.

After

An optimizer proposes prompt revisions from failed simulated outputs, scored against a predefined metric and cost matrix, then surfaces for gated approval.

Consequence

Need provenance for every prompt revision (which failures motivated it, which metrics moved), plus a hold-out scenario set to detect optimization that exploits the harness rather than improving the agent.

Source video ↗

Modality support is an adapter layer over a stable scenario/judgment contract.

Before

Each interface (text chat, voice) gets its own ad-hoc QA approach because the interaction surface differs.

After

Voice adds failure modes such as backchanneling and mid-safety-advice interruptions as new hazard entries and new trace formats, reusing the same simulate-and-judge pipeline.

Consequence

Evaluation infrastructure amortizes across modalities; the marginal cost of a new channel is hazard enumeration and trace capture, not a new harness.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

No live experimentation

Benefit

Eliminates the ethical and legal hazard of randomizing patients into a worse agent variant and avoids irreversible harm from live calls.

Cost or risk

Forfeits the causal ground truth of live traffic; the team must substitute simulated evidence plus clinician-labelled ground truth, which is expensive and only as good as the simulator.

Source video ↗

Simulation as the safety argument

Benefit

Scales testing across thousands of hazardous clinical scenarios without real patients, ethically and quickly.

Cost or risk

Simulation cannot certify reality; it only earns the right to careful real-world exposure, so a residual transfer gap and a staged real-patient rollout remain mandatory.

Source video ↗

Judge tuned for recall

Benefit

0.999 sensitivity means hazards are almost never under-called, which is the correct emphasis where a missed red flag is catastrophic.

Cost or risk

F1 of 0.96 implies non-trivial false positives relative to sensitivity; over-called hazards create triage burden and can mask genuine regressions in a flood of alarms.

Source video ↗

Optimizing against predefined metrics

Benefit

Automated prompt optimization removes subjective, non-reproducible manual prompt engineering and produces improvements within minutes rather than days.

Cost or risk

Any hazard not encoded in the metrics or cost matrix is invisible to the optimizer, so the loop can improve measured behaviour while leaving unmodelled failure modes untouched or worse.

Source video ↗

Evidence-first shipping

Benefit

A per-hazard evidence artifact answers what the software does, what could go wrong, and how that is prevented — the questions a regulator or post-incident review actually asks.

Cost or risk

Model cards and benchmark percentages, however good, do not discharge that burden, so each shipped change requires producing and maintaining evidence rather than citing a static document.
