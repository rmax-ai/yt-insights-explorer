Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Varun Shenoy
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: AI Diffusion and Enterprise Transformation
  • Purpose: To share an AI engineer's perspective on how to successfully deploy AI into real-world economically relevant tasks through extreme software-service co-design. Varun Shenoy, co-founder of Long Lake, discusses the challenges and strategies of diffusing AI into the real world, particularly within traditional services and property management enterprises. Drawing parallels to the adoption of electricity and the automobile, he argues that AI diffusion takes a generation and requires owning outcomes rather than acting as a traditional software vendor. He outlines lessons learned from acquiring and transforming real services businesses, spanning copilot-to-coworker transitions, leveraging real-world enterprise data, and establishing learning loops.

Topic Map

The Challenge of AI Diffusion

  • Explanation: While AI models are improving rapidly, deploying them into real-world economically relevant tasks remains difficult.
  • Key claims:
    • Everyone has seen the magic demos, but real-world enterprises have not changed yet.
    • AI diffusion mirrors general-purpose technologies like electricity, which took decades to reshape factories.
    • AI diffusion is the single most important problem for the next 20 years.
  • Examples:
    • Edison's Pearl Street Station demo of electricity in the 1880s vs. Ford factories reorganizing around electric moving assembly lines in the 1920s.
  • Terminology:
    • AI diffusion
    • general-purpose technology
  • Why it matters: Understanding diffusion prevents unrealistic expectations of immediate enterprise transformation and emphasizes the long-term nature of operational change.

Long Lake's Operating Model

  • Explanation: Long Lake acquires and partners with real-world services businesses (HOA management, architecture, HR services) rather than just selling software.
  • Key claims:

    • They recently announced a $6.3B take-private of American Express Global Business Travel.
    • The solution to AI diffusion is to own outcomes: when AI doesn't work, it's our problem, not the vendor's.
  • Examples:
    • Acquiring and operating 35+ property management, HOA, architecture, and HR businesses.
    • Taking private American Express Global Business Travel.
  • Terminology:

  • Why it matters: Demonstrates that owning the end business forces alignment with actual operational outcomes and guarantees real-world testing environments.

Three Lessons from AI Transformation

      1. Leveraging Real World Data.
      1. Learning Loops Inside the Enterprise.
  • Examples:
    • Transitioning from chat-based copilots to synchronous and asynchronous agents and proactive coworkers.
  • Terminology:

  • Why it matters: Provides a roadmap for moving up the autonomy ladder in enterprise software.

From Copilots to Coworkers

  • Explanation: The spectrum of AI agent autonomy ranging from copilots to async agents to full coworkers.
  • Key claims:
    • Copilots offer instant in-the-moment assistance.
    • Sync agents provide real-time, two-way interaction (e.g., coding agents on desktop).
    • Async agents trigger and respond when ready, working independently over minutes, hours, or days.
    • Coworkers are proactive partners that get work done with you, but you must earn the right to do more by iterating in the field.
  • Examples:
    • Coding agent on desktop vs. coding agent in sandbox running tests and submitting PRs.
  • Terminology:
    • copilot
    • sync agent
    • async agent
    • coworking agent
    • parallelize work
  • Why it matters: Highlights that enterprises cannot jump straight to autonomous coworkers without building trust and capability incrementally.

Leveraging Real World Data

  • Explanation: The most valuable knowledge and operational data do not live on the internet.
  • Key claims:
    • Frontier models learn from humanity's written text, but business-critical tasks involve incomplete data, proprietary scoping, and vendor coordination.
    • Enterprise knowledge lives in people's heads and 20-year-old software.
    • The AI flywheel requires capturing real work traces, evaluating outcomes against ground truth, and improving models.
  • Examples:
    • Closing books with incomplete data, scoping a building construction project from a blueprint, coordinating vendors for a broken roof.
  • Terminology:
    • ground truth
    • real-world evals
    • rich traces
    • out of distribution
  • Why it matters: Explains why general frontier models fail at niche enterprise workflows without proprietary data integration.

Learning Loops Within the Enterprise

  • Explanation: Combining continual learning and user enablement into a single core loop where usage drives agent improvement.
  • Key claims:
    • Continual learning and user enablement are traditionally owned by separate silos (research vs. growth/customer success).
    • The agent only improves if people use it, and people only use it if it's worth adopting.
    • Getting a 100-year-old firm to change processes is hard and requires extreme software-service co-design under the same roof.
  • Examples:
    • Visiting companies, doing lunch and learns, mountain biking with clients, and co-designing software alongside their daily operations.
  • Terminology:
    • continual learning
    • enablement
    • snowball effect
    • extreme software-service co-design
  • Why it matters: Proves that AI deployment is not just a technical challenge but an organizational and cultural integration challenge.

Key Points

AI Diffusion takes a generation

  • Explanation: General-purpose technologies require deep organizational restructuring and equipment replacement, similar to electricity and assembly lines.
  • Evidence: Edison's 1880s power station vs. Ford's 1920s moving assembly line adoption.
  • Practical implication: Do not expect instant enterprise transformation just because foundation models are powerful.

Own the outcomes, don't just sell software

  • Explanation: When operating businesses directly, failures of AI are internal operational problems rather than vendor bugs.
  • Evidence: Long Lake acquiring 35+ real services businesses and a major corporate travel platform.
  • Practical implication: Ultimate accountability forces rigorous testing and integration.

Earn the right to more autonomy

  • Explanation: Start with copilots and sync agents before attempting async long-running agents or coworkers.
  • Evidence: Engineering teams are good at parallelizing coding tasks in sandboxes, but knowledge work requires gradual trust building.
  • Practical implication: Gradually scale agent autonomy based on proven reliability in the field.

Real-world data is out-of-distribution for labs

  • Explanation: The most valuable tasks rely on messy, proprietary enterprise data and workflows that are not on the public internet.
  • Evidence: Examples like closing books with missing receipts or scoping construction projects.
  • Practical implication: Companies must build internal feedback loops and evals based on their own operational traces.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

The Autonomy Spectrum (Copilot to Coworker)

  • How it works: Graduating AI systems from reactive assistant tools to autonomous async agents and collaborative partners.
  • Components:
    • Copilot (AI assistance in the moment)
    • Sync agent (Real-time, two-way interaction)
    • Async agent (Trigger and respond when ready)
    • Long-running agent (Works independently over minutes, hours, or days)
    • Coworker (Proactive partner that gets work done with you)
  • When to use: Mapping enterprise AI product strategy from initial adoption to full autonomy.

The Enterprise AI Flywheel

  • How it works: A continuous learning loop combining real work execution with trace collection, evaluation, and model improvement.
  • Components:
      1. Real Work (People and agents get work done)
      1. Rich Traces (Every interaction and hiccup is observed)
      1. Real-World Evals (Outcomes evaluated against ground truth)
      1. Better Agents (Models and agents improve)
      1. More Impact (Better results drive trust and adoption)
  • When to use: Building internal continual learning systems for enterprise AI workflows.

Core insights

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Agent autonomy should be treated as a graduated ladder — copilot (in-the-moment assistance) → synchronous agent (real-time two-way) → asynchronous agent (works independently for minutes/hours/days) → proactive coworker — where each rung is only unlocked after field-proven reliability, not by model capability alone.

Why it matters

It reframes autonomy as a trust-gated deployment parameter rather than a model property, implying the harness needs per-rung capability boundaries, escalation paths, and a promotion criterion between rungs.

Generalization

Any long-running agent product should ship with an explicit autonomy level as a configurable, auditable dimension of the runtime, with a defined evidence bar (traces, success rate, human override rate) required to raise it.

Deep dives

Evidence-gated autonomy promotion (copilot → sync agent → async agent → coworker)

Research question

What metric set — held-out task success on real traces, human override rate, escalation precision, cost per completed task — reliably predicts when an agent can be promoted one rung up the autonomy ladder without producing field failures the business must absorb?

Why

Autonomy is currently shipped as a product decision; if it becomes a runtime dimension with a quantified promotion gate, teams get a testable criterion instead of a trust narrative, and failures get attributed to the wrong rung rather than to 'the model'.

Source video ↗

Outcome-anchored evals when no single labeled answer exists

Research question

Can downstream business outcomes (books closed, roof fixed, project scoped, deal closed) serve as the ground-truth label for agent evaluation, with rubric-scored intermediate artifacts supplying the process signal?

Why

If outcome linkage is missing, trace collection is just logging; and if only synthetic benchmarks are used, the eval never sees the incomplete-data and proprietary-scoping conditions that define the actual task.

Capturing work traces from people's heads and legacy systems

Research question

How much trace fidelity — session capture, screen/action logging, human-in-the-loop annotation — is required before continual learning on enterprise work produces measurable outcome gains, and at what point does instrumentation disrupt the 20-year-old software it sits on?

Why

The improvement flywheel is bottlenecked at data acquisition, not at model training; without a shadow-instrumentation answer, no continual-learning loop can start inside a traditional enterprise.

Article ideas

4

Earned Autonomy: Why Coworkers Are a Deployment Gate, Not a Feature Flag

Agent autonomy should be a runtime parameter with a quantified promotion criterion built from real field traces; shipping async or proactive modes because the model can now do them is the primary cause of enterprise agent failures, since trust — not capability — is the scarce resource.

Angle

Reframe the copilot-to-coworker progression as a reliability-proving ladder and specify the evidence bar (success on held-out real traces, override rate, escalation precision) needed at each rung.

Source video ↗

Your Enterprise Data Is Out-of-Distribution, and That Is the Product

Because business-critical knowledge lives in people's heads and twenty-year-old systems rather than on the internet, defensible value sits in the integration, scoping, and incomplete-data handling layer — not in the base model, which has near-zero prior on the task.

Angle

Argue that vertical agent budgets should be allocated proportional to task criticality for proprietary data acquisition and instrumentation, and derive the architectural consequence: keep the model behind workflow-specific tooling, never exposed directly to enterprise records.

Source video ↗

One Loop, Not Two: Why Continual Learning and User Enablement Are the Same Pipeline

Splitting model improvement (research) from adoption (customer success) guarantees neither happens; usage is simultaneously the training signal and the adoption signal, so the same instrumentation must serve both — with an explicit guard against entrenching incumbent bad practice.

Angle

Show the org and system boundary change, then confront the feedback-loop bias: traces encode existing workflows, so measure divergence from observed practice over time.

Source video ↗

Accounting for Outcomes Is an Engineering Practice

Owning the downstream business outcome, rather than selling software, is what converts silent agent failures into internal incidents and thereby raises eval rigor; a vendor structurally cannot reach the same reliability because no one absorbs the cost of the miss.

Angle

Use outcome ownership as the forcing function argument, then generalize: align the agent team's success metric with cycle time or error cost instead of deployment and usage counts.

Project ideas

4

Rung-Gate

gatehouse

Promoting an agent one autonomy rung only after it clears a pre-registered threshold on held-out real traces (success rate, human override rate, escalation precision) reduces field failures per completed task relative to promoting on model-version or user-request basis.

Proof of concept

Runtime that exposes autonomy level as a configurable dimension (copilot, sync agent, async agent, coworker) with per-mode permission boundaries, interruption and handoff surfaces, long-duration execution support, and automatic per-mode tracking of success and override events; a promotion gate service that evaluates the tracked evidence before allowing a config change.

Measurement

Field failure rate and cost per completed task per rung, override frequency trend after promotion, and rate of post-promotion rollbacks versus a control group promoted on schedule or by request.

Source video ↗

Outcome-Linked Trace Evals

beyond-evals

Eval suites scored against downstream business outcomes (did the books close, did the roof get fixed, did the project scope hold) predict production agent failures better than synthetic task benchmarks, and adding process-rubric scoring on intermediate artifacts recovers most of the remaining signal.

Proof of concept

Trace capture that records intermediate artifacts and workflow actions (not chat messages), joined to downstream outcome records; an offline replay harness that re-runs scored traces against ground truth and produces a per-workflow eval suite plus a model-selection leaderboard that is continuously re-run rather than decided once.

Measurement

Correlation between offline eval score and observed field failure rate on the same workflows; rank correlation between eval ordering and outcome ordering across candidate models; fraction of production incidents predicted in advance.

Source video ↗

Shadow Instrumentation for Legacy Work

movement-lab

Shadow instrumentation (session capture, screen and action logging, lightweight human annotation) can extract usable work traces from knowledge held in people's heads and twenty-year-old software without disrupting daily operations, and a modest fidelity threshold is sufficient to produce measurable continual-learning gains.

Proof of concept

Non-invasive capture layer over existing tools plus an annotation path; a fidelity dial that progressively degrades trace richness, with an agent fine-tuned or prompted on each fidelity tier and evaluated on the same outcome-anchored tasks.

Measurement

Knee in the trace-fidelity vs. outcome-improvement curve; operator time cost and disruption per captured trace; measurable improvement in outcome success at the minimum viable fidelity tier.

Source video ↗

Adoption-as-Signal

new

Within an enterprise deployment, adoption intensity (breadth of users, frequency of use, override rate) predicts later agent quality gains after controlling for task difficulty, and treating usage traces as the primary training dataset accelerates improvement versus training on expert-redesigned workflows alone.

Proof of concept

Single pipeline that emits both training traces and adoption telemetry with consent and lineage; two arms — agents improved from observed usage traces versus agents improved from expert-redesigned workflows — compared on outcome quality and divergence from observed practice over successive iterations.

Measurement

Lag-correlation between adoption intensity and subsequent quality gain; outcome quality delta between arms at fixed compute; divergence-from-practice index over time as a check on entrenchment of legacy inefficiency.
