Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Dmitry Buykin
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Building and scaling AI agent systems in production for global shipping and logistics at Maersk.
  • Purpose: To share practical engineering lessons and architectural patterns for moving AI agents from prototypes to robust production systems in highly regulated, complex global supply chain environments. Dmitry Buykin presents a practitioner report on deploying AI agents into production for global shipping at Maersk. He highlights the distinction between simple AI prototyping and production reality, introducing the concept of 'tribal dungeons'—hidden knowledge across legacy systems, spreadsheets, and people. He advocates for 'SOPs-as-code' (Standard Operating Procedures) over prompt engineering, emphasizing an architecture built around executable SOP corpora, agent runtime, and SME evaluation benches to ensure safe, scalable, and auditable enterprise AI operations.

Topic Map

The Reality of Global Logistics and State Machines

  • Explanation: Global logistics appears as a single linear workflow on paper, but in reality involves an orchestration of multiple parallel state machines (booking, vessel, equipment, documentation, customs, trucks, terminal, billing). When one state machine drifts, exceptions occur.
  • Key claims:
    • Global logistics is a coordination problem across multiple parallel state machines.
    • The clean 80% of logistics operations is system-driven; the hard 20% (long tail) is semi-automated using spreadsheets, emails, portals, and specialist judgment.
    • Keeping logistics state machines coherent when reality changes is the hardest engineering challenge.
  • Examples:
    • A mobile harbor crane or terminal documentation exceptions requiring manual intervention.
  • Terminology:
    • State machines
    • Happy path
    • Long tail tax
    • Exception workflows
  • Why it matters: Understanding that enterprise operations are collections of misaligned state machines explains why standard LLM chatbot wrappers fail in production.

Tribal Dungeons and Legacy SOPs

  • Explanation: Tribal dungeons refer to processes that exist as tribal knowledge among people, documents, and legacy systems rather than executable knowledge. Legacy SOPs are collections of screenshots and comments rather than actionable code.
  • Key claims:
    • An agent cannot safely execute a process the organization cannot represent.
    • Legacy SOPs explain what a person sees and clicks, but screenshots are not a process.
    • Agent SOPs require preconditions, decision trees, verified identifiers, distilled backend calls, validation, recovery paths, and evidence trails.
  • Examples:
    • Screenshots telling an operator to open a screen, click a tab, copy a value, ask a colleague, and send a reply.
  • Terminology:
    • Tribal dungeons
    • Legacy SOP
    • Agent SOP
    • Evidence trail
  • Why it matters: Bridging the gap between human-readable informal SOPs and machine-executable agent SOPs is essential for reliable automation.

Three-Component Production Architecture

  • Explanation: A robust architecture for enterprise AI consists of three parts: SOP Corpus (executable process memory), Agent Runtime (classifier, execution loop, traces, dynamic tools), and SME Bench (clustering traces, evaluation, correction, and replay).
  • Key claims:
    • The agent loop is not the system; the refinement loop around the agent is the system.
    • SME owns the what (business truth), agent owns the how (executable contract).
    • SOPs-as-code trump prompt engineering by providing versioned, country-scoped, linted, reviewed, and replayed procedures.
  • Examples:
    • A 20:1 ratio of SOP corpus to runtime code demonstrating that business logic memory outweighs runtime code volume.
  • Terminology:
    • SOP Corpus
    • Agent Runtime
    • SME Bench
    • Refinement loop
  • Why it matters: Shifting focus from prompt engineering to code-based SOP governance enables massive scale across 110 countries and thousands of process intents.

Evaluation UX and Making Corrections Actionable

  • Explanation: Handling errors at scale requires turning evaluation noise into actionable patterns. Failed traces are clustered and sent back to SMEs not just for review, but to generate executable corrections.
  • Key claims:
    • Evaluation UX must move from noise to patterns by grouping similar failures and making corrections actionable.
    • A correction only counts when it becomes an executable change—the line between an opinion and a production fix.
    • Quality comes from replaying real examples before every change, not from vibed or uncalibrated model scores.
  • Examples:
    • Clustering documentation, release, and invoice failures across regions A, B, and C to identify missing gates or bad templates.
  • Terminology:
    • Evaluation UX
    • Trace
    • Replay
    • Cognitive effort ratio
  • Why it matters: Enables systematic quality improvement across hundreds of thousands of production iterations.

Building a Cage for Production Agents

  • Explanation: Discovery needs freedom, but production needs a cage. Harnesses do not give agents more freedom; they make dumb mistakes structurally impossible through structural preventions like verify identifiers, classifier eval, tool allowlists, write gates, loop breakers, replay before deploy, and SME review.
  • Key claims:
    • A preventive measure eliminates the unsafe path entirely.
    • Critical paths must stay supervised with safe handoffs and trustable evidence trails.
    • The real outcome is a methodology and architecture, not a standalone chatbot.
  • Examples:
    • Mapping wrong shipments to verify identifiers, wrong workflows to classifier eval, and wrong writes to write gates.
  • Terminology:
    • Structural prevention
    • Write gates
    • Loop breakers
    • Evidence trail
  • Why it matters: Guarantees safety in high-stakes enterprise systems by structurally blocking invalid or dangerous agent actions.

Key Points

SOPs-as-code over prompt engineering

  • Explanation: Writing single big instructions (prompt engineering) does not scale globally. Versioned, country-aware, linted, and reviewed SOPs-as-code provide robust process memory.
  • Evidence: 20:1 ratio of SOP corpus to runtime code across 110 countries and over 10,000 process intents.
  • Practical implication: Enterprises should treat process SOPs as executable software artifacts rather than static documentation.

The refinement loop is the system

  • Explanation: While the agent executes individual tasks, the true power of the production architecture lies in the refinement loop that clusters failures, gathers SME feedback, and updates executable SOPs.
  • Evidence: Over 100,000 incremental corrections applied over 9 months yielding high production accuracy.
  • Practical implication: Invest engineering effort into evaluation benchmarks, trace review UX, and replay infrastructure rather than tweaking model prompts.

Structural prevention through cages

  • Explanation: Do not rely on agent intelligence alone for safety. Use architectural cages—such as write gates, tool allowlists, and strict preconditions—to make dumb mistakes structurally impossible.
  • Evidence: Mapping specific failure modes (wrong workflow, wrong write, wrong SOP edit) to dedicated structural preventions (classifier eval, write gates, replay before deploy).
  • Practical implication: Design agent systems where unsafe paths cannot be traversed by the underlying execution engine.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Three-Component Enterprise AI Architecture

  • How it works: Separates process memory from execution and evaluation, creating a closed-loop system where agent runs generate traces that SMEs evaluate and feed back into executable SOPs.
  • Components:
    • SOP Corpus (executable process memory)
    • Agent Runtime (classifier, execution loop, traces, dynamic tools)
    • SME Bench (clustering traces, evaluation, correction, and replay)
  • When to use: When deploying AI agents into complex enterprise production environments with multi-system workflows and strict regulatory requirements.

The Five-Step AI-Native Operations Blueprint

  • How it works: A methodical lifecycle for building and refining AI-driven enterprise processes from raw tribal knowledge to reliable production execution.
  • Components:
    • Make work representable (collect data and tribal knowledge into executable SOPs)
    • Make execution bounded (tools, scopes, gates, approvals)
    • Make behavior observable (every run produces a trace)
    • Make correction cheap (SME feedback, SOP diff, replay)
    • Make improvement compound (daily refinements, reusable workflows as tools)
  • When to use: When transforming legacy operational workflows into AI-native processes across global organizations.

Examples & Case Studies

Scaling container shipping operations across 110 countries with varying local regulations and legacy backend systems.

  • Illustrates: How the same business logic manifests differently across countries, requiring country-aware SOPs-as-code.
  • Lesson: Prompt engineering fails at multi-country scale due to hidden local variations; structured SOP corpora solve this.

Deploying 200+ container instances with traffic spikes and latencies ranging from a few minutes to 10 minutes due to legacy backend dependencies.

  • Illustrates: Production scaling challenges where agent execution speed is bounded by slow legacy enterprise systems.
  • Lesson: Agent runtime architecture must account for legacy backend latency and scale container instances dynamically.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:

    • Ensure every agent run generates a comprehensive trace for auditing and evaluation.
    • Implement write gates and tool allowlists to structurally prevent agent errors.
  • Strategic:
    • Treat the refinement loop around the agent as the core system, not the agent execution loop itself.
    • Build SME evaluation benches that cluster failures and turn human feedback into executable code changes.
    • Recognize that AI models provide rented intelligence, while adaptive architecture is the permanent enterprise asset.

[A]Architecturehigh noveltystrong evidence

The durable engineering artifact is not the agent execution loop but the refinement loop wrapped around it: trace capture, failure clustering, SME adjudication, executable SOP edits, and replay before redeploy. The agent is a thin interpreter; the system is the feedback machinery that keeps a versioned process corpus converging on business truth.

Why it matters

It relocates where engineering effort and headcount should go. Teams that spend their budget on loop logic, prompt structure, and model choice are optimizing the smallest, most commoditized part of the stack while leaving the actual quality-producing mechanism unbuilt.

Generalization

Any agent deployed against a large, drifting, human-owned process domain needs a first-class corrective loop with the same rigor as the runtime: clustered traces, executable patch proposals, and pre-deploy replay. The runtime is replaceable; the loop is the moat.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltystrong evidence

Process knowledge should be compiled into an executable, versioned SOP corpus rather than expressed as prompts. The corpus carries preconditions, decision trees, verified identifiers, distilled backend calls, validation, recovery paths, and evidence trails, and is country-scoped, linted, reviewed, and replay-tested — a software artifact with a lifecycle, not a document.

Why it matters

It gives process logic the properties prompts lack: diffability, per-region scoping, static checking, review gates, and regression replay. This is what makes 110-country, 10,000-intent coverage tractable instead of a combinatorial prompt explosion.

Generalization

Wherever business logic varies along dimensions (jurisdiction, product, customer tier), express it as scoped configuration-as-code with lint and replay, not as conditional prose inside a single instruction blob.

[P]Practicehigh noveltymoderate evidence

Safety is achieved structurally, not behaviorally. The harness is a cage whose purpose is to make classes of errors impossible rather than discouraged, and each failure class maps to a specific structural control: wrong entity to identifier verification, wrong workflow selection to classifier evaluation, wrong side effect to write gates, non-termination to loop breakers.

Why it matters

It converts safety from a probabilistic property of model behavior into a deterministic property of the architecture. That is the difference between a system that can be audited and supervised and one that can only be hoped about.

Generalization

Enumerate the finite set of catastrophic error classes per deployment and assign each a structural prevention at the boundary (schema check, permission gate, human approval, budget/step cap) instead of writing instructions asking the model not to do it.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

A representation gap gates automation: an agent cannot safely execute a process the organization cannot formally represent. Legacy SOPs encode what a human sees and clicks (screenshots, comments, 'ask a colleague'), which is a UI narrative, not a process specification — so the prerequisite work is process archaeology and formalization, not agent tuning.

Why it matters

It predicts that automation projects fail upstream of the model, in the formalization step, and that the honest unit of work is re-deriving the process from tribal knowledge across people, spreadsheets, and legacy systems. It also implies a cheap feasibility test: can the org write the process down executably?

Generalization

Agent readiness of a domain is bounded by its representational maturity. Audit whether the process exists as executable specification before committing to agentic automation; if not, budget for formalization.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltystrong evidence

Evaluation must be an authoring surface, not a dashboard. Raw failed traces are noise; clustering them into recurring patterns lets SMEs respond with an executable correction rather than an opinion, and a correction is only counted once it lands as a change in the SOP corpus. Replay of real examples gates every change.

Why it matters

It sets a concrete bar for evaluation tooling: an eval system that produces scores but no diffable, deployable patch is incomplete. It also reframes SME time as the scarce resource and clustering as the compression that makes that time affordable.

Generalization

Design evaluation UX around failure clustering plus one-click conversion of a cluster into a reviewable change, with a replay suite as the merge gate. Measure whether feedback terminates in a code/config change, not whether it was recorded.

[E]Empirical Resultmedium noveltymoderate evidence

The addressable surface is the long tail, not the happy path. Roughly 80% of logistics operations are already system-driven; the remaining 20% is semi-automated through spreadsheets, email, portals, and specialist judgment, and it is exactly there that an agent has leverage. Failures arise because operations are many parallel state machines (booking, vessel, equipment, documentation, customs, trucks, terminal, billing) that drift out of alignment.

Why it matters

It explains why generic chatbot wrappers fail: they target the deterministic portion that software already handles and lack the exception-handling and cross-state-machine coordination that the tail requires. It also gives a sizing heuristic for where to deploy agents.

Generalization

Prioritize agent investment in the semi-structured exception tail where human judgment plus heterogeneous tool access dominates, and treat coherence across parallel state machines as the core coordination problem rather than single-task accuracy.

[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Responsibility for 'what' versus 'how' is a hard architectural boundary: the SME owns business truth (which outcome is correct), the agent system owns the executable contract (how it is achieved and evidenced). The corpus-to-runtime code ratio of roughly 20:1 is a signal that the system behaves more like a data/configuration product than an application.

Why it matters

It dictates org design and repo structure: the domain expert edits the corpus through a review pipeline while engineers build generic runtime, tooling, and replay infrastructure. It also warns that headcount and tooling investment should skew toward corpus authoring and evaluation, not runtime features.

Generalization

For agent products in regulated domains, split the system into a generic interpreter plus a large, expert-owned, versioned knowledge artifact, and judge engineering maturity by how much of the behavior lives in the artifact.

Architectural implications

4

Process logic moves out of prompts and into a versioned, linted, country-scoped executable corpus.

Before

Behavior encoded as one large instruction blob per agent, edited ad hoc, with variation handled by prose conditionals.

After

SOPs-as-code with preconditions, decision trees, verified identifiers, backend call distillation, validation, recovery paths, and evidence trails, each versioned and regionally scoped.

Consequence

Behavior becomes diffable, reviewable, statically checkable, and replay-testable; scaling to many jurisdictions becomes configuration growth rather than prompt sprawl, but requires investing in linting, schema, and a corpus toolchain.

Each observed failure class is bound to a structural control at a boundary rather than to an instruction.

Before

Safety pursued by prompting caution and reviewing outputs after the fact.

After

Verify-identifier checks, classifier evaluation, tool allowlists, write gates, loop breakers, and human handoffs on critical paths.

Consequence

Certain error classes become impossible rather than unlikely, but every gate is a hardcoded assumption that must be maintained as process and systems change.

Before

Eval dashboards producing model scores that inform prompt or model swaps.

After

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

The cage vs. the long tail

Benefit

Structural preventions make known catastrophic error classes impossible and keep critical paths supervised.

Cost or risk

A cage defined by enumerated error classes cannot cover novel exceptions, which is precisely the semi-automated 20% the agent was deployed to handle; over-tightening gates can block legitimate corrective action.

Source video ↗

Corpus scale vs. corpus maintenance

Benefit

Versioned, country-scoped SOPs-as-code enable coverage across many jurisdictions and thousands of process intents.

Cost or risk

A corpus twenty times the size of the runtime is a large surface that drifts with upstream systems, making linting, review, and replay permanently load-bearing rather than one-time setup.

Source video ↗

SME adjudication throughput

Benefit

SMEs supply business truth and convert opinion into executable corrections, grounding the system in real process ownership.

Cost or risk

Over 100,000 corrections were needed in nine months, so expert review time becomes the binding constraint; clustering is required to keep that cost sublinear, and coarse clustering can hide rare but severe failures.

Source video ↗

Replay gating vs. deployment velocity

Benefit

Replaying real examples before every change replaces uncalibrated model scores with empirical regression evidence.

Cost or risk

Every corpus change pays a replay and review cost, so small urgent fixes move at suite speed, and behavior not represented in the replay set can regress undetected.
