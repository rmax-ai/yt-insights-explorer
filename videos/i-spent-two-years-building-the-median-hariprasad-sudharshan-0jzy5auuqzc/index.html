Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Hariprasad Sudharshan
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: The commoditization of AI software development and the difference between novelty and the median.
  • Purpose: To help product leaders and engineers understand how AI changes software development economics, why building everything from scratch is a trap, and where true differentiation lies. Hariprasad Sudharshan reflects on two years of building AI applications like RAG systems and token calculators, discovering that despite spending countless hours on custom architectures, prompt chains, and agent design, anyone else using modern AI coding agents arrives at essentially the same median solution. He argues that novelty is scarce and expensive, whereas the median AI software and capabilities are now free and commoditized. He offers practical frameworks for product teams to navigate the AI age, emphasizing that teams should be highly opinionated, leverage existing open-source and agent capabilities rather than reinventing deterministic infrastructure, and focus their scarce novelty budget on truly differentiated ideas.

Topic Map

The Median Software Phenomenon

  • Explanation: Explaining how AI coding agents and LLMs lead independent developers and teams to independently converge on identical solutions for common problems like RAG and token calculators.
  • Key claims:
    • Most AI-assisted software projects converge on the exact same median architecture.
    • The median software is free, commoditized, and built 230 times a minute by coding agents.
    • Team variance and unique judgement inputs are compressed by standardized LLMs.
  • Examples:
    • 244 GitHub repositories matching LLM cost calculators with identical shapes and outputs.
  • Terminology:
    • the median
    • AI slop
    • commoditized capabilities
  • Why it matters: Understanding that standard AI tooling gives everyone identical median output prevents organizations from mistaking commoditized AI wrappers for unique competitive moats.

Novelty vs. The Median in the Age of AI

  • Explanation: Contrasting the scarce element of true novelty with the ubiquitous and accessible median capabilities generated by AI models and harnesses.
  • Key claims:
    • Novelty is scarce and expensive; the median is free and abundant.
    • You get about one unit of true novelty per project.
    • Spending time optimizing prompt chains and agent architecture on common problems yields median results.
  • Examples:
    • Google Search in 1998 combining a basic crawler/index with PageRank novelty.
  • Terminology:
    • novelty budget
    • first principles
    • moat
  • Why it matters: Teams must consciously allocate their scarce novelty budget toward truly unique product features rather than reinventing solved problems.

The Build-First Reflex and Three Questions

  • Explanation: Examining the modern reflex to immediately prompt coding agents to build everything rather than searching for existing open-source solutions, and introducing a three-question playbook.
  • Key claims:
    • Building became cheaper than searching, leading to an explosion of redundant AI software.
    • Leaders must ask three questions before prompting a coding agent: Who has already done this? What do I have that they do not? Where will this live?
    • Demos are expensive ways to learn you have no distribution.
  • Examples:
    • Building a custom RAG system over PDFs when thousands of mature open-source alternatives exist.
  • Terminology:
    • build-first reflex
    • distribution moat
    • scaffolding
  • Why it matters: Saves engineering teams wasted effort building redundant software and forces focus on distribution and unique value.

Key Points

Novelty is scarce, the median is free

  • Explanation: AI models and coding agents have commoditized standard software development, making the median output universally accessible and free.
  • Evidence: Over 1.1 million public repos importing LLM SDKs and 4.3 million AI-related repositories showing convergent solutions.
  • Practical implication: Do not waste limited engineering bandwidth reinventing standard tools; focus entirely on what makes your product uniquely novel.

The danger of the build-first reflex

  • Explanation: Because coding agents make building feel effortless, developers immediately start coding rather than evaluating existing open-source ecosystems.
  • Evidence: Building a custom RAG or calculator in two hours only to find hundreds of identical implementations.
  • Practical implication: Adopt rigorous search and evaluation habits before letting AI agents generate redundant codebases.

Distribution is the moat, not the AI wrapper

  • Explanation: When everyone has access to the same AI models and agents, the software itself ceases to be a competitive advantage.
  • Evidence: GitHub stats showing hundreds of identical LLM cost calculators created by independent developers.
  • Practical implication: Focus on distribution, user acquisition moats, and unique human judgment rather than software implementation details.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

The Three Questions Before Prompting a Coding Agent

  • How it works: A strategic checklist for engineering leaders and product teams before delegating development to AI agents.
  • Components:
    • Who has already done this?
    • What do I have that they do not?
    • Where will this live?
  • When to use: Before starting any new AI-assisted software development project or feature build.

Four Ways Past the Median

  • How it works: Four guiding principles to achieve true product differentiation in an era of commoditized AI software.
  • Components:
    • Be highly opinionated (the median has no opinion).
    • Prompt to production is the median path; decide what's wrong with it and build past it.
    • When AI says done, the last design is free; your job is the next one.
    • Leverage distribution and avoid building endless demos without distribution.
  • When to use: When planning product roadmaps and evaluating whether a feature is genuinely novel.

Examples & Case Studies

Spent two years building and refining a token calculator app across multiple AI platforms, sacrificing social time and sleep.

  • Illustrates: The build-first reflex and the pursuit of the median.
  • Lesson: Nobody copied anyone; independent developers converged on the exact same median calculator because the prompt/tooling stack dictated the outcome.

Asked an AI agent to build a RAG system over PDFs without checking open-source alternatives.

  • Illustrates: The ease of generating standard software via AI coding agents.
  • Lesson: The agent built a textbook median RAG system instantly, but completely ignored mature open-source options.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Ask the three strategic questions before instructing a coding agent to build.
    • Audit your product roadmap for true novelty versus commoditized features.
    • Stop treating AI-generated median software as a unique differentiator.
  • Strategic:
    • Allocate your novelty budget to architecture, unique domain insights, or distribution moats.
    • Accept that standard software development is commoditized and priced near zero.
    • Focus on distribution and human judgment as primary competitive advantages.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • What is the single unit of true novelty in our current product?
    • Are we building a commodity wrapper that any competitor can generate with a prompt?
    • Do we have a distribution moat or are we just building expensive demos?

[!]Failure Modemedium noveltymoderate evidence

The economics of building inverted: agentic code generation made building cheaper than searching, so the default developer behavior became prompt-to-build, producing a large volume of redundant software that ignores mature open-source alternatives.

Why it matters

The search/evaluation step didn't get cheaper at the same rate as generation, so it is now systematically skipped — meaning the cheapest action locally produces the most expensive outcome globally (maintained, undifferentiated code). Explicit gates are needed to reinsert search into pipelines.

Generalization

When generation cost drops faster than evaluation cost, systems over-generate and under-select; the bottleneck migrates to retrieval/evaluation and must be instrumented as a first-class stage.

Deep dives

4

Attributing output similarity to harness versus domain

Research question

How much of the structural similarity between independently built AI applications is attributable to the shared model/harness/context inputs versus the problem domain itself, and can measured divergence be induced by varying harness, context and constraints on an identical spec?

Why

If convergence is an input artifact rather than a domain property, then differentiation is an input-engineering problem with a controllable lever; if it is domain-bound, teams should not attempt to differentiate inside those domains at all. Either answer changes where design review and engineering attention go.

Source video ↗

Pre-build detection of median work

Research question

Can a proposed feature be scored for 'median-ness' before any code is written, by clustering existing mature implementations of the same spec, and does that score predict post-build redundancy?

Why

The three-question gate is qualitative and unenforced. A quantitative pre-build score turns an intention into a checkable input to the build decision, and would let teams fail fast on a spec that already has hundreds of equivalents.

Source video ↗

Making the reuse search execute rather than get skipped

Research question

What mechanism - blocking, gating, or cost-injection - actually causes an agent pipeline to run a reuse/evaluation search before generation, and how much redundant code does that suppress?

Why

The diagnosis is that generation got cheap while search did not, so the expensive stage is skipped. Without an enforcement mechanism, the failure mode recurs regardless of how well teams understand it; the interesting engineering question is what makes the skipped stage non-optional.

Is the novelty budget empirically one unit per project?

Research question

Do shipped products cluster around a single differentiating mechanism sitting on a commodity substrate, and do outcomes improve when engineering effort is concentrated on that mechanism rather than spread across the stack?

Why

The 'one unit of novelty' claim is an allocation rule with no supporting data. If it holds, it justifies deliberately accepting commodity implementations everywhere else and reframes engineering management as protection of a single scarce resource.

Article ideas

3

Generation got cheap, selection did not: why agentic pipelines over-produce and under-choose

The build-first reflex is not a discipline failure but a rational local response to an asymmetric cost change: generation collapsed in cost while evaluation and reuse discovery did not, so the pipeline's bottleneck silently migrated to the stage nobody instrumented. The fix is a first-class, enforced selection stage ahead of generation, not a reminder to search more.

Angle

Treat the reuse search as a pipeline stage with a cost model and a blocking gate, and show how the cheapest local action produces the most expensive global outcome.

Source video ↗

Your architecture is not the moat; the harness already knows it

When the same model and agent stack produces the same competent design across independent teams, implementation detail stops carrying information about team quality and stops functioning as advantage. Architectural differentiation inside commodity domains is a category error; the durable assets are the inputs the harness cannot reproduce - proprietary context, opinionated constraints, and distribution.

Angle

Use the convergence exhibit (identical implementations appearing independently) as the argument, then redirect design-review effort from authoring quality to domain classification and input design.

Source video ↗

One unit of novelty: budgeting the only scarce input left

If roughly one unit of true novelty exists per project, then evening out engineering effort across the stack is a misallocation, and the correct posture is a commodity substrate plus a deliberately protected novel core. Teams should be able to name their novelty unit and the constraints that make it non-reproducible before they write the first prompt.

Angle

Frame the novelty budget as an allocation rule with an operational test: name the novel mechanism, name what the default path gets wrong, and accept the median everywhere else on purpose.

Project ideas

3

Convergence Bench

new

For a fixed spec, a meaningful share of structural similarity across agent-built implementations is attributable to harness and context configuration rather than to the domain, and varying harness/context/constraints produces measurable divergence in file structure, dependency choices and API shape.

Proof of concept

Take 3-4 common specs (cost calculator, basic RAG, ingestion pipeline), run each through N harness/context configurations, and compute pairwise similarity over file tree, dependency set and public interface. Report a variance decomposition of similarity by configuration versus by spec.

Measurement

Pairwise output similarity matrix per spec; fraction of similarity explained by harness/context variation; divergence delta between default and constraint-injected runs.

Source video ↗

Pre-Build Reuse Gate

gatehouse

Blocking code generation until a reuse search and differential-advantage statement is completed reduces redundant implementation volume without reducing delivery speed on genuinely novel work.

Proof of concept

Hook a pre-generation stage into an agent workflow that clusters existing repos and packages for the spec, scores equivalent maturity, and refuses to proceed to generation until the three questions are answered. Compare runs with and without the gate on the same task set.

Measurement

Duplicate implementation rate (new code re-implementing an available mature package), lines of maintained undifferentiated code, cycle time on gated vs ungated tasks, and abandonment rate of tasks that turn out to be fully commoditized.

Source video ↗

Deviation Meter

beyond-evals

The default prompt-to-production output is tightly clustered across teams, and a critique-revise loop against that default measurably increases divergence from the cluster centroid in ways correlated with retained human judgement rather than noise.

Proof of concept

Generate baseline outputs for a task set, capture post-critique revisions, and score each revision by distance from the baseline centroid plus a rubric of explicit opinion/constraint injection. Track whether revisions persist through review or get reverted toward the median.

Measurement

Distance from baseline centroid per revision; count and survival rate of injected constraints; correlation between divergence and downstream retention of the change.

Architectural implications

3

Agent harnesses standardize how a problem is decomposed and implemented, compressing inter-team variance in the resulting architecture.

Before

Architecture and implementation detail are treated as evidence of team capability and as a source of advantage.

After

Architecture inside commodity domains is a baseline to be adopted, not designed; differentiation budget is redirected to novel mechanisms, data, and delivery.

Consequence

Design reviews should ask 'is this domain commodity, and if so why are we authoring it?' rather than evaluating the quality of an authoring effort that any agent reproduces.

Source video ↗

Generation is cheap and evaluation/search is comparatively expensive, inverting the historical build-vs-find order.

Before

Search/selection was relatively costly, so building a tailored implementation was often the rational local default.

After

The pipeline needs an explicit reuse-discovery and differential-advantage gate ahead of the generation step.

Consequence

Engineering process gains a mandatory pre-generation stage (search existing implementations, state what you uniquely have, name the deployment surface), enforced by tooling rather than good intentions.

Source video ↗

Value shifts from producing the first working design to critiquing and iterating past the generic one.

Before

Effort spent reaching a working implementation; the first design was expensive and therefore treated as an endpoint.

After

The first design is effectively free, so review/critique/redesign of agent output becomes the load-bearing human stage.

Consequence

Harnesses should optimize for fast critique-revise loops over agent output (diff review, opinion injection, constraint checking) rather than for one-shot generation quality.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Agent-speed building vs. reuse discovery

Benefit

Coding agents let a team produce a working RAG or calculator in a couple of hours.

Cost or risk

The same output exists in hundreds of other repos, so the time yields maintained undifferentiated code and hides the more important gap (no distribution).

Source video ↗

Standardized harness efficiency vs. loss of team judgement

Benefit

Common LLM/agent scaffolding gives reliable, fast convergence on a competent baseline.

Cost or risk

Team variance and distinctive judgement inputs are compressed, so outputs that should differ end up the same.

Source video ↗

Prompt-to-production as a default path

Benefit

A repeatable path from prompt to deployable software is efficient and low-friction.

Cost or risk

The path itself is the median; following it yields median output, so a team must consciously spend effort identifying and correcting its default choices.

Source video ↗

Cheap demos vs. distribution learning

Benefit

Demos are now cheap to generate and useful for exploring product shape.

Cost or risk

An endless demo stream without distribution is an expensive way to discover that nobody adopts the product.
