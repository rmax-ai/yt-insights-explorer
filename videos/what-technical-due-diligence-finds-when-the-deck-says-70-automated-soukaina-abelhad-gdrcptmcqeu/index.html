Overview

  • Speaker: Soukaïna Abelhad
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Evaluating AI automation claims and productivity metrics during technical due diligence
  • Purpose: Provide investors and technical leaders with a robust framework (the diligence card) to critically evaluate AI startup productivity claims, automation rates, and P&L impact. Soukaïna Abelhad shares insights from conducting technical due diligence on AI startups, explaining how to evaluate metrics like '55.8% faster' and '70% automated' through three critical checks: arithmetic, definition, and the tell. She explains how high AI adoption often increases human review workload and why financial translation of metrics is essential for investors and operators.

Topic Map

Introduction to AI Technical Due Diligence

  • Explanation: Introduction of the speaker and the premise of technical due diligence when encountering inflated AI automation metrics.
  • Key claims:
    • AI decks frequently cite impressive productivity and automation numbers.
    • Due diligence requires verifying whether the numbers support the conclusions drawn.
  • Examples:
    • A controlled experiment showing 55.8% faster development on a single JavaScript HTTP server.
  • Terminology:
    • technical due diligence
    • controlled experiment
    • AI adoption
  • Why it matters: Helps distinguish between genuine productivity gains and marketing hype.

Check 1: The Arithmetic

  • Explanation: Examining whether increased AI output translates to reduced head count or simply increased downstream review workload.
  • Key claims:
    • More work entering the system often increases the downstream review queue burden.
    • Observational telemetry of 10,000+ developers showed 98% more merged PRs but 91% longer PR review time and 154% larger PR size.
  • Examples:
    • Faros AI study showing increased PR volume leading to a bottleneck in human review labor.
  • Terminology:
    • observational telemetry
    • PR review time
    • human labor tracking
  • Why it matters: Proves that AI acceleration often shifts work rather than eliminating it.

Check 2: The Definition

  • Explanation: Dissecting what 'automation' actually means by examining the spectrum from AI drafted to zero human intervention.
  • Key claims:
    • Automation claims sit on a spectrum from AI drafted with human in the loop to straight-through processing inside a named boundary.
    • AI-attributed and autonomous are distinct claims.
  • Examples:
    • Uber's claim that >70% of pull requests are AI-attributed.
  • Terminology:
    • straight-through processing
    • AI-attributed
    • human-in-the-loop
  • Why it matters: Prevents confusing assisted work with autonomous execution.

Check 3: The Tell

  • Explanation: Comparing automated evaluation results against human expert merge decisions to check evaluation maturity.
  • Key claims:
    • Automated graders are often substantially more optimistic than human maintainers.
    • Evaluation maturity is the first credibility signal to look for.
  • Examples:
    • METR study showing a 24.2 percentage-point gap between automated grader scores and maintainer merge decisions.
  • Terminology:
    • automated grader
    • maintainer merge decision
    • ground truth
  • Why it matters: Uncovers discrepancies between model benchmarks and expert judgment.

The P&L Bridge and Financial Translation

  • Explanation: Translating technical metrics into unit economics by calculating the cost per accepted autonomous task.
  • Key claims:
    • A warning or due diligence flag is not enough unless it translates into financial unit economics.
    • Failed, escalated, or reworked tasks belong in the cost bucket.
  • Examples:
    • Engineer.ai (Builder.ai) public warning by WSJ in 2019, revenue estimate cuts in 2025, and Chapter 7 filing.
  • Terminology:
    • P&L bridge
    • cost per accepted autonomous task
    • straight-through success
  • Why it matters: Ensures AI investments survive both technical validation and financial scrutiny.

Key Points

Volume vs. Labor Arithmetic

  • Explanation: High AI adoption increases PR volume, review time, and PR size, which expands review labor requirements rather than reducing headcount.
  • Evidence: Telemetry from 10,000+ developers showing +98% merged PRs, +91% review time, and +154% PR size.
  • Practical implication: Ask for 18 months of workload volume compared against fully loaded human labor.

Defining Automation Boundaries

  • Explanation: Companies conflate AI assistance with autonomous execution. True straight-through processing requires a defined boundary with zero human intervention.
  • Evidence: Uber's >70% AI-attributed PR metric vs. distinct workflow stages (drafted, edited, approved, no human intervention).
  • Practical implication: Clarify where on the spectrum the automation claim sits.

Evaluation Maturity Gap

  • Explanation: Automated evaluation benchmarks often vastly overstate success compared to human domain experts.
  • Evidence: METR findings showing a 24.2 percentage-point gap between automated graders and human maintainers.
  • Practical implication: Always verify who labeled ground truth, whether experts agree, and what bad output escaped.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

The Dilgence Card (Three Checks)

  • How it works: Three rigorous checks applied to AI productivity and automation claims before accepting them in financial or technical evaluations.
  • Components:
    • Arithmetic: Volume vs. labor (18 months, count every human touch)
    • Definition: Which rung? (Assisted, approved, or straight-through)
    • The Tell: Who defines quality? (Ground truth, agreement, judge, escapes)
  • When to use: During technical due diligence, vendor evaluations, or investment reviews of AI companies.

The P&L Bridge

  • How it works: Formula to translate workflow value into captured economic value by factoring in adoption, success, quality, and rework costs.
  • Components:
    • Eligible workflow value
    • Adoption rate
    • Straight-through success
    • Accepted quality
    • Minus AI + review + rework cost
  • When to use: When projecting financial returns or unit economics on AI-driven operational efficiency.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Count every human touch in the workflow rather than just looking at raw code generation volume.
    • Determine whether automation claims refer to AI assistance or zero-intervention straight-through processing.
  • Strategic:
    • Use the P&L bridge framework to calculate cost per accepted autonomous task.
    • Evaluate AI maturity by checking automated test results against expert human review data.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • Who labeled the ground truth for evaluation datasets?
    • What percentage of AI-generated output escapes into production without human review?
    • How do downstream review bottlenecks scale with increased AI-generated workload?

Core insights

6
[E]Empirical Resultmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Cheap AI generation shifts the bottleneck downstream: making code production cheaper increases the volume and size of work entering human review, so the constraint moves to reviewer labor rather than disappearing. The telemetry cited shows output volume rising far faster than review capacity, meaning net labor savings must be measured at the review stage, not the generation stage.

Why it matters

Any system that generates artifacts faster than humans can verify them will saturate on verification. Architecture and capacity planning should size the review/verification stage first and treat generation throughput as an input to that queue, not the headline metric.

Generalization

In any generate-then-verify pipeline (code, documents, tickets, data fixes), optimizing the generator relocates the bottleneck to the verifier; system throughput is governed by verification capacity and artifact size.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

'Automation' is not binary but a ladder: AI-drafted-with-human-in-the-loop at one end and straight-through processing inside a named boundary at the other. 'AI-attributed' output and 'autonomous' execution are categorically different claims, so a single percentage conflates rungs of the ladder.

Why it matters

Agentic system design needs an explicit, named boundary that defines where human intervention is required and where execution is truly straight-through. Without that boundary, autonomy claims are unfalsifiable and the system's operating envelope is undefined.

Generalization

Autonomy should be expressed as a success rate inside a declared operating boundary (inputs, tools, failure handling), not as a percentage of work touched by AI.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

The correct denominator for AI unit economics is the accepted autonomous task, not the attempted or generated one. The P&L bridge multiplies eligible workflow value by adoption rate, straight-through success, and accepted quality, then subtracts AI cost plus review cost plus rework cost — placing failures, escalations, and rework explicitly in the cost bucket.

Why it matters

It converts a technical metric into a per-unit cost figure that can be compared against fully loaded human labor, and it prevents the common error of amortizing AI inference cost across generated tasks while ignoring the human review and rework that generation induces.

Generalization

Any automation ROI model should use cost-per-accepted-output as the unit, with rework and escalation as first-class cost terms rather than excluded externalities.

Deep dives

4

The review-capacity crossover point for AI-generated throughput

Research question

At what ratio of generated-artifact volume to reviewer capacity does marginal AI generation become net-negative, and what artifact granularity cap (PR size, task scope) restores positive marginal value per accepted output?

Why

Aggregate telemetry (+98% merged PRs, +91% review time, +154% PR size) tells you the bottleneck moved but not where the system saturates. Without a crossover curve, teams cannot set generation rate limits, PR size caps, or agent task granularity on any basis other than intuition, and schedulers will keep producing backlog.

Source video ↗

Grader optimism as a measurable function of judge/generator overlap

Research question

Does the automated-grader optimism bias (observed at 24.2 percentage points) scale with the degree of overlap between the judging model/rubric and the generating model, and does it hold across code, document, and data-fix domains?

Why

If optimism is structural rather than noise, every reliability number derived from an eval harness carries a bias term that must be reported and subtracted. Establishing whether the bias is a function of judge independence turns 'trust our eval score' into a calibratable offset, and tells you whether a cheap same-family judge is ever acceptable.

Source video ↗

Declaring the autonomy boundary as an architecture artifact

Research question

What is the minimum specification of a named operating boundary (allowed inputs, tools, failure paths, acceptance criteria) that makes a straight-through success rate falsifiable and comparable across two different agent systems?

Why

Without a declared boundary, 'percent automated' is unfalsifiable and blends assisted work with autonomous execution. A concrete boundary spec converts the claim into a per-boundary straight-through rate plus an explicit escalation path, which is also exactly what routing and escalation code needs to encode.

Source video ↗

Attributing review and rework cost per accepted autonomous task in a shared human queue

Research question

In a queue where reviewers handle both AI-generated and human-generated artifacts, what provenance instrumentation and attribution rule yields a defensible cost-per-accepted-autonomous-task figure?

Why

The P&L bridge names AI cost, review cost, and rework cost as separate terms, but in practice they are commingled in one human queue. Without an attribution method, the cost-per-accepted-output denominator silently absorbs or hides review and rework, and the comparison against fully loaded human labor is not honest.

Project ideas

3

Verifier Saturation Harness

movement-lab

For a fixed reviewer pool, there exists an artifact-size cap below which marginal AI generation yields positive accepted-output-per-reviewer-hour, and above which it is net-negative; a moderate cap on artifact size raises accepted throughput even while total generation drops.

Proof of concept

A synthetic generate-then-verify pipeline where an LLM produces code/doc artifacts at configurable volume and size, a simulated reviewer with finite per-hour capacity and size-dependent review latency accepts or rejects them, and generation volume and max artifact size are swept across a grid.

Measurement

Accepted outputs per reviewer-hour and cost per accepted output as a function of (generation rate, max artifact size); locate the crossover point and the size cap that maximizes accepted throughput.

Source video ↗

Judge-Optimism Meter

beyond-evals

Automated grader optimism bias is monotonically increasing in judge/generator overlap and is stable enough across domains to be reported as a calibrated offset rather than treated as noise.

Proof of concept

Score a shared artifact set with three graders of decreasing independence (same model family, different family, human-anchored rubric) and compare each against a multi-expert acceptance label with measured inter-annotator agreement; track escaped defects the graders passed.

Measurement

Percentage-point gap between grader score and expert acceptance, per grader condition and per domain; correlation between gap size and judge/generator overlap; escaped-defect rate.

Source video ↗

Autonomy Boundary Ledger

gatehouse

Systems that declare an explicit operating boundary and report straight-through success rate inside it produce autonomy claims that are reproducible and comparable, while systems reporting a single blended automation percentage cannot be replicated by an independent evaluator.

Proof of concept

A spec-and-instrumentation harness: a declarative boundary file (allowed inputs, tools, failure paths, acceptance criteria) plus a runtime ledger that tags every task as straight-through, escalated, reworked, or rejected, emitting a per-boundary success rate alongside the legacy blended percentage.

Measurement

Inter-evaluator reproducibility of the autonomy number (do two independent evaluators get the same straight-through rate from the ledger?) versus variance of the blended percentage; share of tasks whose disposition is unclassifiable without a boundary.

Architectural implications

4

AI acceleration moves the constraint from generation to verification.

Before

Instrument and optimize model throughput: tokens, generations, PRs created, tasks attempted.

After

Instrument the review/verification stage as the primary capacity metric: review latency, artifact size, human touches per accepted output, queue depth.

Consequence

Schedulers, batching, and agent scope decisions should target reviewer throughput — e.g., capping PR size or task granularity — or the system will generate backlog instead of savings.

Autonomy claims need a declared operating boundary.

Before

Report a single blended 'percent automated' figure across all workflow stages.

After

Declare a named boundary (allowed inputs, tools, failure paths) and report straight-through success rate inside that boundary separately from assisted/AI-attributed rates.

Consequence

Routing and escalation logic becomes an explicit architectural artifact, and autonomy becomes measurable and comparable across systems.

Source video ↗

Automated graders are biased optimistic proxies for expert acceptance.

Before

Use an LLM-as-judge or rubric score as the reliability metric for the agent.

After

Maintain a calibration set of expert ground-truth decisions with inter-expert agreement, and track escaped defects alongside judge scores.

Consequence

Eval harness must include a human-anchored calibration layer and an escape-analysis loop; judge scores reported with a measured optimism offset.

Source video ↗

Rework and escalation are hidden costs of automation.

Before

Compute cost per generated task or per AI-touched task.

After

Compute cost per accepted autonomous task, loading AI inference + human review + rework + escalation into the numerator.

Consequence

Changes which workflows are worth automating: high straight-through-rate, low-rework tasks get prioritized; attractive-looking high-volume tasks with expensive review may be net negative.
