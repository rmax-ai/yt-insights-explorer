Overview

  • Speaker: Tanmai Gopal
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Security and architecture of company brains and AI agents in enterprise
  • Purpose: To provide enterprise AI engineers with an architectural blueprint for securely building and growing a company brain without leaking sensitive secrets or credentials. Tanmai Gopal, CEO and co-founder of PromptQL, discusses the risks of deploying 'company brains' and open-source AI tools across organizations. He outlines the security risks of sharing company secrets, the failure modes of team-based or agent-based knowledge silos, and proposes a four-rule architecture using a company-wide wiki with deterministic scope enforcement, sandboxing, and virtualized user credentials to prevent data leaks while enabling collaborative AI workflows.

Topic Map

The Company Brain Dilemma and Fear of Leaks

  • Explanation: Companies want a centralized 'company brain' to power AI agents across teams, but fear that interns or bad actors will access sensitive company secrets.
  • Key claims:
    • Building a company brain risks leaking company secrets.
    • Fear of leaks is the biggest barrier preventing companies from deploying open-source AI tools like OpenClaw or Hermes broadly.
    • Banks and Fortune 100 companies have strict security rules against vibe-coded AI agents running inside their networks.
  • Examples:
    • An intern joining a company and immediately gaining access to competitor details and sensitive company information.
  • Terminology:
    • company brain
    • OpenClaw
    • Hermes
    • vibe-coded AI agents
  • Why it matters: Security concerns block enterprises from adopting powerful AI agents and shared knowledge bases.

Growth Dynamics of a Healthy Company Brain

  • Explanation: Analyzing the contribution history and update frequency of a healthy company-wide wiki or brain over time.
  • Key claims:
    • A healthy company brain scales in size and experiences an increasing number of daily updates per day.
    • When AI agents and teams start working successfully, people continuously teach the system new skills, interpretations, and actions.
    • Systemic learning rates compound as users add querying, interpretation, action, and A/B testing skills.
  • Examples:
    • Wiki contributions per day increasing steadily from 0 to over 300 updates per day over a two-month period.
  • Terminology:

    • stewardship
  • Why it matters: Understanding how healthy knowledge graphs evolve helps organizations architect sustainable feedback loops.

Use Cases: Company AI and Collaborative Incident Management

  • Explanation: Exploring how company brains are used either as a shared context for individual AI agents or as a collaborative workspace for teams.
  • Key claims:
    • Use Case 1: Individual AI agents querying the company brain to get work done using existing company knowledge and skills.
    • Use Case 2: Collaborative incident management where multiple team members with different privilege levels collaborate via shared AI context.
    • Both use cases present significant security and escalation challenges.
  • Examples:
    • An engineer fetching logs, investigating codebases, raising a PR, setting up alerts, and deploying to production collaboratively with AI.
  • Terminology:

  • Why it matters: Different use cases require distinct security boundaries between personal context, team context, and company-wide knowledge.

Three Architectural Options for Company Brains

  • Explanation: Evaluating three architectural approaches for managing shared skills and memory: GitHub repos, team silos, and company-wide wikis.
  • Key claims:
    • Option 1: Everybody writes shared skills on GitHub. This fails because people do not have time to write skills for others.
    • Option 2: Keep memory per team (e.g., Slack channel memory). This creates another isolated silo.
    • Option 3: All context goes into a single shared wiki with deterministic scopes and human attribution.
  • Examples:
    • Claude Tag introducing team channel memory, resulting in siloed data that cannot be shared globally.
  • Terminology:
    • GitHub repo
    • team silos
    • shared wiki
    • scopes
  • Why it matters: Choosing the right architecture prevents data silos and unmanageable credential escalation.

The Four Rules for a Secure Company Brain

  • Explanation: Laying down four concrete design rules for building a secure company brain with company AI.
  • Key claims:
    • Rule 1: All context goes into a company-wide wiki and must have a human's name attached.
    • Rule 2: Wiki pages can have scopes that users can control deterministically.
    • Rule 3: Do not store credentials in the cloud sandbox.
    • Rule 4: Virtualize tool and data calls to inject user credentials dynamically so users control access.
  • Examples:
    • Adding context to a wiki page with explicit finance or personal scopes, reviewed and confirmed by a human.
  • Terminology:
    • company-wide wiki
    • scopes
    • cloud sandbox
    • credential virtualization
  • Why it matters: Following these four rules ensures high-quality context without privilege escalation or secret leakage.

Key Points

Grow, don't build, a company brain

  • Explanation: Companies cannot build a massive company brain top-down; instead, each person must own and build their own piece of the company brain incrementally.
  • Evidence: Organizations that are 100 years old cannot top-down engineer a complete company brain; it must emerge from individual contributions.
  • Practical implication: Empower every employee to contribute context and skills self-service while maintaining personal responsibility for security.

Prevent automatic agent memory writes

  • Explanation: Agents should not auto-add memory or wiki pages without human confirmation, as this leads to unverified, low-quality, or leaking context.
  • Evidence: Allowing agents to auto-write leads to untracked changes where nobody knows who added sensitive details.
  • Practical implication: Require agents to suggest additions with scopes, requiring human confirmation before merging into the wiki.

Never store credentials in the code sandbox

  • Explanation: Storing API keys or database credentials inside the cloud code sandbox allows any agent or user with access to execute unauthorized tools.
  • Evidence: If an engineer can merge PRs and an SRE manager can deploy to production, storing shared credentials in the sandbox lets low-privilege agents execute high-privilege actions.
  • Practical implication: Inject user credentials at the HTTP/SQL layer dynamically based on the initiating user's identity.

Four Rules for a Secure Company Brain

  • How it works: Combines a centralized wiki structure with human attribution, deterministic scope controls, sandboxed execution, and dynamic credential virtualization.

  • When to use: When deploying enterprise AI agents or company brains across multi-tier teams and regulated industries.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltystrong evidence

Credential virtualization: rather than storing API keys or DB credentials in the agent's cloud code sandbox, inject the initiating user's credentials dynamically at the HTTP/SQL layer so the agent's effective privileges are bounded by the requesting user's identity, not the sandbox's contents.

Why it matters

This removes the privilege-escalation path where a low-privilege agent or user can execute high-privilege actions simply by reaching shared secrets inside the execution environment. It converts authorization from a static artifact (stored secret) into a per-call, identity-derived decision.

Generalization

Applies to any agent runtime with tool/network access: treat the sandbox as untrusted compute and move credential resolution to an identity-aware proxy layer in front of tools and data.

Deep dives

4

Scope enforcement placement: index-time vs. query-time filtering and derived-artifact leakage

Research question

If a wiki page carries deterministic user-controlled scopes, at which point must filtering occur so that the page is provably absent from a lower-privilege user's context — including through summarization, embedding retrieval, and cached derived artifacts?

Why

The entire 'one shared brain for many privilege levels' design rests on scope enforcement being deterministic. If scopes are enforced only at storage or only at the top-k retrieval step, a low-privilege caller can still reconstruct protected content through summaries, embeddings, or cross-page inference — turning the single shared store into a broad leak surface instead of a local one.

Source video ↗

Attack surface of the credential-virtualizing proxy (confused deputy, SSRF, scope confusion)

Research question

What is the blast radius and failure mode set of an identity-aware layer that injects the initiating user's credentials per HTTP/SQL call — and can a low-privilege caller induce it to make a high-privilege call through confused-deputy, token passthrough, or scope-confusion patterns?

Why

Credential virtualization is presented as the fix, but it relocates trust from the sandbox to a new mediation component that sits on every tool and data call. If that component can be confused about who initiated a call, the escalation path returns in a subtler form, and the fix becomes the vulnerable asset.

Source video ↗

Review economics of the propose-then-confirm memory write path under high update volume

Research question

As wiki update volume scales toward hundreds of updates per day, does human confirmation of agent-proposed, scoped additions remain a real review step, or does it degrade into rubber-stamping with rising auto-approval and override-reversal rates?

Why

The write path is the only mechanism preventing unattributable or sensitive context from entering the brain. Its throughput limit, not its design, determines whether it survives contact with a growing brain — and a rubber-stamped review pipeline is functionally equivalent to the auto-write path it was meant to replace.

Is rising daily update volume a health metric or a noise metric?

Research question

Does an increasing daily wiki update rate correlate with downstream retrieval quality and agent task success, or does it mainly track duplication, churn, and low-value edits?

Why

'Healthy company brain' is operationalized in the talk as a monotonic rise in contributions per day. If that metric is uncorrelated with task outcomes, organizations will optimize a vanity counter while their context store degrades in precision, poisoning agent behavior at scale.

Your sandbox is not your trust boundary: credential virtualization moves the blast radius, it doesn't remove it

Pulling secrets out of the agent's code sandbox and injecting them per-request from the caller's identity is the right default, but it converts a static privilege-escalation bug into a confused-deputy problem at the injection layer — so the article argues the mediation proxy, not the sandbox, is where the security budget belongs.

Angle

Take the talk's four-rule architecture at face value, then stress it: enumerate how an identity-injecting HTTP/SQL layer can be induced to act on behalf of the wrong principal, and specify the invariants (call-bound identity binding, no token passthrough, per-call scope derivation) that must hold for the claim to be true.

Centralized retrieval plus artifact-level scopes beats per-team memory silos and shared skills repos — but only if scope enforcement is provable at context-assembly time, because centralizing context converts every scope bug from a local leak into a company-wide one.

Angle

Contrast the three sharing patterns the talk rejects (shared skills repo, per-team channel memory, auto-write) against the shared-wiki-with-scopes model, and argue that the design's viability is a function of enforcement placement rather than of retrieval quality.

Project ideas

4

Scope-Leak Harness

gatehouse

Query-time scope filtering on a shared context store leaks scoped content into lower-privilege contexts at a measurable nonzero rate via summarization and embedding retrieval, while index-time partitioning does not, and the gap is large enough to be a release blocker rather than a rounding error.

Proof of concept

Build a synthetic company wiki with tiered-scope pages, two retrieval modes (index-time partition vs. query-time filter), and three leak probes: top-k direct retrieval, summarization over mixed-privilege results, and nearest-neighbor reconstruction from embedding indexes; run each probe as a low-privilege caller and attempt to recover protected facts.

Measurement

Leakage rate (recovered protected facts per 100 queries) per filter placement and per probe type, plus the false-exclusion rate showing how much legitimate context index-time partitioning costs.

Source video ↗

Confused-Deputy Injection Proxy Red Team

gatehouse

An identity-aware credential-injecting proxy in front of HTTP/SQL tools can be induced to execute a call with a higher privilege than the initiating caller's identity in at least one of three patterns: replayed session binding, nested tool call inheriting the prior caller, or scope-confusion in a mediated call whose target is attacker-influenced.

Proof of concept

Stand up a minimal injection proxy that resolves credentials per call from a caller identity token, wrap a mock high-privilege data API behind it, and run a suite of adversarial agent traces (prompt-injected nested calls, session-replay, target-rewrite) against it.

Measurement

Number of adversarial traces producing a successful high-privilege call, classified by pattern, and the minimum proxy hardening change that drives each pattern to zero.

Source video ↗

Review-at-Scale Memory Pipeline

beyond-evals

At sustained volume above roughly 100 agent-proposed memory additions per day, human confirmation of scoped wiki additions degrades measurably — reviewer dwell time collapses and the unreviewed-or-auto-approved merge fraction rises — even though the pipeline's stated design requires genuine confirmation.

Proof of concept

Instrument a propose-scope-confirm-merge pipeline with synthetic but realistic agent suggestions at 10, 50, 150 and 300 additions/day, recording dwell time, edit-before-merge rate, and rejection rate per volume tier.

Measurement

Median review dwell time, edits-per-merge, rejection rate, and the slope of auto-approval as a function of daily proposal volume; flag the volume at which dwell time drops below a threshold indicating nominal review.

Source video ↗

Update-Rate vs. Task-Outcome Study

movement-lab

Rising daily wiki update volume is a noisy proxy for brain health: above some threshold, marginal updates correlate with duplication and contradiction in the context store and with flat or declining agent task success, not with improved success.

Proof of concept

Run a longitudinal instrumented deployment (or a simulated one seeded with the reported 0-to-300+ updates/day trajectory) that logs update rate alongside context-store duplication/contradiction metrics and a fixed agent task suite's success and error rates.

Measurement

Correlation between weekly update volume and agent task success rate, plus duplication and contradiction density in the store; report whether success improves, plateaus, or degrades past a volume inflection.

Architectural implications

3

Credentials are currently colocated with agent execution (secrets inside the cloud code sandbox).

Before

Secrets stored in the sandbox; any process or agent with sandbox access inherits their full authority.

After

Sandbox holds no secrets; an identity-aware layer injects the calling user's credentials per HTTP/SQL call.

Consequence

Authorization becomes per-request and identity-derived, collapsing the escalation path from low-privilege agent to high-privilege action, at the cost of needing a mediating proxy for every tool/data call.

Shared agent context is partitioned by team/channel rather than by artifact scope.

Before

Memory siloed per team (Slack channel memory), unshareable globally.

After

One company-wide wiki where each page carries deterministic, user-controlled scopes and human attribution.

Consequence

A single retrieval surface can serve mixed-privilege collaborators (e.g., incident response) while enforcement moves to page-level grants.

After

Consequence

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Single company-wide wiki vs. team-isolated memory

Benefit

Avoids silos and lets every agent/user retrieve the same shared context, enabling cross-privilege collaboration.

Cost or risk

Concentration risk: all sensitive context lives in one system, so the scope mechanism becomes a single critical enforcement point; a scope bug leaks broadly rather than locally.

Source video ↗

Shared skills repositories (GitHub) as the collaboration model

Benefit

Reuses familiar review tooling and existing developer workflows.

Cost or risk

Contribution economics fail — individuals have no time to write skills for others — so the repo stays empty and the shared-brain goal is not met.

Source video ↗

Agent autonomy in memory curation

Benefit

Captures agent-discovered context automatically and reduces manual authoring burden.

Cost or risk

Unverified, low-quality, or leaking context enters the brain with no responsible owner, making later remediation and attribution impossible.

Source video ↗

Convenience of shared credentials in the sandbox

Benefit

Simple setup: tools and agents can authenticate without per-user plumbing.

Cost or risk

Privilege escalation — a low-privilege agent inherits the highest privilege represented by any stored credential.
