Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Felienne Hermans
  • Channel: Domain-Driven Design Europe
  • Main topic: How AI and Large Language Models challenge our understanding of programming, community identity, and the values we assign to complex versus simple software systems.
  • Purpose: To provoke critical reflection on software engineering culture, values, biases, and the impact of AI by drawing parallels with linguistics, history, and sociology. Felienne Hermans, Professor of Computer Science Education at VU Amsterdam and high school CS teacher, explores how the rise of AI and LLMs caused her to doubt everything about programming. Reflecting on her creation of Hedy (a localized programming language), she examines how the programming community values hard, complex technologies over accessible ones, mirrors parallels with historical biases like von Neumann's war involvement, and contrasts programming with practices like knitting and chess to question why we build what we build.

Key Points

Elitism in Programming

  • Explanation: The tech industry looks down on spreadsheets and beginner languages, labeling them as 'not real programming'.
  • Evidence: Reactions from conference attendees when Felienne discussed her research on spreadsheets.
  • Practical implication: We must broaden our definition of programming to include accessible tools used by domain experts.

Localization and Cultural Inclusivity

  • Explanation: Programming languages are predominantly English-centric, excluding non-English speakers and cultures.
  • Evidence: Challenges faced when implementing Hedy in Arabic, including differences in numeral systems.
  • Practical implication: Localization is essential for global software accessibility and digital equity.

The 'Hard = Valuable' Fallacy

  • Explanation: Developers tend to equate difficulty with value, dismissing simple or user-friendly solutions.
  • Evidence: The contrast between how complex languages (C++, Haskell) and easy tools (Excel, Hedy) are regarded in academia and industry.
  • Practical implication: Value should be measured by the human problems solved and accessibility provided, not just technical difficulty.

The Purpose of Programming

  • Explanation: Programming is not just about writing efficient code; it is a human craft akin to knitting, literature, or art.
  • Evidence: Peter Naur's theory building perspective and David Graeber's philosophical insights.
  • Practical implication: Focus on the human, creative, and collaborative aspects of software development.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Programming as Theory Building (Peter Naur)

  • How it works: Programming is defined not merely as writing code, but as building a theory that allows people to understand, explain, and argue about complex domains.
  • Components:
    • Doing things intelligently
    • Explaining systems
    • Answering queries and arguing about code
  • When to use: When evaluating software design, team collaboration, and long-term maintainability.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

The programming community systematically equates technical difficulty with value, so making a capability easier is experienced as a loss of prestige rather than a gain in accessibility.

Why it matters

AI tooling and agent frameworks are constantly attacked as 'not real engineering' precisely because they lower the skill floor. Teams that internalize this bias underinvest in high-leverage, low-complexity infrastructure and over-engineer to signal legitimacy.

Generalization

Whenever a technology reduces the effort required to achieve an outcome, the incumbent practitioner community will discount the outcome to defend the status signal embedded in the effort.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

The Deep Blue precedent suggests that automating the competitive core of a human practice does not replace the practice: chess remained a human activity after a machine beat the world champion.

Why it matters

This is a concrete, dated counter-example to the assumption that AI coding capability means the disappearance of programming as a human discipline. It predicts a re-centering of human work around craft, intent, and community rather than raw code production.

Generalization

When a machine surpasses humans at a practice's measurable objective, the practice can survive by shifting its purpose from the score to the experience of participants.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Programming is framed as theory building: the artifact is not code but the shared mental theory the code encodes, which means code emission (including by LLMs) can be separated from the durable engineering asset.

Why it matters

If code is a lossy byproduct of a theory, then AI-generated code without a maintained human theory is a maintenance liability regardless of how correct it looks. This argues for investing in intent capture, decision records, and shared understanding rather than only in generation throughput.

Generalization

Any pipeline that automates artifact production but not the underlying model held by humans degrades the system's ability to evolve the artifact.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Localization is a first-class engineering constraint, not a translation layer: numeral systems, scripts, and syntax assumptions embedded in a language or tool surface as unexpected technical failures.

Why it matters

The Arabic-versus-Western-Arabic numeral collision shows that locale assumptions live deep in parsing and token representation. LLM tokenizers and prompt templates inherit the same class of hidden assumptions and will fail silently for non-English, non-Latin-script users.

Generalization

Any layer that hardcodes a dominant language's notational conventions (digits, direction, delimiters) creates failure modes that only appear for excluded user populations.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Why it matters

Locale Fuzz for LLM Toolcalls

beyond-evals

LLM tokenizers and tool-call parsers produce higher silent failure rates for inputs containing Eastern Arabic-Indic numerals and non-Latin scripts than for Western Arabic numerals and Latin script, and these failures are not surfaced as errors.

Proof of concept

Build a fuzzer that generates tool-call prompts and code snippets with numeral and script variations (Arabic-Indic digits, Devanagari, Hebrew RTL) and measures silent wrong outputs versus explicit errors across major LLM APIs.

Measurement

Silent failure rate per script and numeral system versus Latin baseline; proportion of failures caught by standard English-centric evals.

Theory Ledger

new

Maintaining a minimal human-held theory artifact (decision records plus executable specifications) alongside LLM-generated code increases the rate at which the system can be correctly evolved by humans after generation, compared to code-only handoff.

Proof of concept

Two teams implement the same feature with LLM generation; one maintains a theory ledger, the other receives only the generated code. Both then implement a novel change request; measure time, correctness, and regressions.

Measurement

Time to implement a novel change, correctness of the change, and count of regressions introduced.

Prestige Bias Audit

movement-lab

Engineering teams rate identical solutions as higher quality when presented with complexity-signaling language than with accessibility-signaling language, independent of function.

Proof of concept

Randomized survey with developers presenting two descriptions of functionally identical systems; one framed with complexity signals (e.g., custom C++ extension), the other with accessibility signals (e.g., spreadsheet macro). Measure perceived quality, maintainability, and adoption intent.

Measurement

Difference in Likert ratings and adoption intent between complexity-primed and accessibility-primed descriptions.

Architectural implications

3

Localization is treated as a surface concern rather than a structural property of a tool.

Before

Locale and script conventions are assumed to be English/Latin and handled (if at all) by a translation layer or UI strings.

After

Locale and notational conventions become explicit parameters of the language, parser, and tooling design, validated across numeral and script systems.

Consequence

Systems that ignore this will produce silent syntax-level failures for non-dominant-language users that cannot be fixed by translation.

Source video ↗

Code is treated as the deliverable and the primary unit of review.

Before

Reviews, handoffs, and metrics center on the code artifact.

After

The artifact is treated as a lossy encoding of a human-held theory; when generation is automated, theory capture and transfer become the maintained asset.

Consequence

Organizations that automate code production without preserving intent and rationale accumulate unmaintainable systems that pass review.

Source video ↗

Prestige is assigned to solutions by their implementation difficulty.

Before

Tool, language, and design choices are justified by complexity and the difficulty they signal.

After

Value is assigned by the human problem solved and the accessibility provided.

Consequence

This shifts what gets prototyped, funded, and kept in an AI-era stack toward simpler, higher-leverage abstractions.
