Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Sean Brown, Andy West, Sebastian Lemaire, Whitney Zimmerman
  • Channel: McKinsey & Company
  • Main topic: How organizations can build effective strategies and mobilize resources in an increasingly intense and uncertain competitive environment.
  • Purpose: To provide executives and strategists with frameworks, research insights, and practical advice on bridging the gap between bold strategic choices and sustained performance through effective mobilization and institutional capability building. In this episode of Inside the Strategy Room, McKinsey strategy leaders discuss why traditional strategy development is failing in today's high-uncertainty and high-intensity competitive landscape. They examine how the competitive reality has shifted, why many companies suffer from competitive advantage blindness, and why mobilization is the critical differentiator between market leaders and laggards. Drawing from research featured in Harvard Business Review and the upcoming book Strategy in Value, the guests explore how CEOs must adapt strategic decision-making, treat strategy as institutional investment, and confront bias and anchoring.

Topic Map

The Evolving Competitive Landscape and Strategic Uncertainty

  • Explanation: Discussion on why 2026 presents a critical strategy moment due to geopolitical shifts, AI disruption, supply chain uncertainty, and inflation, and how rapid market share shifts increase competitive intensity.
  • Key claims:
    • Strategy is an unfreezing moment of opportunity amidst high uncertainty.
    • Clients are digesting major disruptions including geopolitics, AI, supply chain uncertainty, and inflation.
    • Competitive intensity has grown as traditional industry boundaries shift and digital players enter traditional sectors.
  • Examples:
    • In France, new players in banking captured 15 million clients over five years while traditional banks lost 2 million clients.
  • Terminology:
    • shufflerate
    • competitive intensity
    • market share
  • Why it matters: When shufflerates are high and uncertainty is high, long-term strategic thinking becomes harder, requiring a higher level of conviction and debiasing.

Competitive Advantage Blindness and the Power Curve

  • Explanation: Examining why 57% of companies are confident they know their competitive advantage, but only 10% have real data backing it up, and how companies perform on the economic profit power curve.
  • Key claims:
    • Competitive advantage is often a weak signal that companies fail to track rigorously.
    • 9 out of 10 industrial companies experienced meaningful competitive advantage erosion, yet 75% had it priced into their share price.
    • Winners on the power curve invest twice as much as competitors in a given re-allocating capital.
  • Examples:
    • Research surveying 1,000 companies on competitive advantage found only 10% tracked drivers at a market level.
  • Terminology:
    • power curve
    • competitive advantage
    • economic profit
  • Why it matters: Not knowing whether your competitive advantage is strengthening or eroding creates a dangerous blind spot in strategic decision-making.

AI as a Strategic Inflection Point

  • Explanation: Analyzing how artificial intelligence disrupts markets across productivity, innovation, and value chain restructuring.
  • Key claims:
    • AI should be treated as a strategic inflection point rather than a mere productivity tool.
    • AI is exceptional at seeing weak signals and synthesizing competitive advantage data.
    • Companies must evaluate AI across three vectors: productivity gains, innovation/defense of profit pools, and new market structures/disintermediation.
  • Examples:
    • McKinsey tested 50 individual companies with AI tools to analyze sources of advantage and disruption threats.
  • Terminology:
    • hyperpersonalization
    • disintermediation
    • weak signals
  • Why it matters: AI disrupts traditional value chains and requires companies to rethink their strategic positioning and differentiation.

The Standard for Strategy and Mobilization

  • Explanation: Defining the standards required for effective strategy and why mobilization must be treated as an institutional capability rather than an afterthought.
  • Key claims:
    • Vague strategy is impossible to mobilize; setting a high standard is essential.
    • Anchoring to last year's budget is a primary trap that undercuts mobilization.
    • Mobilization must be institutionalized so that strategic choices are automatically backed by aligned resource reallocation.
  • Examples:
    • Comparing companies that rose up the power curve versus those that fell down due to strategic missteps.
  • Terminology:
    • anchoring effect
    • mobilization muscle
    • value creation thesis
  • Why it matters: Without proper mobilization and institutional standards, bold strategic choices fail to translate into sustained performance.

Key Points

Competitive Advantage Blindness

  • Explanation: Most companies believe they know their competitive advantage, but very few track it rigorously with market-level data.
  • Evidence: Survey of 1,000 companies showed 57% were confident, but only 10% tracked drivers at a market level.
  • Practical implication: CEOs must establish rigorous data-driven tracking of competitive advantage to avoid eroding differentiation.

The Danger of Budget Anchoring

  • Explanation: Anchoring strategy to last year's budget undermines strategic re-allocation and prevents bold moves.
  • Evidence: Research on corporate budgeting habits shows the first year of strategic financial plans is often forgotten in favor of incremental budgeting.
  • Practical implication: Organizations must make the first year of strategic financial plans their actual budget starting point.

Mobilization as an Institutional Capability

  • Explanation: Mobilization cannot be ad hoc; it requires institutional processes, clear language, and dedicated resources.
  • Evidence: Winners on the economic profit power curve invest twice as much as competitors to make a difference.
  • Practical implication: Treat mobilization as an ongoing institutional investment rather than an afterthought to strategy formulation.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

The Ten Tests of Strategy

  • How it works: A diagnostic tool used to measure the quality and distinctiveness of any given corporate strategy.
  • Components:
    • Beating the market
    • Tapping true sources of advantage
    • Quality of historical strategy trajectory
    • Customer fragmentation
    • New platforms
  • When to use: When evaluating the robustness and potential impact of a corporate strategy against market averages.

AI Strategic Vectors

  • How it works: A three-vector framework for tracking and incorporating artificial intelligence into corporate strategy.
  • Components:
    • Productivity gains through automation and workflow redesign
    • Innovation to expand and defend profit pools (hyperpersonalization)
    • Deliberate choices to shape new market structures and disintermediation
  • When to use: When assessing AI disruption and opportunities within an industry.

Examples & Case Studies

In France, new digital and insurance players entered banking over five years, capturing 15 million clients while traditional banks lost 2 million clients.

  • Illustrates: The speed at which market share and competitive boundaries shift across industries.
  • Lesson: Traditional industry boundaries no longer protect incumbents from cross-sector competitors.

McKinsey analyzed 50 industrial companies by tearing down their sources of advantage and value, comparing capital markets view with disruption threats.

  • Illustrates: How AI can be used to detect weak signals and erosion of competitive advantage.
  • Lesson: 9 out of 10 companies faced meaningful competitive advantage erosion, yet most had it priced into share prices.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Audit your company's competitive advantage with rigorous market-level data rather than assumptions.
    • Uncouple next year's budget from last year's incremental baseline.
    • Evaluate your strategy against the ten tests of strategy.
  • Strategic:
    • Treat mobilization as an institutional capability embedded in culture and operating models.
    • Stack-rank bold moves and portfolio choices alongside other funding commitments.
    • Leverage AI to detect weak signals of competitive erosion and shifting profit pools.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • Are we confident in our competitive advantage, or are we anchored to past success?
    • How well do our strategic choices translate into actual capital reallocation?
    • What weak signals in our market are we currently failing to measure?

Core insights

[!]Failure Modemedium noveltystrong evidence

There is a large, measurable gap between self-reported confidence and data-backed knowledge: 57% of companies believed they knew their competitive advantage but only 10% actually tracked the drivers with market-level data, and 9 of 10 industrial companies experienced meaningful advantage erosion while 75% had it priced into their share price. The generalizable engineering lesson is that confidence in system quality is a poor proxy for instrumented measurement of the specific mechanisms that create the advantage.

Why it matters

Engineers who rely on team confidence about agent or system quality are likely to miss silent capability erosion, because the erosions are weak signals that only surface with deliberate, driver-level instrumentation.

Generalization

Belief-versus-measurement gaps are a structural risk in any system where the value-creating mechanism is not directly observed; instrument the specific drivers of advantage, not aggregate satisfaction.

[M]Mechanismmedium noveltymoderate evidence

LLMs are positioned as weak-signal detectors: the claim is that AI is exceptional at seeing weak signals and synthesizing competitive advantage data, and McKinsey operationally tested this by running AI tools against 50 individual companies to analyze sources of advantage and disruption threats. This suggests an emerging role for agentic systems as synthesis layers over fragmented, low-signal evidence rather than as pure generators.

Why it matters

It points to a concrete agent architecture: ingest many heterogeneous weak signals and synthesize them into a defensible conclusion, rather than prompt for a single answer.

Generalization

Weak-signal synthesis is a distinct, testable capability class for LLM systems and can be benchmarked by comparing AI-derived conclusions against human analyst conclusions on the same corpus.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

AI should be evaluated along three distinct vectors rather than as a single productivity tool: productivity gains through automation and workflow redesign, innovation to expand and defend profit pools via hyperpersonalization, and deliberate choices to shape new market structures and disintermediation. The third vector is the architecturally interesting one because it implies AI changes where the system boundary and value capture sit, not just how fast a step runs.

Why it matters

Evaluating an AI system only on efficiency (the first vector) systematically misses boundary-restructuring effects (the third vector) that can invalidate the surrounding architecture.

Generalization

Any AI evaluation rubric should be multi-vector: efficiency, capability expansion, and structural/boundary change, and these vectors have different failure modes and measurement approaches.

[!]Failure Modemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Anchoring to a prior baseline ('last year's budget') is identified as a primary trap that undercuts reallocation, and the proposed correction is to make the first year of the strategic plan the actual budget starting point rather than deriving from the incremental previous year. This is a resource-allocation failure mode with a direct analogue in iterative system tuning, where anchoring to the previous eval baseline or run configuration suppresses larger reallocation of effort, context, or compute.

Why it matters

Incremental anchoring is comfortable but systematically prevents the bold reallocations that separate top performers; the correction is procedural, not motivational.

Generalization

Baseline anchoring is a general failure mode in any iterative allocation loop (budgets, compute, context, tooling) and is mitigated by re-deriving the allocation from the target rather than the prior state.

[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Mobilization is only effective when institutionalized so that choices are automatically backed by aligned resource reallocation, rather than being ad hoc; it requires institutional processes, clear language, and dedicated resources. The architectural reading is that execution capability should live in durable infrastructure (runtimes, harnesses, workflows) that enforces alignment, not in discretionary per-task effort.

Why it matters

Ad hoc execution does not compound and does not survive personnel or context changes; institutionalized mechanisms make the desired behavior the default rather than an act of will.

Generalization

If a desired behavior is not encoded into the system's default path (harness, policy, pipeline), it will degrade under load and turnover.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Vague strategy is impossible to mobilize; setting a high standard of specificity is framed as a precondition rather than a nice-to-have. This implies a spec-quality gate: ambiguous objectives cannot be reliably executed downstream, no matter how strong the execution capability.

Why it matters

Agent and pipeline reliability is bounded by the specificity of the objective it is handed; investing in spec precision may yield larger reliability gains than improving the executor.

Generalization

Treat objective clarity as an upstream dependency of execution reliability; testable by comparing task success rates across vague versus precisely specified objectives on the same executor.

Benchmarking LLM weak-signal synthesis against expert human analysts

Research question

How can we measure and validate LLM weak-signal synthesis capability (precision, recall, calibration) on heterogeneous, low-signal corpora, relative to expert human analysts?

Why

The claim that AI is exceptional at weak-signal synthesis is currently untested and unbenchmarked. Without a rigorous measurement framework, agent architectures built on this premise risk overconfidence and silent failure.

Operationalizing the three-vector AI evaluation with distinct metrics

Research question

How do you decompose an AI system's impact into productivity, profit-pool defense/innovation, and structural/disintermediation vectors with separate measurable indicators, and can a system excel on one vector while failing on another?

Why

Evaluating only on productivity hides architecture-level boundary changes and disintermediation effects. Without per-vector instrumentation, organizations miss the most disruptive and strategically important effects.

Instrumenting competitive advantage drivers vs. self-reported confidence

Research question

How do you instrument the specific drivers of competitive advantage at market granularity to detect silent erosion before it shows up in aggregate outcomes?

Why

The 57% confidence vs. 10% data-backed knowledge gap, and the 9-in-10 erosion detection failure, show that confidence is a poor proxy for measurable advantage. Driver-level instrumentation is necessary to detect silent erosion.

Avoiding baseline anchoring while preserving regression detection

Research question

Why

Baseline anchoring suppresses bold reallocation, but evaluation requires stable baselines. Reconciling target-derived allocation with reliable regression measurement is a practical design problem for iterative systems tuning (compute, context, tooling).

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Budget anchoring in resource allocation

Benefit

Incremental budgeting from a prior baseline is predictable, low-friction, and easy to defend.

Cost or risk

It systematically suppresses bold reallocation and undercuts mobilization, so strategic choices never translate into resource movement.

Competitive advantage blindness

Benefit

High confidence without driver-level data allows faster, less contested decisions.

Cost or risk

Advantage erosion goes undetected even while the market continues to price the advantage in, creating a delayed and severe correction.

Vague strategy as an execution constraint

Benefit

High-level, non-committal strategy is flexible and avoids committing to hard trade-offs.

Cost or risk

It is impossible to mobilize, so even strong execution capability cannot compensate for ambiguous objectives.

Reallocation intensity as a differentiator

Benefit

Cost or risk
