Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Andy Doyle & Molly Wood
  • Channel: Microsoft
  • Main topic: Enterprise AI agent transformation, workforce change management, and human-centric technology adoption at Kantar
  • Purpose: To explore how a non-tech enterprise successfully integrated AI agents into everyday operations, managed employee anxiety around workplace change, and fostered a culture of continuous learning and agent building. Andy Doyle, Chief People & Agent Officer at Kantar, discusses how the global marketing data and analytics company transformed its workforce by deploying over 15,000 AI agents. Kantar transitioned from traditional software deployment to a human-centric change management approach, achieving high daily usage rates, significant time savings, and new cultural paradigms where non-technical employees build AI agents to solve business problems.

Key Points

Agents Outnumber Employees

  • Explanation: Kantar operates with over 15,000 AI agents for a workforce of 12,000 employees, ranging from personal productivity helpers to enterprise tools.
  • Evidence: 15,000+ agents deployed across 12,000 employees with an 85% daily usage rate.
  • Practical implication: Enterprises can scale operational efficiency by enabling employees to build and utilize multiple specialized agents.

Business-Led Agent Building

  • Explanation: Agents were built largely by business professionals rather than dedicated IT teams, ensuring solutions directly target actual operational friction.
  • Evidence: The people agent for HR queries built by non-tech staff achieving high automation rates.
  • Practical implication: Democratizing agent creation bridges the gap between technical capability and business domain expertise.

Continuous Weekly Training vs. One-Off Rollouts

  • Explanation: Kantar conducts weekly training sessions every Tuesday rather than relying on initial onboarding to ensure teams keep pace with fast-evolving AI capabilities.
  • Evidence: Weekly Tuesday training programs covering new AI features and agent building.
  • Practical implication: Ongoing education prevents employees from falling back into legacy work habits.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Kantar decouples agent creation from agent governance: an internal 'Agent Factory' lets business units generate agents freely while centralizing IP control and governance. This is a federated-authoring / centralized-control architecture rather than either fully centralized IT delivery or ungoverned citizen development.

Why it matters

Most agent platforms force a choice between a single centrally-owned assistant and unmanaged sprawl of user-created bots. This pattern suggests the viable middle is a platform boundary: permissive authoring surface plus a central registry/policy layer that owns identity, IP, and lifecycle — the same split that internal developer platforms use for services.

Generalization

Agent platforms should be designed as authoring plane + control plane, not as a monolith or a free-for-all.

Project ideas

4

Promotion Gate Prototype for No-Code to Pro-Code Agents

gatehouse

Automated promotion gates based on data sensitivity, blast radius, and autonomy level can let business-authored agents graduate to governed production without manual review of every agent, while catching high-risk agents before they ship.

Proof of concept

Define a three-tier promotion policy and implement a gate that blocks or requires review when an agent touches sensitive HR data or requests high-autonomy tool access; test against the People Agent scenario.

Measurement

False positive and false negative rate of the gate against expert review; time-to-production for low-risk agents; number of high-risk agents intercepted before production.

Source video ↗

Agent Usage Depth Telemetry

beyond-evals

Seat and session usage metrics overstate agent integration; depth metrics (tasks completed end-to-end, human handoff rate, escalation rate) will reveal a materially lower value-producing usage rate than headline daily usage.

Proof of concept

Instrument a small agent deployment to log task completion, handoff, and escalation events; compare depth metrics against simple daily-active usage over 4–6 weeks.

Measurement

Ratio of depth-active users to daily-active users; end-to-end task completion rate; handoff and escalation rates; correlation with self-reported time savings.

Source video ↗

Agent Catalog & Staleness Scorer

gatehouse

In an agent portfolio where agents outnumber employees, a lightweight catalog with ownership metadata, staleness scoring, and duplicate detection can reduce orphaned agents and redundant agent creation by a measurable margin within one quarter.

Proof of concept

Build a registry that ingests agent definitions from no-code, low-code, and pro-code tiers, assigns ownership, computes last-run and last-modified staleness, and flags near-duplicate intents; test on a synthetic or small real portfolio.

Measurement

Percentage of agents with identified owner; number of duplicate clusters detected; median time from staleness flag to deprecation or refresh; reduction in new duplicate agents after catalog introduction.

Source video ↗

Weekly Enablement as Release-Coupled Service

movement-lab

Treating enablement as a fixed weekly cadence versioned with platform releases will reduce capability drift and legacy-workflow fallback more than one-off onboarding, measurable through feature adoption and fallback rates.

Proof of concept

Run a weekly Tuesday training loop for one business unit, publish training as release artifacts, and instrument adoption of new agent-building features and fallback to legacy workflows.

Measurement

Time from feature release to first successful employee-authored agent using it; percentage of employees attempting new features within two weeks; decline in legacy-workflow fallback events.

Architectural implications

4

Agent supply is federated across business units rather than owned by IT.

Before

Central IT team authors and ships a small number of enterprise agents.

After

Business professionals author no-code/low-code agents, with central control limited to governance and IP.

Consequence

The platform must provide policy, identity, data-boundary, and lifecycle primitives at the authoring layer, because reviewers cannot inspect every agent before it ships.

Source video ↗

Agents are tiered by creation complexity (no-code, low-code, pro-code) rather than by business function.

Before

Agents are categorized by team, department, or use case.

After

Agents are categorized by the skill level required to build and maintain them.

Consequence

The tier becomes a promotion path: an agent can graduate from personal no-code prototype to governed pro-code enterprise tool, which requires a defined escalation and ownership handoff.

Source video ↗

Agent population exceeds employee population while spanning personal helpers to enterprise tools.

Before

A single assistant with a single tool surface, memory model, and evaluation suite.

After

Thousands of narrow agents with heterogeneous scopes and owners.

Consequence

Discovery, deduplication, per-agent cost/usage attribution, and deprecation become first-class platform features rather than afterthoughts.

Source video ↗

Adoption is driven by a recurring weekly enablement loop tied to new feature releases.

Before

One-off onboarding at launch; users operate a frozen feature set.

After

Continuous feature ingestion by users on a fixed weekly cadence.

Consequence

Enablement must be versioned alongside the platform, and training content becomes part of the release process.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Democratized agent creation

Benefit

Solutions target real operational friction because domain experts, not IT, define the problem.

Cost or risk

Central governance and IP control become harder to enforce as authorship spreads to non-engineers.

Source video ↗

Agent sprawl (agents outnumber employees)

Benefit

Enables many narrow, domain-fit agents rather than one generic assistant.

Cost or risk

True ROI of decentralized deployments becomes difficult to measure across thousands of small agents.

Source video ↗

Failing forward / low-stakes experimentation

Benefit

Lowers the psychological barrier that stops employees from adopting agents at all.

Cost or risk

A tolerant stance toward failed attempts can coexist with inadequate review of agents that reach production or touch sensitive data.

Source video ↗

No-replacement headcount policy

Benefit

Addresses job-security anxiety directly, which the summary ties to adoption.

Cost or risk

Agents must absorb the workload of departing staff, raising the reliability bar for agents that were built by non-technical authors.

Source video ↗

Broad-simultaneous rollout over staged pilot

Benefit

Surfaces unexpected grassroots use cases and builds employee ownership.

Cost or risk

Less control over early usage patterns and weaker ability to intercept bad practices before they spread.
