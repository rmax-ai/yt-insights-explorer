Overview

  • Speaker: Mihail Eric
  • Channel: Stanford Graduate School of Business
  • Main topic: Artificial Intelligence history, terminology, architecture, and business applications in 2026
  • Purpose: To educate business students on the technological foundations, architecture trends, terminology, and enterprise software opportunities of artificial intelligence in 2026. Mihail Eric, Head of AI at Monaco and Stanford CS alumnus, delivers a guest lecture at Stanford Graduate School of Business titled 'AI in 2026: Soup to Nuts.' He traces the history of AI from 2012 deep learning to 2017 transformers and 2024 reasoning models. Eric provides a terminology primer covering agents, harnesses, system prompts, tools, and skills, contrasts pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning, and explores infrastructure economics including neoclouds and data providers.

Topic Map

History of AI

  • Explanation: Chronological timeline of AI breakthroughs leading to 2026, breaking down deep learning, transformers, ChatGPT, and reasoning models.
  • Key claims:
    • AI progress is built on 15+ years of waves rather than appearing overnight in 2022.
    • 2012-2016 deep learning saw the rebirth of neural networks driven by consumer gaming GPUs and ImageNet data.
    • 2017 transformer architecture introduced next-token prediction on web data, becoming the backbone of all modern LLMs.
  • Examples:
    • ImageNet dataset created by Fei-Fei Li at Stanford enabling image classification rebirth.
    • Attention Is All You Need paper published by Google researchers in 2017.
  • Terminology:
    • Deep learning
    • Transformer architecture
    • Next-token prediction
    • ImageNet
  • Why it matters: Grounds current AI capabilities in decades of cumulative technological breakthroughs rather than sudden hype.

Terminology Primer: The Agent Loop

  • Explanation: A structural breakdown of what constitutes an AI agent, including models, harnesses, system prompts, tools, and skills.
  • Key claims:
    • An agent is a model combined with instructions, tools, and an execution loop.
    • The interface and harness are just as important as the underlying intelligence model.
    • Context engineering dynamically assembles the model input based on goals and retrieved skills.
  • Examples:
    • Building an investment memo for a seed-stage company using Claude Opus 3.5 with product management skills and web search tools.
  • Terminology:
    • Agent
    • Harness
    • System prompt
    • Tools
    • Skills
    • Context engineering
  • Why it matters: Provides non-technical business students with the precise vocabulary needed to evaluate and build AI software workflows.

The Modern-Day Model Recipe

  • Explanation: The three-stage training recipe for modern AI models: pretraining, supervised fine-tuning, and alignment/reinforcement learning.
  • Key claims:
    • Pretraining processes the entirety of web data via next-token prediction, creating base completion models.
    • Supervised fine-tuning provides input-output pairs to align model outputs with specific tasks like memos or spreadsheets.
    • Reasoning reinforcement learning uses verifiable rewards and test-time compute to unlock complex math and coding tasks.
  • Examples:
    • OpenAI o1 solving complex math benchmarks like the AIME using test-time compute and verifiable rewards.
  • Terminology:
    • Pretraining
    • Supervised fine-tuning
    • RLHF
    • Test-time compute
    • Verifiable rewards
  • Why it matters: Demystifies how raw web data is transformed into reasoning agents capable of enterprise workflows.

Enterprise Applications and Vertical Agents

  • Explanation: Examination of vertical software agents across coding, legal, sales, and customer support verticals.
  • Key claims:
    • Vertical software agents represent one of the biggest economic opportunities in enterprise software.
    • The playbook involves building a platform to capture domain-specific workflows and codifying them via agent harnesses.
    • Outcome-based pricing is replacing traditional seat-based SaaS pricing as agents automate complete workflows.
  • Examples:

Infrastructure Economics: Neoclods and Data Providers

    • AI infrastructure makes up over $2T in economic value, spanning data providers, neoclouds, model labs, and inference providers.

  • Examples:

Key Points

Models vs. Harnesses

  • Explanation: The intelligence layer (model) must be paired with a robust harness (tools, prompts, skills) to create useful business applications.
  • Evidence: Transformer models existed in 2017, but consumer interfaces and harnesses unlocked mass adoption in 2022.
  • Practical implication: Companies should invest in capturing domain workflows via custom harnesses rather than solely relying on raw model intelligence.

Test-Time Compute and Reasoning

  • Explanation: Giving models more compute capacity at test time ('thinking') dramatically improves performance on hard math and coding problems.
  • Evidence: OpenAI o1 AIME math benchmark accuracy scales logarithmically with test-time compute.
  • Practical implication: Unlocks multi-step autonomous agent workflows for complex enterprise tasks.

Outcome-Based Pricing Models

  • Explanation: Traditional per-seat SaaS pricing is giving way to pricing based on successful task completion or token usage.
  • Evidence: Customer support and vertical agents charge per resolved ticket rather than per human agent seat.
  • Practical implication: Business founders must align software pricing with direct business outcomes generated by AI agents.

The harness (instructions, tools, execution loop, interface) is the primary differentiator for useful AI applications, not raw model intelligence. The same transformer intelligence existed in 2017 but only produced mass adoption once harnesses and consumer interfaces were built around it.

Why it matters

Shifts engineering investment from chasing the newest model to designing the orchestration layer (system prompts, tools, skills, context assembly) where product value and defensibility actually accrue.

Generalization

For any capability where the underlying engine is commoditizing, the wrapping runtime/orchestration layer becomes the locus of competitive advantage and product differentiation.

[M]Mechanismmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Context engineering is a distinct runtime responsibility that dynamically assembles the model input from the user goal plus retrieved skills, rather than a static prompt. It sits inside the harness between goal intake and model invocation.

Why it matters

Treating context assembly as a dynamic, goal-driven runtime component (not a fixed system prompt) changes how memory, retrieval, and skill selection are architected and where latency/cost are incurred.

Generalization

Any agent runtime benefits from separating static instructions from dynamic, goal-conditioned context assembly, enabling selective retrieval and skill injection.

[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Reasoning reinforcement learning is gated by verifiability: it uses verifiable rewards plus test-time compute, which is why it unlocks math and coding but is described as applied to those specific verifiable domains.

Why it matters

Implies the strongest reasoning gains are concentrated where an automated correctness signal exists; domains without verifiable rewards cannot directly use this training loop and may need proxy rewards or human evaluation.

Generalization

Training/optimization loops scale fastest where an automated, cheap, correct reward signal is available; progress stalls in domains lacking verifiable outcomes.

Project ideas

Harness Ablation Testbed

beyond-evals

For a fixed base model, systematic variation of harness components (system prompt, tool set, context assembly) yields larger variance in task success rate than swapping between top-tier models.

Proof of concept

Build a testbed that runs a suite of business tasks (e.g., investment memo drafting) through multiple harness configurations and 3-4 different models, capturing success metrics.

Measurement

ANOVA of task success rate across harness configs vs across models; effect size comparison.

Source video ↗

Proxy Reward Forge

new

LLM-generated preference rankings can serve as a sufficiently consistent proxy reward signal to improve model performance on non-verifiable advisory tasks via reinforcement learning, achieving at least 80% of the improvement seen in verifiable domains.

Proof of concept

Curate a dataset of advisory tasks (e.g., business strategy recommendations). Generate multiple model outputs, use an LLM judge to rank them, and fine-tune a model using the rankings as a reward signal. Compare to baseline and to a verifiable task (e.g., math).

Measurement

Human-expert quality rating improvement on held-out advisory tasks vs baseline; compare to math task improvement.

Source video ↗

Workflow-to-Harness Compiler

new

Semi-automated capture of domain expert actions can be compiled into reusable harness components (prompts, tools, skills) with a reuse rate exceeding 60% across new instances of the same workflow.

Proof of concept

Instrument a workflow (e.g., seed-stage investment memo creation). Record expert steps, generate harness artifacts, and apply them to new companies. Measure reuse (how many artifacts are used unchanged) and task success.

Measurement

Percentage of harness components reused across tasks; improvement in task success vs. generic agent.

Source video ↗

Dynamic Compute Router

movement-lab

A difficulty predictor can dynamically allocate test-time compute such that average accuracy on a mixed-difficulty math/coding benchmark matches uniform high compute, while reducing average compute by at least 30%.

Proof of concept

Train a lightweight model to predict task difficulty from the query. At inference, allocate compute budget based on prediction. Compare to fixed budgets (low, high).

Measurement

Accuracy vs. average compute tradeoff curve; Pareto improvement over fixed budgets.

Architectural implications

3

Model intelligence becomes a swappable component while harness, tools, skills, and context engineering carry the product value.

Before

Architecture centered on selecting the strongest model as the core value driver.

After

Architecture centered on the harness/orchestration layer, treating models as replaceable backends.

Consequence

Model-agnostic abstractions and versioned harnesses become first-class engineering artifacts, enabling rapid model swaps without product rewrite.

Context is assembled dynamically per goal from retrieved skills rather than baked into a static system prompt.

Before

Fixed system prompt containing all instructions and context.

After

A runtime context-engineering stage that composes goals + retrieved skills before model invocation.

Consequence

Introduces new latency/cost and retrieval-reliability concerns, and makes skill selection/ranking a testable subsystem.

Reasoning capability is coupled to verifiable reward signals, so only some tasks benefit from the reasoning RL loop.

Before

Assumption that stronger models uniformly improve all tasks.

After

Domain-specific expectation that verifiable domains (math, coding) advance fastest, others lag.

Consequence

Architects should route verifiable tasks to reasoning models with test-time compute and treat non-verifiable domains as needing separate evaluation and reward design.
