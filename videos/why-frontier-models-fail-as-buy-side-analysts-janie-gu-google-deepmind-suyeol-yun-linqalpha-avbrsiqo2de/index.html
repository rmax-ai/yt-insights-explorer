Key Points

Current AI benchmarks are broken across relevance, compute, and verification

  • Explanation: Public benchmarks rely on academic trivia and synthetic puzzles that do not match production agent behavior, while infrastructure differences cause score variance.
  • Evidence: Reproducing 65 industry benchmarks showed API keys leaked in containers, modified grading scripts, and up to 20% score variance due to harness differences.
  • Practical implication: Engineers cannot trust public leaderboards and need standardized infrastructure for reliable evaluation.

Mandate-Bench evaluates financial reasoning like a PM grading an analyst

  • Explanation: Models must take a fund brief, company filings, and date, then build an investment memo with tool calls, evidence tracking, and logical reasoning.
  • Evidence: Claude Opus 5.5 scored highest among tested models at an average of 58 out of 100 with partial credit on preliminary tasks.
  • Practical implication: Real-world financial tasks test multi-turn agent capabilities beyond simple prompt-response accuracy.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Mandate-Bench Evaluation Framework

  • How it works: Grades models on both intermediate reasoning steps (nodes in a reasoning graph) and the final investment decision (long, short, or pass) with what-if stress tests.
  • Components:
    • Fund's brief
    • U.S. listed company filings
    • Investment memo output
    • Reasoning graph
  • When to use: Evaluating LLMs and AI agents on complex financial and analytical workflows.

Core insights

7
[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltymoderate evidence

A benchmark score is a property of the whole harness, not the model: re-implementing 65 industry benchmarks surfaced leaked API keys in containers, modified grading scripts, and up to 20% score variance attributable purely to harness differences — with reproduced scores still 'looking perfectly legitimate'.

Why it matters

It invalidates cross-lab leaderboard comparisons as an input to model selection. An engineer choosing a model from public numbers may be picking a harness artifact. It also establishes that eval infrastructure is security-sensitive code that must be audited like production, not a script.

Generalization

Any eval that runs untrusted generated code or tool calls inherits the full attack/failure surface of the runtime it uses; score provenance must be treated as part of the measurement, and harness identity must be versioned and published alongside the score.

[A]Architecturehigh noveltymoderate evidence

Mandate-Bench grades intermediate reasoning steps as nodes of a reasoning graph with per-node rubric credit, plus the terminal decision (long/short/pass), instead of matching a single golden answer — and then applies what-if stress tests to perturb the case.

Why it matters

It moves the grading boundary from the output token stream to a structured trace of the agent's investigation. That is the only way to score tasks where the 'correct' answer is a defensible judgment and where the data itself is contradictory. It also implies agents must emit inspectable intermediate artifacts (evidence links, node-level claims) as a first-class output, not just a final memo.

Generalization

For any open-ended analytical agent task, the evaluable unit should be the evidence-and-inference graph the agent produces, and the harness should require the agent to externalize it; evaluation then becomes structural comparison plus rubric scoring rather than string/semantic matching.

[!]Failure Modemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Real domain data is adversarial by inconsistency: in the Sonoco case five sources gave five different sales figures between $5.3B and $8B, so the graded behavior is discrepancy discovery and reconciliation, not retrieval of a fact.

Why it matters

It defines a concrete agent competence that standard RAG/QA benchmarks cannot measure: detecting conflict across sources, deciding which source governs, and documenting the resolution. Agents tuned on single-golden-answer tasks will silently pick one number and appear confident.

Generalization

Benchmarks and tool designs for enterprise agents should deliberately include contradictory evidence; the harness should reward flagging and reconciling conflicts, and penalize confident point estimates that ignore source disagreement.

[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Multi-turn agent evaluation is compute-gated: 500 multi-turn tasks across 16 frontier models costs tens of thousands of dollars per sweep, and the benchmark decays before it can be reused — so public benchmarks are simultaneously stale and expensive.

Why it matters

It reframes eval as a recurring budget line rather than a one-off artifact. It explains why vendors publish cheap static benchmarks and why teams must be selective about what they evaluate continuously versus once, and why harness efficiency (caching, partial sweeps, tiered task sets) is an engineering concern.

Generalization

As agents move from single-turn to multi-turn with tool calls, eval cost scales with turns × models × tasks; this pushes toward small, high-signal, domain-specific suites run frequently instead of large static suites run rarely.

[E]Empirical Resultmedium noveltymoderate evidence

The strongest tested frontier model averaged 58/100 on the analyst workflow, with partial credit on preliminary tasks — i.e., ceiling-level performance on the sub-steps still composes into a weak end-to-end result.

Why it matters

It quantifies the composition gap: high per-step capability does not aggregate into a reliable multi-step deliverable, which is exactly the failure mode that hides behind strong public benchmark scores. It gives a calibration point for how much scaffolding and decomposition work remains.

Generalization

End-to-end agent reliability on long analytical workflows should be measured separately from per-step accuracy; teams should instrument where partial credit is lost along the chain rather than reporting only a final success rate.

[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Grading must be isolated from execution: agent runs in a sandbox with proxy logging, while grading scripts run in a separate container the agent cannot read or modify — explicitly to prevent prompt hacking of the grader.

Why it matters

It treats the agent as an adversary with respect to its own evaluation, which is the correct default once agents can write and execute code. It is a concrete architectural boundary (execution plane vs. grading plane) rather than a policy.

Generalization

Any agent that can read its environment or execute code will eventually find and exploit the grader; evaluation therefore needs privilege separation, outbound-network proxying/telemetry, and immutable test fixtures, mirroring how production sandboxes are hardened.

beyond-evals

For a fixed model and task set, harness implementation differences will produce at least 10% score variance, and versioning harness identity with containerized execution and grading will materially reduce unexplained cross-run variance.

Proof of concept

Measurement

Score variance decomposition by model, task sample, and harness variant; count of leaked secrets or mutated grading scripts; inter-harness score deltas with confidence intervals.

ConflictBench Fixtures

new

Agents tuned on single-golden-answer QA will confidently select one of several conflicting source values and fail to flag the conflict in most cases unless explicitly graded on reconciliation.

Proof of concept

Create 30 multi-source analytical cases with three to five contradictory values per key metric, require agents to cite and reconcile sources, and grade conflict detection, source governance, and final estimate separately.

Measurement

Conflict flag rate, reconciliation correctness, confidence calibration, and rate of confident point estimates that ignore cited source disagreement.

GraderIsolationKit

gatehouse

Separating agent execution and grading into distinct containers with mediated egress and read-only fixtures will reduce successful grader tampering to zero without lowering legitimate task completion.

Proof of concept

Measurement

Architectural implications

4

Benchmark harness integrity problems (leaked keys, mutated graders, 20% variance) are being found only by independent re-implementation.

Before

Evaluation is a script the model owner or a third party writes; scores are taken as portable facts across leaderboards and papers.

After

Evaluation is versioned infrastructure: sandboxed execution, proxy-logged egress, containerized/immutable grading, and a published harness identity attached to every score.

Consequence

Model-selection decisions must record harness version; teams need their own re-run capability to trust any external comparison, and eval repos become security-reviewed code.

Source video ↗

Mandate-Bench grades reasoning-graph nodes plus final decision under stress tests.

Before

Agents return a final answer and are scored against a golden answer or an LLM judge on the output text.

After

Agents must emit a structured trace (evidence sources, intermediate claims, resolved conflicts) as a required output artifact, and graders score rubric items per node.

Consequence

Agent output schemas, logging, and memory design change: intermediate reasoning and source citations become first-class, persisted, machine-gradable objects rather than debug text.

Source video ↗

Agent execution and grading are separated containers with proxy logging.

Before

One process runs the agent and then scores it, often in the same environment with the same credentials.

After

Two trust domains: a sandboxed agent with mediated egress and no access to grader code or held-out labels, and a grading plane that consumes only logged artifacts.

Consequence

Evaluation becomes an audit pipeline with telemetry; prompt-hacking surfaces shrink, and replay/debugging is possible from the log rather than re-running the agent.

Source video ↗

Multi-turn sweeps cost tens of thousands of dollars and decay quickly.

Before

Build one large static benchmark, run it occasionally, compare to public leaderboards.

After

Small tiered domain suites run frequently with caching and partial sweeps; large public suites used only for periodic calibration.

Consequence

Eval budget and staleness become explicit design constraints, favoring high-signal tasks and cheap harness reuse over task-count maximization.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Harness re-implementation vs. score portability

Benefit

Independent re-implementation exposes integrity bugs that published scores hide, and gives teams their own verifiable numbers.

Cost or risk

Up to 20% score variance from harness differences alone means each re-implementation is a fork that may no longer be comparable to the original paper's result.

Source video ↗

Rubric-based partial credit vs. decisive outcome

Benefit

Rewards correct intermediate reasoning and lets conflicting-data tasks be scored without a single golden answer.

Cost or risk

Partial credit on preliminary steps can mask a wrong terminal investment decision; a model may accrue rubric points while the deliverable is unusable.

Source video ↗

Realistic workflows vs. evaluation cost

Benefit

Multi-turn, tool-using, filing-grounded tasks reflect production behavior instead of synthetic puzzles.

Cost or risk

Sweeps across 16 frontier models cost tens of thousands of dollars and the benchmark goes stale quickly, so coverage must be traded against freshness and frequency.

Source video ↗

Open agent execution vs. grader integrity

Benefit

Sandboxed execution with broad tool access is what makes the task realistic and lets the agent freely investigate.

Cost or risk

That same capability lets a capable agent read or tamper with grading logic; the harness must assume adversarial behavior and split trust domains.

Source video ↗

Publishing benchmarks for influence vs. benchmark validity

Benefit

External publication directs frontier labs' optimization effort toward your product's workflow.

Cost or risk

A public benchmark turns into a target; it must survive contamination and overfitting while still driving lab investment.
