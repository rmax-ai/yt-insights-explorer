Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Felix Rieseberg
  • Channel: Silicon Valley Girl
  • Main topic: AI engineering, autonomous AI agents, and shifting from manual coding and prompting to high-level workflow orchestration with tools like Claude Rieseberg.
  • Purpose: To educate creators, developers, and knowledge workers on how to effectively use AI agentic tools by shifting mindset from micromanagement and prompt tinkering to high-level workflow orchestration, validation, and setting domain-specific rules. In this interview, Marina Mogilko hosts Felix Rieseberg, engineering lead for Claude at Anthropic, to discuss how software engineering, knowledge work, and productivity have radically transformed with the advent of advanced AI agentic systems like Claude Cowork and Claude Projects. Rieseberg explains that he no longer writes code directly, but instead acts as a conveyer belt or manager coordinating autonomous AI agents. The conversation dives deep into practical frameworks for prompt engineering, the limitations of current AI, the shift from automation to augmentation, and how teams can leverage AI agents for complex, multi-step workflows without needing extensive technical setups.

Topic Map

The Death of Traditional Coding

  • Explanation: Felix Rieseberg shares that as an engineering lead for Claude at Anthropic, he no longer writes code himself. Instead, his role has evolved into managing autonomous AI agents that handle file execution, crash data analysis, and self-debugging.
  • Key claims:
    • Engineering leads and developers are shifting from writing code to orchestrating AI agents.
    • AI agents like Claude Cowork can build entire features and debug themselves in short timeframes.
    • Humans act more as verifiers and workflow directors rather than manual typists of code.
  • Examples:
    • Building Claude Cowork in about a week and a half without Rieseberg writing individual lines of code.
    • Feeding crash data directly into Claude so it can analyze and fix errors independently.

Automation vs. Augmentation and the Moat of Empathy

Key Points

Stop Treating AI as a Simple Chatbot

  • Explanation: Users who only ask AI to summarize emails or draft simple texts are missing the true power of new intelligence models.
  • Evidence: Rieseberg notes that treating AI like a conveyer belt for entire engineering workflows yields 10x results compared to simple single-prompt tasks.
  • Practical implication: Upgrade your prompting from single questions to multi-threaded agentic instructions with persistent context.

The Importance of Adversarial Reviews

  • Explanation: To ensure quality, use separate AI agent sessions to critique and attack the output generated by the primary model.
  • Evidence: Adversarial reviews mimic red-teaming in cybersecurity, effectively catching flaws before human review.
  • Practical implication: Implement a 'judge' agent in your workflow to evaluate the outputs of your primary worker agent.

Empathy Over Technical Memorization

  • Explanation: As coding and execution costs approach zero, understanding user needs and business context becomes the primary driver of success.
  • Evidence: Top performers at companies like Anthropic focus heavily on human emotion, workflow empathy, and outcome definition rather than syntax memorization.
  • Practical implication: Invest time in understanding customer pain points and product-market fit rather than just technical implementation details.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

The Three-Step Feedback-to-Rules Framework

  • How it works: A systematic method for transforming ad-hoc AI corrections into permanent agent behaviors.
  • Components:
      1. Collect your feedback across multiple sessions.
      1. Turn feedback into structured rules and principles.
      1. Find hidden rules and integrate them into ongoing prompts or skills.
  • When to use: When working with AI agents on repetitive writing, coding, or knowledge work tasks to prevent recurring mistakes.

Model Experience (MX) over User Experience (UX)

  • How it works: Designing workflows by putting yourself in the shoes of the AI model to see what context, data, and tools it needs to succeed.
  • Components:
    • Pretend you are the model executing the task.
    • Identify what missing raw data, raw files, or constraints would block you.
    • Provide comprehensive primary sources rather than fragmented slices of data.
  • When to use: When setting up autonomous agent workspaces like Claude Projects or custom automated pipelines.

[M]Mechanismmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Corrections given to an agent should be compiled into a persistent, reusable rule/skill layer rather than repeated as per-session prompt edits: collect feedback across multiple sessions, convert it into structured rules and principles, then mine for hidden rules and fold them into ongoing prompts or skills.

Why it matters

It relocates correction handling from ephemeral chat context into a durable artifact that every future run inherits, which is the difference between a workflow that improves monotonically and one that regresses to the same mistakes each session.

Generalization

Any agent system whose instructions are hand-tuned per conversation needs an explicit promotion path: observation -> rule -> skill artifact, versioned and reused across sessions and agents.

Deep dives

Rule promotion pipeline: from episodic correction to versioned skill artifact

Research question

Does compiling per-session corrections into a persistent, versioned rule/skill layer measurably reduce repeat-mistake frequency without introducing rule bloat, conflicts, or stale-rule regressions?

Why

The claim that corrections should become durable rules is the load-bearing mechanism of the whole talk, yet it is asserted with no storage format, ownership model, conflict resolution, or retirement policy. Without that, 'skills' become an unversioned prompt junk drawer and the improvement loop silently inverts.

Source video ↗

Review topology: does context separation or adversarial framing drive defect detection?

Research question

Is a fresh-context adversarial critic materially better at catching defects than an in-context self-critique given the same adversarial instructions, and what is the cost per caught defect for each topology?

Why

The talk asserts self-judging is unreliable but never isolates the cause. If adversarial framing alone does the work, a second agent role is wasted latency and token spend; if context separation is the cause, then generator/critic separation is a hard architectural boundary that must be enforced in the orchestration layer.

Observability as an agent input surface: schema requirements for autonomous repair

Research question

What trace/crash schema properties (structure, locality, reproduction context, blast radius) are required for an agent to produce an accepted fix rather than a plausible-looking patch, and what is the introduced-regression rate?

Why

Turning telemetry into a prompt changes what the logs must contain. If traces are not machine-actionable, the repair loop degrades into hallucinated fixes; if they are, the human verifier becomes the throughput ceiling and the safety gate for autonomous edits.

Falsifying the conveyor-belt multiplier

Research question

Why

A ~10x claim with no baseline, task set, or measurement method is the kind of number that gets copied into decks and budgets. It needs a defined baseline and a decomposition boundary, or orchestration investment is unjustified.

Article ideas

3

Your Agent Is the Worst Judge of Its Own Work

Self-critique inside a single context is structurally compromised, not merely under-prompted: quality control requires a distinct critic role with its own context and instructions, and teams that skip this are shipping unreviewed generation while believing they have a review step.

Angle

Treat the worker/judge boundary as an architectural decision with a measurable price (latency, tokens, disagreement reconciliation) rather than a prompting trick, and show what that boundary buys in defect detection.

Source video ↗

Prompt Editing Is Not Learning

An agent that is corrected in chat has not learned anything; until corrections are promoted into a persistent, versioned rule artifact, every session regresses to the same baseline. The promotion path — observation to rule to reusable skill — is the actual unit of engineering progress, and it comes with its own maintenance burden of deduplication, conflict resolution, and retirement.

Angle

Reframe correction handling as a data pipeline with storage, versioning, and stale-rule risk instead of a conversational habit, and name the new failure surface that rule accumulation creates.

Source video ↗

Verification Is the Bottleneck Nobody Budgeted For

When generation cost approaches zero, throughput stops being limited by how fast agents produce artifacts and starts being limited by human verification capacity — so the ~10x orchestration claim is capped by reviewer bandwidth unless verification itself is engineered to be cheap and high-leverage.

Angle

Invert the productivity narrative: measure not output velocity but accepted-output velocity, and argue that specification quality and review ergonomics are the real levers.

Source video ↗

Project ideas

4

RuleLedger

beyond-evals

Promoting corrections into a versioned rule artifact reduces the repeat-mistake rate on previously corrected behaviors by a measurable margin over per-session prompt editing, and the gain does not decay as the rule set grows past a conflict threshold.

Proof of concept

A/B harness that runs the same agent task suite twice: once with baseline prompts, once with accumulated promoted rules. Each human correction is captured, converted into a structured rule, and written to a versioned store with provenance. Instrument rule conflicts and stale-rule hits.

Measurement

Regression rate on previously corrected behaviors, rule conflict count per 100 rules, and task-success delta between baseline and rule-augmented runs across repeated sessions.

Source video ↗

CriticTopologyBench

beyond-evals

A fresh-context critic with shared output artifact catches more real defects per dollar than in-context self-critique with identical adversarial instructions; if the two perform comparably, context separation is not the causal factor.

Proof of concept

Run the same generated outputs through four review topologies — in-context self-critique, in-context adversarial framing, fresh-context critic, multi-critic ensemble — against a seeded defect set with known ground truth. Log verdicts and disagreements.

Measurement

Defect-detection rate (precision/recall against seeded defects), cost per caught defect in tokens and wall-clock, and false-positive rate per topology.

Source video ↗

Telemetry Repair Loop

gatehouse

Structured crash reports are directly sufficient as agent task input for autonomously accepted fixes at a non-trivial rate, but fix-acceptance collapses when reproduction context is missing from the trace schema.

Proof of concept

Build a repair loop fed a backlog of real crash reports, with the agent proposing patches and a human verifier gating acceptance. Ablate trace fields (stack, repro steps, environment, frequency) to find the minimum actionable schema.

Measurement

Fix-acceptance rate, introduced-regression rate per accepted fix, reviewer minutes per accepted fix, and acceptance delta across trace-schema ablations.

Source video ↗

Conveyor Bench

movement-lab

Conveyor-belt multi-agent orchestration outperforms single-agent multi-turn and single-prompt baselines on accepted-output velocity, but only above a task-decomposition complexity threshold, below which coordination overhead makes it strictly worse.

Proof of concept

Build identical deliverables three ways (single prompt, single-agent multi-turn, multi-agent orchestration) across a graded set of task complexities, then measure wall-clock, token cost, rework, and human acceptance.

Measurement

Accepted-output velocity (accepted deliverables per hour), total token cost per accepted deliverable, rework cycles, and the complexity breakpoint where orchestration stops winning.

Architectural implications

4

Corrections currently live as one-off prompt adjustments inside ephemeral sessions; the speaker describes promoting them into persistent rules and skills.

Before

Human edits the prompt per session; each new session starts from the same baseline and repeats the same mistakes.

After

A rule/skill artifact accumulates where correction exists, owned and reused across agents and sessions.

Consequence

Introduces a new layer that must itself be maintained: rule storage, deduplication, and versioning become engineering artifacts subject to review, and stale or conflicting rules become a new failure surface.

Quality control moves from same-session self-critique to a separate judge/reviewer agent that attacks the worker's output.

Before

One agent produces and evaluates its own work inside a single context.

After

Generator and adversarial critic occupy distinct contexts, with the critic's verdict gating output before human review.

Consequence

Adds latency and token cost per task and requires orchestration for routing outputs to the critic and reconciling disagreements, but converts evaluation into an independently specified role rather than an implicit one.

Crash data is fed directly into the agent, which analyzes and fixes errors independently.

Before

Telemetry is consumed by humans who triage and then write fixes.

After

Telemetry is an agent input; the repair loop runs with the human as verifier of the resulting change.

Consequence

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Self-evaluation vs. adversarial review

Benefit

Adversarial review by a separate session catches flaws before human review, mimicking security red-teaming.

Cost or risk

Requires a second agent role, extra runs, and reconciliation of critic vs. worker disagreement; without it, a session grading its own work is 'risky and prone to errors'.

Source video ↗

Autonomous code generation by agents

Benefit

Large features can be produced in short timeframes with the human no longer writing lines of code.

Cost or risk

The human is repositioned as verifier, so verification capacity — not generation — becomes the limiting factor and the point where defects pass through.

Source video ↗

Automation vs. augmentation

Benefit

Automation yields economic value by removing friction from tedious work.

Cost or risk

Friction removal does not by itself enhance human capability; treating the two as the same leaves the augmentation benefit unrealized.

Source video ↗

Investing in technical skill depth vs. adaptability

Benefit

Adaptability and user/business understanding pay off as the tooling churns.

Cost or risk

Skill half-lives are short, so specific technical knowledge depreciates quickly; over-investing in memorized mechanics is a depreciating asset.
