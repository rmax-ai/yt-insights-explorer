Overview

  • Speaker: Evan Kormos
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Trusting agents in policy-driven workflows
  • Purpose: Provide a practical framework for enterprise software engineers and managers on how to build trust, establish governance, and evaluate quality when deploying probabilistic AI agents in policy-driven business workflows. Evan Kormos from Coinbase discusses how to safely integrate AI agents into enterprise workflows by establishing clear policies, separating build-state from run-state, structuring workflows as directed acyclic and cyclic graphs, and implementing hill-climbing quality evaluation loops to transition from fully supervised to autonomous operations.

Topic Map

  • Key claims:

    • Language such as 'must', 'may', and 'should' defines requirement levels.
    • Build-state and run-state must be separated as two different moments in time.
  • Examples:
    • Defining rules for commercial lending or token listing.
  • Terminology:
    • policy

  • Why it matters: Clear policies provide the necessary boundaries before agents are introduced into organizational workflows.

Agents Meet Process

  • Explanation: Agents operate across the enterprise layers of people, process, and technology by working alongside people, executing parts of the process, and using technology.
  • Key claims:
    • Agents cut across people, process, and technology layers.

Quality and Evaluation

  • Explanation: Unlike deterministic software tested with unit tests, probabilistic agents require representative datasets and statistical evals across traces to build confidence.
  • Key claims:
    • Probabilistic agents require new evaluation techniques beyond exact output comparison.
    • Datasets and evals together build confidence in agent outputs.
    • Quality is a team sport involving people, process, and technology.
  • Examples:
    • Code review refinement loops where drafts are checked and refined iteratively.
  • Terminology:
    • evals

    • traces
    • confidence mechanism
  • Why it matters: Evaluating probabilistic outputs requires statistical volume and repeatable test runs rather than single test cases.

Turning It On (Hill-Climbing)

  • Explanation: Deploying agents involves starting fully supervised on every output, sampling as confidence climbs, and eventually handling only exceptions through human oversight.
  • Key claims:
    • Start supervised with every output reviewed by humans.
    • Sample outputs as confidence scores cross policy thresholds.
    • Transition to humans handling exceptions only once high confidence is established.
  • Examples:
    • Moving from 0.70 confidence threshold checking every output to 0.90 threshold with sampling and exception handling.
  • Terminology:
    • hill climbing
    • confidence threshold
    • sampling
    • exception handling
  • Why it matters: A gradual ramp-up ensures safety and trust before letting agents run unsupervised.

Key Points

Separate policy from the running system

  • Explanation: Policies are authored and updated in build-state, while systems execute continuously in run-state.
  • Evidence: Distinction between build-state configuration and run-state execution.
  • Practical implication: Allows rapid product updates without breaking core organizational compliance.

Workflows as Directed Graphs

  • Explanation: Workflows should be modeled as DAGs or DCGs to manage dependencies and enable parallel reviews.
  • Evidence: Mention of Apache Airflow and LangGraph for graph state management.
  • Practical implication: Enables parallel reviews and clear operational visibility into bottlenecks.

Confidence through Evals and Datasets

  • Explanation: Traditional unit tests are insufficient for probabilistic AI; instead, use representative datasets and repeated statistical evals.
  • Evidence: Comparison of traditional unit tests with agent eval datasets and runtime traces.
  • Practical implication: Provides statistical confidence in agent outputs rather than binary pass/fail results.

Hill-Climbing Adoption Strategy

  • Explanation: Begin with 100% human supervision and gradually decrease human intervention as agent confidence metrics cross thresholds.
  • Evidence: Graph showing confidence rising from 0.70 (every output) to sampled, and finally 0.90 (exceptions only).
  • Practical implication: Safely reduces operational overhead over time while maintaining high quality.

Core insights

6
[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Split the system into two temporally distinct states: a build-state where policy (institutional intent, the 'system prompt') is authored and revised, and a run-state where execution happens continuously. Policy is a versioned artifact owned outside the runtime, not code embedded in the agent loop.

Why it matters

This is the boundary that lets compliance/rules change at product velocity without redeploying or re-validating the execution engine, and it gives a natural diff/audit surface for governance review. It also implies the runtime must read policy as configuration/data at invocation time rather than compile it in.

Generalization

Any agent operating under organizational rules needs a policy layer whose lifecycle is decoupled from the execution layer; otherwise every rule change becomes a code change and every code change becomes a compliance review.

[P]Practicehigh noveltymoderate evidence

Autonomy is granted as a graded, measurable ladder rather than a binary decision: run 100% human review on every output, then move to statistical sampling once the confidence score crosses a policy-defined threshold, then move to exception-only human handling. The confidence metric is an operational control input that directly sets the human review fraction — it is not just a dashboard number.

Why it matters

Engineers typically treat the supervised/unsupervised choice as a launch-day switch or as per-task trust. Making the review fraction a function of a measured confidence score converts a judgment call into a tunable controller with an explicit, auditable ramp, and it bounds the blast radius of early errors.

Generalization

Treat human oversight as a dialed resource allocated proportionally to measured uncertainty, not as a phase you exit; the same pattern applies to auto-retry rates, cache trust, and canary traffic.

[M]Mechanismmedium noveltymoderate evidence

For probabilistic components, unit tests are replaced as the confidence mechanism by representative datasets plus repeated statistical evals over runtime traces — confidence is established through volume and repeatability, not exact-output assertions.

Why it matters

This changes what a 'passing build' means: a single green run carries almost no information about a stochastic agent, so the harness must be built to run N repetitions of a case set and report a distribution, and traces must be captured as first-class eval inputs from production rather than discarded after debugging.

Generalization

Any component whose output distribution is non-degenerate needs a statistical acceptance criterion, and the eval suite must be sized to produce a usable confidence interval rather than a binary verdict.

Source video ↗
[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Agent workflows cannot always be modeled as DAGs: refinement loops (draft → check → refine) require directed cyclic graphs, and the graph representation is what makes parallelism, dependency order, and exception paths explicit.

Why it matters

Choosing DAG-only orchestration forces you to fake iteration with re-submission or unrolled retries, which hides the loop from observability and inflates cost/latency tracking. Explicit cycles make the refine loop a named, bounded, inspectable step with its own termination condition.

Generalization

When the unit of work is an agent that must self-correct, the orchestration substrate needs loop semantics (with budgets and exit conditions), which is a different primitive than task-graph execution.

Source video ↗
[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltyweak evidence

'Deterministic outside, adaptive inside': keep the surrounding harness — policy enforcement, graph control flow, thresholds, review routing, exception handling — deterministic and testable with ordinary software engineering, and confine the probabilistic behavior to the inner decision steps.

Why it matters

It tells you where to put the boundary so that failures are attributable: if control flow is deterministic, an eval regression can be blamed on the model step rather than on the orchestrator, and the orchestrator can be unit-tested normally. It also means the safety properties (what must never happen) live in code you can reason about, not in the model.

Generalization

Reliability in stochastic systems comes from pushing nondeterminism into the smallest possible region with the tightest typed interface.

Deep dives

5

Calibrating and monitoring a confidence metric that controls the human review fraction

Research question

If the confidence score directly sets the review fraction (100% → sampling → exception-only), what calibration procedure and drift detector keep the earned autonomy level honest across model versions, prompt changes, and shifting input distributions — and what triggers automatic de-escalation back down the ladder?

Why

The entire hill-climbing scheme is only as safe as the scorer's calibration. A silently drifting scorer promotes the agent past a threshold it has not earned, and once oversight is exception-only there is almost no inspection surface left to notice. This converts the scorer from a reporting artifact into the most safety-critical component in the system, and it is currently specified with illustrative numbers (0.70, 0.90) rather than a derivation.

Source video ↗

Compiling deontic modality ('must'/'may'/'should') into tiered runtime enforcement

Research question

Can graded obligation language in a policy artifact be deterministically compiled into enforcement tiers — hard block, permitted-with-review, advisory — such that one policy source generates both the agent's instructions and its guardrail configuration, and does that coupling eliminate drift between the written rule and the runtime check?

Why

If it works, policy authorship becomes the single source of truth for enforcement severity and gives a principled default for routing decisions (reject vs. human review vs. log-only) instead of ad-hoc thresholds. If it does not work, we learn where natural-language obligation is too ambiguous to compile and needs human-in-the-loop authoring — both outcomes are actionable. This is explicitly flagged in pass 1 as inference, not a stated claim, so it is a genuine research gap.

Sizing the statistical eval harness: repetitions, confidence intervals, and cost

Research question

How many repeated runs per test case are required before an eval verdict on a probabilistic agent is stable enough to gate an autonomy step, and how does that repetition count scale eval cost and wall-clock time against the review labor it replaces?

Why

A single green run carries almost no information about a stochastic component, so 'passing build' has to be redefined as a distributional claim. Without a stated repetition count and an acceptance criterion expressed as a confidence interval, teams will either under-run evals and ship noise or over-run them and price the harness out of CI. This is directly tied to a key claim flagged as needing verification.

Loop primitives for agent orchestration: budgets, exit conditions, and per-node attribution

Research question

What is the minimal cyclic-graph primitive (named loop node, iteration budget, explicit termination condition, per-iteration trace span) that supports draft-check-refine cycles without unbounded cost or latency, and does it preserve per-node eval attribution when the same node executes repeatedly?

Why

DAG-only substrates force iteration to be faked via re-submission or unrolled retries, which hides the loop from observability and inflates cost tracking. But adding cycles introduces non-termination risk and makes it hard to attribute an eval regression to a specific step. The tradeoff needs a concrete primitive, not just the observation that cycles exist.

Policy compliance as a property of the (policy, context) pair

Research question

Holding the policy artifact fixed, how much does adherence to each individual policy clause change when the composition of the agent's context window (retrieved documents, tool outputs, conversation history length) is ablated, and is that degradation attributable to specific clause types?

Why

The build-state/run-state split stabilizes the policy text but not the context it competes with for model attention. If adherence is context-sensitive, then a policy artifact that passed review at build-state can silently violate itself at run-state with no diff to review — a governance failure mode that no amount of policy versioning catches.

Article ideas

3

Your Confidence Score Is a Control Plane, Not a Dashboard Number

Once a confidence threshold decides how many outputs a human actually inspects, the scorer stops being reporting infrastructure and becomes the component that sets operational risk. Teams must treat it accordingly: calibration harnesses, drift detection, per-node reliability diagrams, and an automated path back down the autonomy ladder — because the failure mode of a miscalibrated scorer is invisible by construction once oversight is exception-only.

Angle

Take the audience's instinct that 'adding a confidence score' is a reporting feature and invert it: show the engineering obligations (calibration, monitoring, rollback) that follow the moment the score gates review routing.

Source video ↗

Policy Is Not a Prompt: Why Rule Changes Shouldn't Be Deploys

Embedding institutional rules inside the agent loop makes every compliance change a code change and every code change a compliance review, collapsing two different clocks into one. Separating an authored, versioned policy artifact from a stable runtime lets rules ship at product speed, makes diffs the audit surface, and — critically — means the execution engine stays constant while the constraints move.

Angle

Reframe the build-state/run-state split as a release-engineering argument rather than a governance argument, with the reviewer's diff as the deliverable.

Source video ↗

Your DAG Is Lying About Your Refine Loop

Draft-check-refine is a cycle, and forcing it into a directed acyclic graph doesn't remove the loop — it just hides the iteration in re-submitted pipeline runs, unrolled retries, or a single opaque agent call. The cost shows up as invisible latency, unattributable eval regressions, and no place to attach a termination budget. Model the cycle explicitly and give it a name, a budget, and an exit condition.

Angle

Concrete comparison: what an unrolled DAG retry looks like in traces versus a named cyclic node, and which one you can put an eval gate on.

Project ideas

3

Autonomy Governor

gatehouse

A per-node calibrated confidence gate with drift detection keeps error escape rate within a fixed tolerance while cutting human review volume substantially versus fixed-rate sampling — and detects scorer drift before error escape rate rises, enabling automatic de-escalation to a higher review fraction.

Proof of concept

Wrap a policy-driven workflow (e.g. a document/decision triage step) in a controller that takes a confidence score and outputs a review fraction. Instrument with a labeled holdout set to measure escape rate at each oversight level, plus a drift monitor on the score distribution. Include a de-escalation path that widens sampling when drift is detected.

Measurement

Error escape rate as a function of review fraction across thresholds; detection latency between induced drift and the de-escalation trigger; review hours saved at matched escape rate versus a fixed 20% sampling baseline.

Source video ↗

Modality-to-Guardrail Compiler

gatehouse

Deontic markers in policy text can be compiled into enforcement tiers (hard block / permitted-with-review / advisory) that agree with human compliance reviewers' own tier assignments at a high rate, and the compiled guardrails eliminate the drift that accumulates when the same rule is hand-written into both a prompt and a runtime check.

Proof of concept

Parse a corpus of real policy clauses, classify modality and map to enforcement tier, emit both an instruction fragment for the agent and a runtime guardrail config from the same source. Diff against tiers assigned independently by human reviewers; run a drift audit comparing prompt text and guardrail config in a hand-maintained system.

Measurement

Classification agreement (and confusion matrix) versus human-assigned tiers; count and severity of prompt-vs-guardrail mismatches before and after compiling from a single source.

Source video ↗

Repetition-Count Harness for Stochastic Evals

beyond-evals

There is a stable, measurable repetition count per test case beyond which eval verdicts stop flipping across reruns, and that count — not a hand-picked N — should set the CI gate; below it, verdict instability dominates the signal and 'passing' is noise.

Proof of concept

Build a harness that runs a fixed case set N times per case and reports per-case pass-rate distributions, verdict-flip rate as N increases, and cost/latency per N. Sweep N across a range and identify the knee where verdict stability converges.

Measurement

Verdict flip rate and CI width versus N; compute cost per eval run at the chosen N; false-promote rate for autonomy gates when N is set below the knee versus at it.

Before

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Sampling-based human oversight

Benefit

Reduces human review load once confidence is high, allowing throughput to scale without headcount.

Cost or risk

Sampling means most outputs are never inspected, so any error class the confidence metric does not capture can persist undetected and only surfaces through downstream damage; the guarantee degrades from 'verified' to 'statistically spot-checked'.

Source video ↗

Threshold-gated autonomy (hill climbing)

Benefit

Gradual ramp-up means autonomy is only extended after evidence accumulates, containing early failure blast radius.

Cost or risk

The whole scheme rests on the confidence metric being calibrated; a drifting or miscalibrated scorer silently promotes the agent past a threshold it hasn't actually earned, and there is no stated mechanism for detecting that drift or re-lowering autonomy.

Source video ↗

Cyclic (DCG) workflows

Benefit

Models draft-check-refine loops and exception paths explicitly instead of hiding them.

Cost or risk

Cycles introduce non-termination and unbounded cost/latency risk; each loop needs an explicit budget and exit condition, and per-step eval attribution gets harder when the same node executes repeatedly.

Source video ↗

Policy compliance under shifting agent context

Benefit

Separating policy from run-state keeps rules stable while prompts/context evolve.

Cost or risk

As the context window composition changes, the agent's effective adherence to policy can change even when the policy text has not — compliance is a property of the (policy, context) pair, not of the policy alone.

Source video ↗

Higher-quality inputs

Benefit

Speaker claims better outputs at lower compute cost.

Cost or risk

Input curation/upstream verification has its own labor and latency cost, and the claimed compute savings are unquantified — the crossover point where curation pays for itself is unknown.
