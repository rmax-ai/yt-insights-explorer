Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Hrishi Olickel
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Data infrastructure transformation enabled by cheap AI intelligence
  • Purpose: To introduce the concept of just-in-time ontologies, explain why single ontologies fail, and demonstrate how agentic pipelines and Pareto-surfing can achieve high-accuracy data integration at a fraction of traditional costs. The video explores how the declining cost of AI intelligence enables a shift from rigid upfront enterprise ontologies and data warehousing to just-in-time ontologies. It argues that data infrastructure has traditionally been bottlenecked by the high cost of transformation and universal schemas, which invariably fail. By leveraging cheaper, specialized LLMs, agents, and bounded decision models, organizations can integrate data at the point of decision rather than forcing a universal superschema.

Key Points

Transformation has always been expensive

  • Explanation: Enterprise data architecture is built on the assumption that transforming data schemas is costly, leading to rigid upfront data warehousing and master data management.
  • Evidence: Historical pricing charts for storage, compute, and IP transit vs. the emerging cost curve of intelligence.
  • Practical implication: If transformation is cheap, data pipelines can be fluid and constructed on demand.

There is no universal schema

  • Explanation: Enterprise attempts at building single universal ontologies (superschemas) fail because a customer entity definition depends entirely on the specific decision being made.
  • Evidence: High failure and abandonment rates of major government and enterprise IT migrations like NHS and US Air Force ECSS.
  • Practical implication: Stop trying to build enterprise-wide data models upfront; instead, merge source and decision schemas just-in-time.

Checking is more expensive than doing

  • Explanation: In agentic data pipelines, verifying and auditing model outputs costs significantly more in tokens and compute than generating the initial answer.
  • Evidence: Cost breakdowns of the Ohio campaign finance pipeline showing audit and settling disagreements consumed the majority of the budget.
  • Practical implication: Design pipelines with multi-tier model review where cheap models propose and capable models check.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Stop spending months designing rigid upfront database schemas.
    • Use bounded pilots to test multiple model architectures against a small data slice.
    • Implement checking ladders in code rather than relying entirely on chat prompts.
  • Strategic:
    • Embrace just-in-time ontologies where data is transformed at the moment of decision.
    • Treat intelligence as a cheap, abundant resource rather than a scarce bottleneck.
    • Build repeatable pipelines (hanks and codons) that mature in cost and performance automatically.
  • Questions to investigate:

  • Data architecture has historically been constrained by expensive compute and transformation.
  • Universal superschemas and single enterprise ontologies invariably fail because data and decisions are decoupled.
  • Just-in-time ontologies merge source and decision schemas dynamically at query time.
  • Checking and auditing model outputs dominate pipeline costs and require rigorous programmatic guards.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Four distinct schemas must align for correct data interpretation — source schema, storage schema, decision schema, and the model's own prior — and they are only simultaneously knowable at the moment of decision, meaning ontology merging should be deferred just-in-time rather than fixed upfront.

Why it matters

Reframes schema design from a static, upfront modeling exercise into a runtime concern tied to a specific decision context, which changes where ontology logic lives in a pipeline (at inference time, not in the warehouse DDL).

Generalization

Any system that maps heterogeneous data into a decision-ready representation should treat the mapping as decision-scoped and computed at query/decision time rather than as a global invariant.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Treating the model's prior as an explicit fourth schema means model choice participates in the semantic layer, and correlated failures arise because different LLM families share sign/spelling conventions — so intersecting priors across models is an error-reduction mechanism, not just redundancy.

Why it matters

Gives a concrete reliability lever: cross-model intersection reduces correlated prior-driven errors, and warns that 'bigger model' can be worse (memorization traps) rather than uniformly better.

Generalization

Where multiple models share training-data conventions, their errors are correlated; ensembles only help to the extent priors are actually independent, so diversity must be measured, not assumed.

Why it matters

Inverts the usual cost intuition (generation as the expensive step) and implies the checking ladder is the primary cost and reliability surface to optimize.

Generalization

As generation becomes cheap, the bottleneck shifts to validation; system architecture should be organized around verification cost and escalation policy rather than generation throughput.

[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Operationally enables teams to hold accuracy constant while sliding along the cost/latency frontier, which is essential for high-volume pipelines like entity resolution over hundreds of thousands of records.

Any AI pipeline with heterogeneous per-item difficulty should allocate model capacity per item via a frontier search rather than a single global model choice.

Schema/ontology alignment is deferred to decision time rather than fixed in the warehouse layer.

Before

Upfront master data management and universal enterprise ontologies encode entity semantics once, globally.

After

Ontology merging happens just-in-time per decision, combining source and decision schemas at runtime.

Consequence

Ontology logic becomes a runtime component (agent/pipeline code) that must be versioned, tested, and audited like application code, not a DDL artifact.

After

Consequence

Cost modeling, latency SLOs, and observability must instrument the checking path as the primary expense and failure surface.

Before

After

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Cross-model verification vs correlated priors

Benefit

Intersecting model priors dramatically reduces errors by catching prior-driven mistakes.

Cost or risk

Different LLM families can share sign and spelling conventions, producing correlated failures that defeat naive ensembling.

Bigger models vs memorization traps

Benefit

Larger models offer more capability and may resolve harder items.

Cost or risk

Bigger models are not always better because of memorization traps, so scaling capacity can degrade specific tasks.

Source video ↗

Cheap generation vs expensive verification

Benefit

Cheap intelligence makes on-demand transformation and just-in-time ontology merging economically viable.

Cost or risk

Auditing and settling disagreements consumes the majority of the pipeline budget, so savings on generation may be eaten by verification.

Just-in-time ontologies vs upfront enterprise models

Benefit

Avoids the failure mode of universal superschemas by merging source and decision schemas only when needed.

Cost or risk
