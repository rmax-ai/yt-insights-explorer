Overview

  • Speaker: Stephanie Jarmak
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Evaluating, testing, and building trust in AI coding and business agents
  • Purpose: To provide AI engineers and teams with a rigorous, scientific framework for evaluating coding and business agents, understanding failure modes in tool use, and designing evaluation experiments around real-world business needs. Stephanie Jarmak discusses how to evaluate, test, and build trust in AI agents when stakes are high. Drawing on her background as a researcher and AI engineer at Omni Analytics, she frames the evaluation of agents not as a simple matter of trusting metrics, but as a scientific process of questioning how and why agents use tools, how harnesses affect outcomes, and how to balance automation with human review loops.

Key Points

Do not trust proxy metrics blindly

  • Explanation: Lower costs or higher benchmark scores can mask underlying failures where agents fail to invoke required tools.
  • Evidence: A 28% reduction in observed cost on code search tasks while the agent missed relevant evidence and trusted empty results.
  • Practical implication: Always verify that tool utilization and operational mechanics match expectations alongside cost and performance metrics.

Harness selection dictates model ranking

  • Explanation: Changing the evaluation harness can invert model performance superiority.
  • Evidence: Terminal-Bench-4 results showing Opus 5 winning on OpenHands while Astra won on the PI harness.
  • Practical implication: Test models inside your exact production harness rather than relying on public leaderboard rankings.

Separate public benchmarks from internal evaluations

  • Explanation: Public leaderboards help map the landscape, but internal evaluations are required for product and process improvement.
  • Evidence: Spiky nature of model training makes public benchmarks unreliable for specific legal or enterprise use cases.
  • Practical implication: Build internal evaluation datasets that reflect your organization's unique workload families and task distribution.

Trace every step from evidence to conclusion

  • Explanation: Failures can occur at availability, invocation, return, or interpretation stages of an agentic workflow.
  • Evidence: Search-limit tests and evidence tests revealing that agents ignore available information due to harness constraints.
  • Practical implication: Maintain comprehensive trace logs and run ablation tests on evidence sources and components separately.

Enforce quality gates actively

  • Explanation: Logging or flagging errors in audit queues is insufficient if the agent proceeds without human intervention.
  • Evidence: Software factory audit trials showing approval queues finding faults but failing to enforce stopping conditions.
  • Practical implication: Decouple detection from enforcement so that safety gates have hard execution-stopping power when thresholds are breached.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Review your agent evaluation pipelines to check if agents actually use required tools during runs.
    • Audit your scoring rules and metrics (such as precision vs recall) to ensure they align with business goals.
    • Test models inside your own harness rather than trusting public leaderboard rankings.
  • Strategic:
    • Establish internal evaluation benchmarks tailored to your organization's specific task families and workloads.
    • Design experiments to capture total operating cost, reliability, and workload coverage together.
    • Enforce hard execution gates rather than relying solely on audit logs and human review queues.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • Are our evaluation harness configurations identical to our production deployment environment?
    • How do our agents behave when search tools return empty results?
    • Do our quality gates actively stop execution when faults are detected, or do they just log warnings?

Core insights

7
[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltystrong evidence

Evaluation results are dominated by the harness-model pairing, not the model alone: the same benchmark produces different winners depending on the scaffold wrapping the model.

Why it matters

It invalidates using third-party leaderboards to select a model for a custom runtime. The scaffold (tool loop, prompting, context handling, retry logic) is a first-class variable that interacts with model weights, so a model's measured capability is only meaningful conditional on a specific harness. Engineers must evaluate the exact production configuration rather than a nominally identical model.

Generalization

Any non-deterministic system whose performance is mediated by an external controller (orchestrator, runtime, tool layer) has capability that is only defined jointly with that controller; benchmark scores are properties of configurations, not of components.

[!]Failure Modehigh noveltymoderate evidence

Cost reduction is a proxy metric that can be inversely correlated with correctness: a prompt change cut observed cost 28% while the agent silently stopped invoking the search tool and accepted empty results as ground truth.

Why it matters

Optimizing on cost/latency without a tool-utilization invariant lets agents 'succeed' cheaply by skipping the expensive evidence-gathering step. The failure is silent because the metric improves and no error is raised, so it will not surface in dashboards. Production systems need an assertion that required tools were actually called, independent of the cost/score metrics.

Generalization

Any efficiency metric becomes gameable by removing work; pair every cost/latency objective with a mechanism-verification assertion (was the tool called, was evidence returned, was it cited) that the optimizer cannot satisfy by abstaining.

Source video ↗
[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Agent failure requires positional diagnosis along an evidence pipeline — evidence was unavailable, uncalled, unreturned, or misinterpreted — and each stage must be tested with a separate ablation (search-limits test vs. known-relevant-evidence test).

Why it matters

It converts a monolithic 'agent failed' signal into four distinct, separately testable fault classes with different fixes: expand retrieval limits, fix tool selection, fix the tool's return contract, or fix the agent's interpretation of results. Without the split test you cannot tell whether better evidence would even help, since better evidence can be ignored.

Generalization

Treat every retrieval-augmented or tool-using system as a staged data pipeline with per-stage instrumentation and per-stage injections, rather than scoring only the terminal output.

Source video ↗
[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Scoring function choice determines whether an eval measures business success: high recall achieved by over-fetching (grepping 295 files to find 6) can pass the metric while failing the actual task, so precision/recall/F1 and their answer keys must be audited rather than accepted.

Why it matters

Aggregate metrics like F1 hide the asymmetry between over-fetch (context bloat, cost, downstream confusion) and under-fetch (missing evidence). An eval whose scoring rule rewards recall will select for an agent whose behavior is unacceptable in production, so the metric can be aligned to the wrong objective while appearing rigorous.

Generalization

Evaluation metrics are alignment artifacts: the scoring rule selects the agent's behavior, so the rule must be derived from the business cost of each error type, not inherited from a generic benchmark.

Source video ↗
[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Reliability cannot be inferred from a relative score: repeated runs of the same task are not independent outcomes, so score variance must be measured and reported separately from mean quality, alongside performance and total operating cost.

Why it matters

It reframes evaluation reporting as a four-axis vector (quality, reliability, performance, total cost) rather than a single number. A system that is slightly worse on average but far tighter in run-to-run variance may be the correct production choice, and the reverse is undetectable from a single aggregate.

Generalization

For stochastic systems, treat distributional properties (variance, tail behavior across repeated trials) as a separately reported dimension of the result, not as noise to be averaged away.

Source video ↗
[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Public benchmarks and internal evaluations serve different functions and should not substitute for one another: public benchmarks provide a landscape shortlist but are spiky from training exposure, while internal evals must mirror actual product workflows and domain tasks.

Why it matters

It gives a defensible division of labor: public leaderboards for candidate elimination, internal evals for selection and regression. Using a leaderboard to make the final choice over-indexes on tasks whose 'spiky' exposure to training data does not reflect your domain's failure modes.

Generalization

External standardized benchmarks are filters for candidate sets; only workload-matched evaluations support deployment decisions.

Source video ↗
[A]Architecturemedium noveltyweak evidence

Safety gates and audit queues only work if they have enforcement authority; a gate that logs a warning instead of halting execution is not a gate.

Why it matters

This relocates the gate from the observability/annotation plane into the control plane of execution. Human-in-the-loop designs decay into post-hoc auditing unless the runtime structurally blocks until approval, which means approval state must be a dependency of tool invocation rather than a recorded event.

Generalization

Any approval, budget, or policy checkpoint must be a blocking precondition in the execution graph, not an emitted event; otherwise the failure mode is silent bypass under load.

Deep dives

4

Harness-model interaction and rank inversion prediction

Research question

Which harness features mediate model capability enough to invert rankings, and can a small diagnostic suite predict harness sensitivity without running the full harness × model matrix?

Why

If rankings invert across harnesses, then leaderboard scores cannot select a model for a custom runtime. Engineers need a cheap way to identify which harness features interact with which model behaviors, so they can prioritize paired evaluations of the exact production configuration instead of exhaustively benchmarking every combination.

Source video ↗

Online detection of missing-evidence acceptance

Research question

Can a runtime detect when an agent is trusting an empty tool result as evidence of absence, at the transition from tool return to definitive answer, and halt or re-prompt before task completion?

Why

The failure is silent: cost improves, no error is raised, and post-hoc trace review is too late. A runtime invariant would convert a latent evaluation finding into an enforceable execution guard, making missing evidence a blocking condition rather than an audit finding.

Source video ↗

Metric alignment against business error costs

Research question

How do precision/recall weighting and answer-key choices select agent behavior that fails business needs, and how can scoring rules be audited against explicit false-positive and false-negative costs?

Why

A recall-oriented metric can reward over-fetching that bloats context and confuses downstream reasoning, so the eval can be internally rigorous while aligned to the wrong objective. The engineering consequence is that scoring rules must be derived from business costs and re-scored under alternate weightings before they are trusted.

Source video ↗

Reliability variance as a separate reportable axis

Research question

What is the within-task versus between-task variance decomposition for repeated agent runs, and how should reliability be reported alongside quality, performance, and total cost?

Why

A single relative score hides run-to-run instability, and repeated runs are not independent tasks. Reporting variance separately lets teams choose a slightly lower-mean configuration with tighter tails, and prevents aggregate scores from overstating confidence in production behavior.

Project ideas

4

Missing Evidence Guard

gatehouse

An online invariant that flags a definitive answer following an empty return from a required tool will detect trust-empty-result failures with higher precision than post-hoc score review, and a blocking re-prompt will reduce the rate of definitive answers after empty evidence.

Proof of concept

Instrument the tool layer to tag required-tool calls, return status, and final-answer events; replay existing eval tasks with empty-result injection; compare warning-only logging against a blocking re-prompt at the tool-return-to-answer transition.

Measurement

Rate of definitive answers after empty required-tool returns, false-positive guard triggers, task accuracy, and total cost across warning-only versus blocking modes.

Source video ↗

Harness Sensitivity Probe

beyond-evals

A small set of harness-feature probes predicts pairwise model-rank inversions across harnesses better than public benchmark rank, allowing teams to shortlist production configurations without running the full harness × model matrix.

Proof of concept

Build two harness variants that differ in retry policy, context assembly order, and error surfacing; run two models on a subset of tasks; compare predicted inversions against full paired evaluations.

Measurement

Rank-inversion prediction accuracy, rank correlation with full matrix results, and evaluation cost versus exhaustive paired runs.

Source video ↗

Metric Cost Re-Scorer

beyond-evals

Re-scoring an existing retrieval evaluation under business-cost weights that penalize over-fetching will change the selected agent or configuration relative to F1, demonstrating metric-induced behavior selection.

Proof of concept

Take an eval set with retrieval traces, map false positives and false negatives to explicit business costs, and re-rank configurations under F1 versus cost-weighted scoring.

Measurement

Configuration ranking change, over-fetch ratio, and the delta between metric-selected and business-cost-selected winners.

Source video ↗

Blocking Approval Gate Reference

gatehouse

A blocking approval dependency in the tool-call path reduces execution of flagged actions to zero, while a warning-only gate allows a nonzero fraction of flagged actions to proceed.

Proof of concept

Implement two gate modes in a tool dispatcher, warning-only and blocking, and replay a set of flagged agent actions through both.

Measurement

Fraction of flagged actions that execute, added latency from human review, and throughput impact under suspension.

Architectural implications

4

Evaluation harness determines model rankings, so the harness is effectively part of the system under test.

Before

Harness treated as a disposable test scaffold; model chosen from public leaderboards and then dropped into a custom production runtime.

After

Harness/config becomes a versioned artifact pinned alongside the model, and eval runs execute inside the exact production scaffold rather than a generic runner.

Consequence

Configurations are versioned and regression-tested as units; upgrading either the model or the harness requires re-running the paired eval matrix because the ranking may invert.

Source video ↗

Agent failures decompose into availability, invocation, return, and interpretation stages.

Before

Trace review is post-hoc log reading; failures are diagnosed from the final answer and a flat log of tool calls.

After

Tool layer emits stage-tagged spans (requested, executed, returned payload, agent's stated interpretation) with separate injection points for search-limit and known-evidence tests.

Consequence

Per-stage fault attribution becomes automatable; instrumentation cost moves into the tool abstraction boundary, and 'agent failed' gets replaced by a specific stage and remediation.

Source video ↗

Cost and quality metrics can move in opposite directions from actual correctness.

Before

Optimization targets observed cost and primary eval score; tool usage assumed to follow from task success.

After

Instrumentation asserts required-tool invocation and non-empty evidence independently of cost/score, making mechanism conformance a separate pass/fail dimension.

Consequence

Cheap-but-broken configurations get rejected before deployment; cost becomes a valid target only within the set of configurations that satisfy mechanism invariants.

Source video ↗

Approval gates must block execution rather than record it.

Before

Gate implemented as warning emission into a log or audit queue reviewed later.

After

Approval becomes a blocking dependency that the tool-call path cannot bypass; audit queue holds executing work in suspension.

Consequence

Latency and throughput now include human review time by construction, which must be modeled in the capacity plan rather than discovered in production incidents.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Cost/latency optimization vs. evidence gathering

Benefit

Cheaper, faster runs and lower total operating cost per task.

Cost or risk

The agent can achieve cost reductions by abstaining from the expensive retrieval step and treating an empty result as a valid answer; the metric improves while correctness silently degrades.

Source video ↗

Recall vs. precision in retrieval-driven agents

Benefit

High recall guarantees the needed evidence is somewhere in the assembled context.

Cost or risk

Achieved by over-fetching (295 files for 6 needed), inflating context, cost, and the probability the agent latches onto irrelevant evidence; passes recall-based scoring while failing the business requirement.

Source video ↗

Public benchmark comparability vs. production fidelity

Benefit

Standardized leaderboards give a fast, externally comparable landscape view for shortlisting.

Cost or risk

Scores are spiky due to training exposure and are measured under a third-party harness, so they can invert under your own scaffold and mislead final selection.

Source video ↗

Automation with human review loops vs. enforced gates

Benefit

Audit queues preserve human oversight without blocking the pipeline.

Cost or risk

If the gate only logs warnings, faulty execution proceeds and the review becomes post-mortem; the safety property is nominal rather than enforced.

Repeated-run sampling vs. run-to-run independence

Benefit

Multiple samples appear to provide statistical evidence about system reliability.

Cost or risk

Repeated runs are not independent tasks, so treating them as samples overstates confidence; relative score alone cannot establish reliability.
