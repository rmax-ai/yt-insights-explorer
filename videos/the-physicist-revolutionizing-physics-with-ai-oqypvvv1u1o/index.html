Overview

  • Speaker: Jonathan Gorard
  • Channel: Curt Jaimungal
  • Main topic: Applying AI and formal verification to fundamental physics and mathematics
  • Purpose: To explore how AI, formal verification, and automated reasoning will transform mathematics and theoretical physics, and to introduce Lanyon.AI's approach to formalizing scientific computing. Jonathan Gorard, applied mathematician and co-founder of Lanyon.AI and the Wolfram Physics Project, discusses the impact of AI on mathematics and physics. He covers automated theorem proving, formal verification, the timeline of AI capabilities in math, the crisis of physics and math, and why physics requires 'neuro-symbolic' domain-specific languages rather than standard LLMs.

Topic Map

The Impact of AI on Mathematics and Physics

  • Explanation: Discussion on how AI is making mathematics and physics executable, moving beyond code-based progress to algorithmic and scientific proof generation.
  • Key claims:
    • AI is making scientific progress distinct from just mathematical or code-based progress.
    • Physics needs to become executable in the same way software and mathematics have become executable.
  • Examples:
    • Maryna Viazovska's 8- and 24-dimensional sphere packing proof automatically formalized using Lean.
    • OpenAI's formal proof of the Navier-Stokes blowup conjecture.
  • Terminology:
    • Formal Verification
    • Automated Theorem Proving
    • Lean
    • Neuro-symbolic AI
  • Why it matters: It changes the fundamental nature of how mathematicians and physicists work, threatening traditional workflows and requiring new tooling.

Science as Process vs. Science as Product

  • Explanation: Distinguishing between the end-products of science (the papers and theorems) and the process of discovery and curiosity.
  • Key claims:
    • Society and funding agencies reward the product of science, but scientists are motivated by the process.
    • AI threatens to automate the process, leaving humans disconnected from the intrinsic joy of discovery.
  • Examples:
    • Curie cancer, solve nuclear fusion, generate clean energy as products vs. protein folding and quantum gravity as process.
  • Terminology:
    • Science as Product
    • Science as Process
    • Hedonism
    • Cognitive Friction
  • Why it matters: If AI automates the process of thinking and discovery, the human motivation for doing science collapses.

The Limits of Traditional LLMs in Physics

  • Explanation: Why standard LLMs talking in English are inadequate for physics, and why domain-specific formal languages are needed.
  • Key claims:
    • English is a poorly suited language for efficient physical reasoning.
    • Neuro-symbolic architectures combining LLMs with domain-specific proof assistants are necessary.
  • Examples:
    • Toki Pona as an example of minimal expressive language.
    • Wightman and Haag's axioms in algebraic quantum field theory.
  • Terminology:

Key Points

Executable Mathematics and Physics

  • Explanation: Just as software became executable, and mathematics became executable via proof assistants, physics must become formally verifiable and executable.
  • Evidence: Recent automated formalizations of Fields Medal-winning results in Lean.
  • Practical implication: Physicists will need to use formal verification tools to validate their models against physical reality.

The Transmissibility of Understanding

  • Explanation: Solving a problem computationally does not automatically transmit understanding to human brains; understanding requires cognitive friction.
  • Evidence: Reading a math paper vs. reproducing the proofs yourself.
  • Practical implication: We risk relying on AI black boxes without building human intuition or verifiable conceptual frameworks.

Under-constrained Physical Theories

  • Explanation: Most fundamental physics theories are massively under-constrained compared to mathematics, making formalization much harder.
  • Evidence: Einstein's field equations vs. Wightman axioms in quantum field theory.
  • Practical implication: We need new formal languages that capture physical intuition without sacrificing mathematical rigor.

Core insights

6
[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Reliable AI-for-science systems are architected as a neuro-symbolic split: an LLM neural generator produces candidate reasoning, while a separate symbolic proof checker performs rigorous verification. The generative spark and the correctness guarantee live in different components.

Why it matters

Separating probabilistic generation from deterministic symbolic verification is the mechanism that makes AI scientific reasoning trustworthy; it places the trust boundary at the verifier rather than the model, which changes where evaluation, guardrails, and failure handling belong.

Generalization

For any high-stakes generative pipeline, pair a neural proposal step with an independent symbolic or rule-based verifier rather than trying to make the generator itself reliable.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Physics is harder to formalize than mathematics because its theories are massively under-constrained: unlike math (e.g. Wightman axioms) there is no tight axiomatic scaffold, so the same formal machinery that works for theorems does not transfer directly and new formal languages are needed.

Why it matters

It signals that 'just point a prover at the domain' fails when the domain lacks well-defined constraints, implying that modeling the constraints themselves is the hard part of the tooling.

Generalization

Automated reasoning works best over tightly constrained, well-specified formalisms; domains with loose or ambiguous specifications need an intermediate formalization layer before any solver can help.
