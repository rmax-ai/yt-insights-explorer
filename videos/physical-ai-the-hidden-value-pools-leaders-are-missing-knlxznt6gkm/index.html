Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Lucia Rahilly, Ani Kelkar, Mark Patel
  • Channel: McKinsey & Company
  • Main topic: Physical AI and its economic potential, value pools, and strategic implications for business leaders
  • Purpose: To educate business leaders on the shift towards Physical AI, identify hidden economic value pools, analyze hardware and software value chain distributions, and provide actionable strategies for adopting physical AI technologies. McKinsey Global Editorial Director Lucia Rahilly hosts partners Ani Kelkar and Mark Patel to discuss Physical AI—the transition of AI systems from digital/virtual spaces into physical operations, autonomous vehicles, manufacturing, robotics, and industrial environments. They explore macroeconomic figures showing physical work accounts for 44% of global working hours, detail the addressable market scale up to $370B by 2040, outline China's supply chain lead in EV and robotics components, and provide business leaders with a three-step framework to build, invest, and embrace physical AI.

Topic Map

Introduction to Physical AI

  • Explanation: Lucia Rahilly introduces the concept that machines are moving from following static instructions to sensing, deciding, and acting in the physical world.
  • Key claims:
    • AI is moving beyond the virtual space into autonomous vehicles, factories, warehouses, and medical systems.
    • Humanoid robots capture immense attention but are expected to account for a tiny fraction (2% by 2045) of physical AI value.
  • Examples:
    • Robots moving from pre-programmed repetitive trajectories to goal-fulfilling adaptive intelligence.
  • Terminology:
    • Physical AI
    • Humanoid robot
    • Sense-decide-act architecture
  • Why it matters: Business leaders often focus solely on humanoid robots while missing the broader ecosystem of physical AI applications.

The Scale of the Physical Economy

  • Explanation: Mark Patel outlines the massive scale of physical work in the global economy relative to digital work.
  • Key claims:
    • 44% of all paid work hours globally across sectors are tied to physical work.
    • An average person in the US spends 12 hours a week on unpaid household physical work.
    • Out of 60 million full-time worker equivalents of annual tasks, only 13% are automatable today.
  • Examples:
    • Cleaning, cooking, manufacturing, construction, transportation, and warehousing.
  • Terminology:
    • Physical economy
    • Paid work hours
    • Automataable tasks
  • Why it matters: Demonstrates the immense addressable market and scale of physical work that physical AI can impact.

Project ideas

4

Field Data Flywheel for Pick & Place

beyond-evals

For a pick & place task, reliability improves at a measurable rate as a function of real-world edge-case data volume collected in deployment, and fine-tuning with field data closes the last-mm gap faster than additional simulation data.

Proof of concept

Deploy a pick & place robot in a controlled warehouse-like environment; instrument it to log all edge cases; run periodic fine-tuning with collected data; measure task success rate vs. data volume. Compare against a simulation-only baseline that receives an equivalent amount of synthetic data.

Measurement

Task success rate (e.g., picks per hour without human intervention) as a function of number of edge-case samples and fine-tuning iterations; compare against simulation-only baseline.

Source video ↗

Cost-per-Performance Benchmark for Robotics Components

new

A standardized benchmark suite mapping component cost (e.g., actuators, harmonic drives) to performance on a set of manipulation tasks will reveal that suppliers with lower cost per unit of performance are not the cheapest per unit, and that EV-derived components dominate on this metric.

Proof of concept

Build a benchmark suite of 5 manipulation tasks (pick & place, peg insertion, etc.); measure performance (success rate, cycle time) for actuators from different suppliers; compute cost per unit performance; compare EV-derived vs. purpose-built components.

Measurement

Cost per successful task completion normalized by cycle time; ranking of suppliers.

Source video ↗

Value Pool Migration Simulator

new

Task Decomposition for Automatability

beyond-evals

Architectural implications

3

Deployment and field operations are a distinct value pool (15-25% via software/operations in field ops), not just a support function.

Before

Field deployment treated as one-time integration; training data generated offline from simulation only.

After

Runtime ingests field edge cases as first-class data, feeding continuous fine-tuning loops back into the model.

Consequence

Responsibility for data collection, telemetry, and retraining moves into the operational layer, requiring feedback plumbing between production and training pipelines.

Value concentrates in narrow task classes (pick & place, simple tool manipulation) rather than general-purpose form factors.

Before

Building toward broad general-purpose capability as the primary abstraction.

After

Scoping capability around a task platform with explicit boundaries and decomposable skills.

Consequence

System design optimizes for bounded task performance and throughput rather than open-ended generalization, changing evaluation and tooling choices.

Source video ↗

Hardware, software, and deployment each capture a comparable but non-dominant share of value (25-35%, 20-30%, 15-25%).

Before

Assuming a single layer (e.g., model or robot) captures most of the economics.

After

Allocating investment across a layered stack with explicit value-per-layer mapping.

Consequence

Teams must decide layer ownership deliberately, since no single layer monopolizes returns.

Benefit

Simulation and synthetic data accelerate initial model training at scale and low cost.

Cost or risk

They fail to cover real-world edge cases, so models remain unreliable in production until field data closes the last-mm gap.
