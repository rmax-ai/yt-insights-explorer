Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Supriya Subudhi
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: A/B Testing AI Products and Probabilistic Features
  • Purpose: To educate AI engineers and product managers on the pitfalls of A/B testing generative AI features and provide a five-question framework for scaling AI products successfully. Supriya Subudhi, Staff Product Manager at Bumble, discusses why A/B testing AI products is uniquely challenging when features are probabilistic. Drawing from Bumble's experience shipping an AI photo feedback feature, she outlines five major lessons: users saying they want a feature does not predict usage; top-line A/B test wins can hide harmful long-tail outputs; green dashboards can mask high generative AI costs; swapping models changes results; and global rollouts are hindered by fragmented AI regulations.

    • A positive result from an A/B test for an AI product is where the journey begins, not where it ends.
  • Examples:
    • Comparing two versions of an email or feature against a live baseline.
  • Terminology:

  • Why it matters: Traditional product management heuristics fail when products are probabilistic.

1. Users Asked for It, Then Ignored It

  • Explanation: Research showed 78% of Bumble users wanted AI photo feedback, but when shipped, only 10-15% actually used it.
  • Key claims:
    • General openness to AI does not predict whether people will use a specific AI feature.
    • Always measure what people do, not what they say they will.
  • Examples:
    • Bumble users expressing strong desire for profile feedback during customer interviews but failing to click or use the feature upon release.
  • Terminology:
    • Adoption rate
    • Customer research
    • User intent
  • Why it matters: Stating intent in surveys does not equal active engagement; education and user-flow integration are necessary.

2. The Average Win Hides the Tail

  • Explanation: Top-line metrics looked positive with increased profile updates, but disaggregated data showed the AI was telling already-smiling users to smile more.
  • Key claims:
    • A positive average can sit on top of individual outputs that are wrong.
    • Success evaluation must examine the long tail of data by segments, not just averages.
  • Examples:
    • AI telling users to smile in photos where they were already smiling.
    • Spotify AI DJ vs. budgeting app error scopes.
  • Terminology:
    • Long-tail data
    • Evals
    • Guardrails
  • Why it matters: A positive A/B test can mask harmful or absurd model behaviors for subset populations.

3. Green Dashboard, Red Bill

  • Explanation: Generative AI success scales cost linearly or exponentially with usage, leading to expensive bills despite positive user engagement.
  • Key claims:
    • With generative AI, success makes the bill bigger.
    • The best LLM route cost 10x more than a simpler approach plus running evals.
  • Examples:
    • Bumble choosing the best LLM for highest accuracy, resulting in massive operational costs compared to simpler recommendation models.
  • Terminology:
    • LLM cost
    • ROI
    • Evals cost
    • Token usage
  • Why it matters: High-performing genAI features can destroy unit economics if cost is not factored in early.

4. Swap the Model, Flip the Result

  • Explanation: Switching to cheaper LLMs or alternative models reduced costs but also reduced accuracy and test win rates.
  • Key claims:
    • A winning result belongs to the model, not the feature.
    • Test cheaper and non-AI versions from the start.
  • Examples:
    • Swapping out top-tier LLMs for cheaper alternatives and seeing the A/B test outcome flip from positive to neutral/negative.
  • Terminology:
    • Model swap
    • Prompt tuning
    • Cost-accuracy tradeoff
  • Why it matters: Feature success is tightly coupled with the underlying model choice rather than the product concept alone.

5. There May Not Be a Global Rollout

  • Explanation: Meta's Meta AI and Bumble's photo feedback feature faced geographic restrictions due to localized AI regulations.
  • Key claims:
    • AI regulations are constantly changing and differ across the world.
    • Bring legal and compliance in before the experiment.
  • Examples:
    • Meta AI being available only in the US and Canada; Bumble photo feedback blocked in international markets initially.
  • Terminology:
    • AI regulations
    • Compliance
    • Global rollout
    • Regional restrictions
  • Why it matters: Building a great feature is useless if legal compliance prevents deployment in key markets.

Key Points

Stated user intent does not equal feature adoption

  • Explanation: Users consistently report wanting AI features in research, but actual usage rates drop dramatically upon launch without careful UI integration and awareness.
  • Evidence: 78% expressed interest in AI dating tips during research, but only 10-15% of users actually used the photo feedback feature in production.
  • Practical implication: Integrate AI naturally into existing user flows with education and awareness.

Top-line averages can mask harmful model failure modes

  • Explanation: An overall positive A/B test result can hide negative or nonsensical edge cases in the long tail of user segments.
  • Evidence: Photo feedback increased overall photo updates, but sub-analysis revealed it instructed already-smiling users to smile.
  • Practical implication: Examine long-tail data and segment performance rather than relying solely on top-line conversion metrics.

GenAI cost scales with success

  • Explanation: Unlike traditional ML models where incremental serving costs are low, generative AI cost scales directly with user engagement and model size.
  • Evidence: Using top-tier LLMs yielded high quality but incurred 10x the cost of simpler approaches plus evaluation overhead.
  • Practical implication: Do not automatically pick the best model from the start; measure short-term versus long-term ROI.

Regulatory fragmentation limits global scaling

  • Explanation: Varying international AI laws mean features cannot always be rolled out globally at once.
  • Evidence: Meta AI and Bumble photo feedback restricted to specific countries due to regional regulatory hurdles.
  • Practical implication: Involve legal and compliance teams early before running experiments or planning global rollouts.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Check long-tail data and segment breakdowns in A/B tests, not just top-line averages.
    • Involve legal and compliance teams prior to running AI experiments.
    • Test cheaper, non-LLM baselines alongside advanced models from day one.
  • Strategic:
    • Embed AI capabilities natively into existing user workflows instead of building isolated standalone features.
    • Account for cumulative inference and evaluation costs when calculating genAI unit economics.
    • Recognize that feature success is tied to the model architecture and prompt engineering, not just the product concept.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • Are our AI evaluation metrics capturing harmful user-specific failure modes?
    • How do our inference costs scale as daily active users increase?
    • What are the compliance requirements for AI features across all target international markets?

Project ideas

4

Model-Portable Eval Qualifier

beyond-evals

A model-agnostic regression suite with per-capability assertions can pre-qualify a cheaper substitute model such that passing the suite predicts non-inferior A/B outcomes, while an unqualified swap flips the outcome.

Proof of concept

Build an eval suite for a photo-feedback-style task, score a top-tier model and a cheaper alternative against capability assertions, then run a small A/B or simulated experiment to compare eval predictions with measured lift.

Measurement

Rank correlation between eval pass rate and A/B lift; false-promotion rate for models that pass evals but fail in experiment; detection of outcome flips after model swaps.

Segment-Sliced Output Auditor

new

Output-level sampling sliced by user segment will detect materially more absurd or harmful generations than aggregate lift metrics alone, at a review cost that is small relative to the cost of shipping a tail failure.

Proof of concept

Replay stored generations from a probabilistic feature, sample outputs by segment, run rubric-based human or LLM review, and compare detected defects against what aggregate dashboard metrics would have missed.

Measurement

Defect detection rate by segment; review cost per detected defect; percentage of absurd outputs invisible to aggregate metrics.

Cost-Per-Success Router

gatehouse

Routing requests to a cheaper model with a stronger eval-and-guardrail layer can achieve lower cost per successful outcome than a top-tier LLM route without reducing user acceptance below a predefined threshold.

Proof of concept

Measurement
