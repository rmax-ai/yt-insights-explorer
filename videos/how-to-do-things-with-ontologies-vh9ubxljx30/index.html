Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: John Beverley
  • Channel: John Beverley
  • Main topic: How philosophical training in ontology engineering and formal ontologies provides unique value in solving big data interoperability challenges.
  • Purpose: To explain the value philosophers bring to ontology engineering, how ontologies solve big data interoperability challenges, and why demand for ontologists is growing. John Beverley, Assistant Professor at the University of Buffalo and VP of the National Center for Ontological Research (NCOR), presents on 'How to Do Things with Ontologies.' He discusses big data problems such as volume, velocity, variety, and veracity, focusing on information silos caused by incompatible storage systems and lack of standardized semantics. He introduces ontologies as formally well-defined, machine-interpretable controlled vocabularies and explains the role of Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) as a top-level architecture rooted in philosophical realism. Throughout the talk and subsequent Q&A, Beverley emphasizes why philosophers are uniquely suited for ontology engineering due to their training in conceptual analysis, formal logic, and handling disparate usage across scientific and industrial domains.

Philosophical Foundations: BFO, Realism, and Methodological Convictions

  • Explanation: Exploring Basic Formal Ontology (BFO), created by philosopher Barry Smith at the University of Buffalo, as the ISO-standard top-level architecture used across over 700 projects and government agencies.
  • Key claims:
    • Realism: Ontologies are designed to represent the world rather than simply concepts about the world.
    • Adequatism: BFO is non-reductive; classes and relations motivated by research communities are not paraphrased away.
    • Fallibilism: BFO is committed to tracking scientific research over time, recognizing that models might change.
  • Examples:
    • Distinguishing between independent continuants (material entities like tables or agents) and realizable entities (roles and dispositions like professor role or solubility).
    • Modeling Barry Smith as an instance of Agent, which is a subclass of Object, which is a subclass of Material Entity.
  • Terminology:
    • Basic Formal Ontology (BFO)
    • realism
    • adequatism
    • fallibilism
    • continuant
    • occurrent
    • disposition
    • role
  • Why it matters: Using a rigorous top-level ontology like BFO prevents ad-hoc modeling errors and provides guardrails for cross-domain interoperability.

    • They are trained to frame problems in multiple ways, identify subtle conceptual distinctions, and justify positions rigorously.
    • Philosophers excel at finding ontological solutions to messy domain problems, such as resolving ambiguity in virus definitions or pathogen dispositions.

Key Points

Big data problems stem from information silos, not just data volume

  • Explanation: Organizations struggle more with data incompatibility, proprietary formats, and lack of integration than raw storage capacity.
  • Evidence: NIST estimated lack of interoperability costs industrial datasets $21-43 billion; McKinsey estimates mid-size companies spend $20-50 million annually due to silos.
  • Practical implication: Computing strategies alone cannot solve fragmentation without semantic integration frameworks.

Realism over conceptualism in ontology design

  • Explanation: Ontologies should model the world as it is rather than modeling human concepts of the world to maintain coordination.
  • Evidence: Going from the world to words keeps researchers coordinated, whereas going from words to concepts leads to divergent definitions.
  • Practical implication: Ontology engineers must avoid subjective conceptual modeling and ground definitions in metaphysical reality.

Growing high-demand career path for philosophers in ontology

  • Explanation: There is a severe talent shortage of skilled ontologists across government agencies, financial institutions, and tech sectors, resulting in lucrative career opportunities.
  • Evidence: Job postings from organizations like Johns Hopkins APL, Morgan Stanley, Citi, and the University of Pennsylvania explicitly require philosophy backgrounds.
  • Practical implication: Philosophical training in logic and conceptual analysis translates directly into high-paying applied tech roles.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Basic Formal Ontology (BFO)

  • How it works: Provides a top-level ontological architecture with general categories (continuant, occurrent, material entity, realizable entity) that specific domain ontologies extend downward from.
  • Components:
    • Continuant (independent and dependent continuants)
    • Occurrent (processes, temporal regions)
    • Material Entity
    • Realizable Entity (roles, dispositions, functions)
  • When to use: When building domain ontologies that require rigorous interoperability, consistency checking, and alignment with standards like ISO.

Hub & Spoke Strategy

  • How it works: Uses a top-level hub ontology (like BFO) connected to multiple modular spoke ontologies representing specific domains via downward population.
  • Components:
    • Hub ontology (BFO)
    • Spoke ontologies (domain-specific modules)
    • Downward population hierarchy
    • Shared semantics guardrails
  • When to use: When integrating disparate data sources and ensuring consistent cross-domain validation and interoperability upfront.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

The interoperability problem is architectural, not storage-related: the primary cost of big data is incompatible storage systems and non-standardized semantics (information silos), not raw volume. The offered remedy is a formally-defined, machine-interpretable controlled vocabulary placed as a semantic layer between heterogeneous repositories and their consumers.

Why it matters

It relocates integration responsibility from per-silo application code to a shared formal semantic layer, which is exactly the boundary question engineers face when wiring multiple data sources, retrieval stores, or tool schemas into an agent.

Generalization

Any system integrating N heterogeneous sources should invest in one shared machine-interpretable semantic layer rather than N^2 point-to-point mappings; the mapping/querying burden collapses onto the shared layer.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Realism as a modeling discipline: definitions should be anchored to the world (referents) rather than to human concepts about the world. The claimed asymmetry is that world-to-words pathways keep independent parties coordinated, while words-to-concepts pathways produce divergent, mutually incompatible definitions.

Why it matters

It inverts a common practice of defining shared vocabularies by agreeing on concept labels; if coordination is the goal, the anchor must be an external referent, which materially changes how shared schemas, memory keys, and tool contracts are authored.

Generalization

In any multi-agent or multi-team setting, ground shared identifiers in observable environment state/entities rather than in negotiated concept labels to reduce semantic drift.

Source video ↗
[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

A top-level ontology such as BFO functions as a stable upper architecture that constrains domain models, reused across 700+ projects and government agencies. Its three stated commitments are realism (represent the world), adequatism (non-reductive: do not paraphrase away classes/relations that research communities need), and fallibilism (commit to revising the model as scientific findings change).

Why it matters

This is a concrete pattern for a two-tier schema regime — a small, revisable upper layer enforcing invariants, plus flexible domain layers — which is directly analogous to designing constrained core abstractions for agent memory, tool interfaces, or shared state.

Generalization

Separate a minimal invariant core schema from domain-extensible layers; make the core explicitly revisable so accuracy improvement does not require rewriting domain layers.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

The ontology distinguishes independent continuants (material entities such as tables or agents) from realizable entities (roles and dispositions, e.g., a professor role or solubility) — i.e., capabilities and roles are modeled as first-class entities that inhere in continuants rather than as boolean state flags.

Why it matters

Modeling unexercised capabilities as explicit entities enables reasoning about what an entity could do, not just what it currently is — relevant to capability registries, tool availability, and permission modeling in agent runtimes.

Generalization

Represent latent capabilities/dispositions as distinct typed entities attached to substrates, so planners can reason over potential behavior independent of current activation state.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Adequatism vs. model parsimony

Benefit

Non-reductive modeling preserves every class and relation that a research community needs, maximizing fidelity and adoption.

Cost or risk

Refusing to paraphrase classes away grows the model and can sacrifice simplicity/compactness, complicating maintenance and reasoning.

Source video ↗

Fallibilism vs. downstream stability

Benefit

Committing to track scientific research keeps the top-level ontology accurate over time.

Cost or risk

If the upper ontology may change, downstream adopters face version churn and migration costs on a foundation they depend on.

Source video ↗

Neutrality vs. domain expertise

Benefit

A domain-neutral ontologist can arbitrate conflicting definitions without being invested in one camp.

Cost or risk

The neutral arbiter lacks deep domain practice, so resolution quality depends on eliciting and checking expert input rather than native expertise.

Source video ↗

Realism vs. speed of vocabulary agreement

Benefit

Anchoring to the world rather than to concepts keeps independent parties coordinated.

Cost or risk

Realist commitments require resolving what actually exists in the domain, which is slower and more contentious than agreeing on shared labels.
