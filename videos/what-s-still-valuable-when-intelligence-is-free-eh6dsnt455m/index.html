Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Clifford Sosin (with Patrick O'Shaughnessy)
  • Channel: Invest Like The Best
  • Main topic: The economics and limits of artificial intelligence, intelligence as a commodity, and what remains valuable when intelligence becomes free and ubiquitous.
  • Purpose: To analyze how the democratization and cost collapse of intelligence affect business models, investing strategies, economic moats, and capital allocation. In this in-depth conversation, Clifford Sosin and Patrick O'Shaughnessy explore the economic implications of AI becoming cheap, abundant, and ubiquitous. They discuss Jevons's paradox applied to intelligence, the nature of complex dynamic systems, supply versus demand-driven advantages in business, and what complements to intelligence (like attention, data, physical proximity, and regulatory capture) retain value as intelligence costs collapse toward zero.

Topic Map

Intelligence as a Commodity and Jevons's Paradox

  • Explanation: Discussion on how falling costs of intelligence lead to vastly increased usage, but also hit economic limits where further intelligence yields diminishing returns due to the unknowledgeable nature of complex dynamic systems.
  • Key claims:
    • Intelligence is becoming a cheap, ubiquitous commodity.
    • As intelligence costs collapse, usage explodes (Jevons's paradox).
    • Intelligence is a gadget used to make predictions about complex dynamic systems, but intelligence has fundamental limits.
  • Examples:
    • Weather forecasting models where tiny initial rounding errors compound over time to completely alter predictions.
    • Grinder (the dating app) constrained by being a smaller company despite having attention and data.
  • Terminology:
    • Jevons's paradox
    • Complex dynamic systems
    • Unknowability
  • Why it matters: Understanding the limits of intelligence helps investors avoid the trap of assuming infinite intelligence solves all business and forecasting problems.

Complements to Intelligence: Data, Attention, and Physical Proximity

  • Explanation: When intelligence becomes free and ubiquitous, what you want to own are the complements: proprietary data, attention, love, physical proximity, scale, and regulatory capture.
  • Key claims:
    • If intelligence becomes cheap and ubiquitous, owners of complements capture the rents.
    • Data and attention are primary complements to intelligence.
    • Physical proximity and scale create durable moats that pure intelligence cannot easily replicate.
  • Examples:
    • Meta's core business of personalized TV/social media capturing attention and data.
    • TSMC's physical scale and manufacturing capability as a hard-to-replicate supply advantage.
  • Terminology:
    • Complements
    • Value chain
    • Bottleneck
  • Why it matters: Directs capital allocation toward owning scarce complements rather than commoditized software or pure intelligence tools.

Supply-Side versus Demand-Side Advantages

  • Explanation: Examining whether businesses should focus on supply-side efficiency or demand-side market dynamics, and how AI alters the competitive landscape.
  • Key claims:
    • Markets are U-shaped: terrible products have no demand, good products have high willingness to pay but small markets, and cheap products capture large markets.
    • AI agents will automate low-cost optimization, flattening price discrepancies.
    • The best businesses combine quantitative strategy with qualitative human judgment.
  • Examples:
    • Garbage collection businesses where local density matters more than software intelligence.
    • Carvana's infrastructure-heavy business model compared to pure software plays.
  • Terminology:
    • Price elasticity of demand
    • Marginal cost
    • Aggregation economics
  • Why it matters: Helps investors evaluate whether operational efficiency or market structure provides sustainable competitive advantage.

Education, Skill Building, and Human Judgment

  • Explanation: How education needs to adapt in an AI-driven world, emphasizing fundamental skills and the ability to direct models rather than just consume outputs.
  • Key claims:
    • Kids need to learn foundational skills (like math) deeply so they can direct models effectively.
    • Without fundamental understanding, humans cannot judge or correct AI outputs.
    • Ambition and the willingness to take hard intellectual risks remain uniquely human.
  • Examples:
    • Students cheating on essays versus being forced to build rigorous foundational understanding.
    • Using AI as a video explainer for children instead of simple chatbots.
  • Terminology:
    • Bimodal education
    • Verification loop
    • Foundational skills
  • Why it matters: Informs how individuals and institutions should structure learning and cognitive development in the age of AI.

Key Points

Intelligence has fundamental limits when applied to complex dynamic systems.

  • Explanation: Even super-intelligent models cannot perfectly predict weather, human behavior, or macroeconomic cycles because of initial condition sensitivity and unquantifiable realities.
  • Evidence: The historical example of weather forecasting models diverging after a single decimal point is rounded.
  • Practical implication: Do not overestimate what AI can predict; acknowledge uncertainty in complex systems.

Owners of scarce complements capture value when a core input becomes free.

  • Explanation: As AI intelligence costs drop to near zero, the economic value shifts to proprietary data, attention, physical infrastructure, and regulatory moats.
  • Evidence: Meta leveraging massive user attention and data streams versus pure algorithmic competitors.
  • Practical implication: Invest in businesses that own proprietary data and user attention rather than generic software wrappers.

Vibe coding and hackathons change corporate culture expectations.

  • Explanation: Companies are shifting toward internal hackathons and high-frequency experimentation, changing how software and products are built.
  • Evidence: Carvana's pizza party/hackathon culture approach applied to enterprise software development.
  • Practical implication: Organizations must adopt AI tooling rapidly to avoid being outpaced by leaner competitors.

The Intelligence-Complement Framework

  • How it works: Identifies primary inputs that are commoditizing (intelligence/algorithms) and pairs them with scarce complementary assets (data, attention, physical proximity) to find durable business moats.
  • Components:
    • Commoditized Intelligence
    • Scarce Complements (Data, Attention, Scale)
    • Regulatory Capture
  • When to use: Evaluating tech investments and business sustainability in a rapidly deflating AI cost environment.

The U-Shaped Market Demand Curve

  • How it works: Analyzes market structure based on product quality and price, explaining why poor products fail, moderate products face fierce competition, and differentiated products capture extreme value.
  • Components:
    • Terrible product (zero demand)
    • Good product (high price, small market)
    • Cheap/ubiquitous product (massive volume, low margin)
  • When to use: Assessing pricing power and market sizing for software and AI products.

  • Illustrates: The butterfly effect and sensitivity to initial conditions in complex dynamic systems.
  • Lesson: Simulation and intelligence are bounded by error compounding in complex systems.

Meta spending massive amounts on data centers, video infrastructure, and personalized content delivery.

  • Illustrates: Owning attention and data as complements to intelligence.
  • Lesson: Scale and attention provide an economic moat that raw algorithms alone cannot overcome.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Intelligence is a prediction gadget applied to complex dynamic systems, and it hits a hard ceiling there: tiny perturbations in initial conditions compound until predictions diverge completely, so more model capability does not buy proportional predictive accuracy in chaotic domains.

Why it matters

Justifies a hard architectural boundary: separate tasks where scaling inference reliably improves outcomes (closed, well-conditioned domains) from tasks where it does not (human behavior, macro cycles, weather-like chaos), and design the latter for uncertainty propagation rather than point predictions.

Generalization

Any agent that forecasts a chaotic target needs uncertainty budgets and fallback policies, not just a bigger model; capability spend should be allocated by domain conditioning, not uniformly.

Why it matters

Decides where architectural boundaries and defensibility belong: building on a commoditizing model layer means the durable asset must be the data capture, feedback loop, distribution, or physical/regulatory asset your system sits on top of.

Generalization

For any system built on a rapidly deflating input cost (model calls, compute, tokens), value accrues to whichever layer in the chain is hardest to replicate; design the system to own that layer.

Project ideas

Verifier Substitute Bench

gatehouse

Executable ground-truth tests detect injected model errors with higher recall per dollar than cross-model critique when the human reviewer lacks domain expertise.

Proof of concept

Generate outputs across three task types, inject labeled errors, and compare verifiers: unit/executable tests, retrieval-grounded checks, cross-model critic, and non-expert human baseline.

Measurement

Error-detection recall, precision, false-block rate, and cost per detected error for each verifier.

Capability Saturation Mapper

beyond-evals

On chaotic forecasting targets, accuracy versus inference budget saturates and becomes perturbation-fragile, while well-conditioned targets improve monotonically with budget.

Proof of concept

Benchmark synthetic chaotic series against stable series, vary model/inference budget, and measure accuracy under initial-condition perturbations.

Measurement

Accuracy-cost slope, saturation threshold, perturbation degradation, and abstention rate by domain.

movement-lab

Architectural implications

3

Value accrues to complements, not to the commoditizing intelligence layer.

Before

Architect around model capability as the differentiating asset, with data as an incidental byproduct.

After

Architect explicitly around capture of proprietary data, attention, distribution, or physical/regulatory assets, treating models as swappable commodity components behind an abstraction.

Consequence

Model providers become replaceable dependencies; the durable subsystem is the data/feedback capture path, which must be instrumented from day one.

Source video ↗

Prediction of complex dynamic systems diverges under initial-condition sensitivity.

Before

Design pipelines that emit point predictions and treat higher capability as monotonically better accuracy.

After

Design for uncertainty propagation, bounded-horizon predictions, and explicit abstention or human escalation in chaotic domains.

Consequence

Evaluation shifts from accuracy-on-averaged-benchmarks to behavior under perturbation and graceful degradation.

Source video ↗

Verification, not generation, is the bottleneck.

Before

Human-in-the-loop is a late-stage approval step with limited domain grounding.

After

Verification is a first-class subsystem with independent ground truth, graders, and domain-specific checks that run continuously rather than at the end.

Consequence

Architecture gains a parallel evaluation path whose quality bounds overall system reliability and gates release.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Commodity intelligence and usage elasticity

Benefit

Cheap, ubiquitous intelligence enables workloads that were previously uneconomic, expanding what can be automated.

Cost or risk

Elastic demand means total spend can rise even as unit cost falls, and dependency on volume-intensive inference grows.

Universal agent-driven optimization

Benefit

Agents automate low-cost optimization and remove manual inefficiency from operations.

Cost or risk

If every competitor runs equivalent optimizers, the resulting efficiencies and price discrepancies are arbitraged away, eroding their value as an edge.

Source video ↗

Owning a complement without scale

Benefit

Data and attention are the primary complements to commoditized intelligence and appear to confer a moat.

Cost or risk

A smaller holder of the same complement remains constrained; the bottleneck moves elsewhere and the asset fails to convert into advantage.

Source video ↗

Delegating to models without foundational skill

Benefit

Models can be directed with minimal domain knowledge, lowering the entry barrier to producing outputs.

Cost or risk

Without foundational understanding, users cannot judge or correct outputs, so errors pass through unverified and the verification loop collapses.
