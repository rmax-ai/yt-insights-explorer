Overview

  • Speaker: Lucia Rahilly (Host), Tanguy Catlin, Lari Hämäläinen
  • Channel: McKinsey & Company
  • Main topic: Improving the economics of agentic AI and ROI for enterprise business leaders
  • Purpose: To provide business leaders with research-backed guidance on improving the economic returns of agentic AI through deliberate workflow redesign, cost management, and strategic capability ownership. McKinsey leaders discuss the current state of AI adoption, the gap between individual productivity gains and enterprise-level return on investment (ROI), token usage and pricing dynamics, agentic workflows, and a five-step CEO to-do list for building an agentic operating model.

Key Points

Adoption vs. Value Creation

  • Explanation: Most companies are using AI for personal tooling (chatbots) rather than enterprise-wide re-engineering.
  • Evidence: 89% adoption rate versus only 6% reporting positive EBIT impact.
  • Practical implication: Companies must move beyond individual productivity gains to redesign end-to-end business workflows.

Token Consumption Surge

  • Explanation: Agentic workflows and automated coding assistants generate massive token usage, causing budgets to be exceeded.
  • Evidence: 93% of CIOs report exceeding their AI token budget; token usage growing at ~2,000% CAGR.
  • Practical implication: Establish strict governance and token efficiency measures to control operational costs.

The Economics of Intelligence Formula

  • Explanation: An AI agent pays for itself if its success probability clears the verification-to-task time ratio.
  • Evidence: P(success) > verification time / do-it-yourself time.
  • Practical implication: Evaluate agent deployments based on error recovery costs and verification overhead.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Economics of Intelligence / Agent ROI Framework

  • How it works: Compares the probability of agent success against the time required to verify the output versus doing the task manually.
  • Components:
    • Probability the agent succeeds (P)
    • Time to check the agent's work (verify)
    • Time for human to do the task (do)
  • When to use: When deciding whether to deploy an AI agent for a specific enterprise task.

The CEO To-Do List for Building the Agentic Operating Model

  • How it works: A five-step roadmap for enterprise leaders to successfully integrate and scale agentic AI.
  • Components:
    • Set differentiation strategy before technology strategy
    • Own your data on proprietary context
    • Build agentic capability as a discipline and redesign workflows
    • Reshape the boundary of the firm (deliberate build, buy, partner)
    • Run economics as tightly as deployment
  • When to use: When planning or executing an enterprise AI transformation.

Core insights

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Agent deployment has an explicit economic threshold: an agent only lifts productivity when P(success) exceeds the ratio of verification time to do-it-yourself time. Verification cost, not raw model quality, is the gating variable for whether an agent is worth running.

Why it matters

Gives engineers a computable go/no-go criterion for a given task instead of intuition, and reframes the design goal from 'maximize model accuracy' to 'make verification cheap relative to the task' (cheap checkers, deterministic assertions, tight output contracts).

Generalization

Any automated system with a probabilistic success rate and a required human review step should be evaluated on the review-cost-to-manual-cost ratio, not just accuracy; this applies to code review bots, data pipelines, and document generation alike.

[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltymoderate evidence

Identical agentic tasks can vary up to 30x in cost because agents take probabilistic execution paths, whereas classic software has near-deterministic unit cost.

Why it matters

Cost becomes a distribution rather than a fixed unit price, which breaks per-task budgeting, capacity planning, and any SLAs that assume predictable spend. It implies retry budgets, per-task cost caps, and step-count/loop-count limits as first-class runtime primitives.

Generalization

Any system with probabilistic control flow (retries, tool-call loops, self-repair) needs variance-aware cost controls, not average-cost accounting.

Source video ↗
[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

60% of agentic token spend goes to refinement rather than initial generation, making the repair/verify loop — not the first-shot generation — the dominant cost center of an agent architecture.

Why it matters

Optimization effort should target the loop structure (how many refinement passes, what triggers a retry, how much context is re-sent each pass) instead of first-token generation quality. It also explains why naive 'bigger model' upgrades may not reduce total cost.

Generalization

In iterative generation systems, cost is governed by iteration count and context re-transmission per iteration; architectural choices that bound iterations usually beat model-quality improvements for cost control.

[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltymoderate evidence

For any technology where capability improvements unlock new consumption patterns (Jevons-style effects), track total cost of consumption, not unit price, as the governing metric.

Why

Article ideas

4

Stop optimizing tokens per unit and start optimizing tokens per verified outcome

The classic cloud cost playbook — wait for unit prices to fall — is inverted in agentic AI: cheaper per-token inference unlocks more ambitious consumption, so total spend is governed by loop count, context re-transmission, and verification cost rather than model price. Cost control is therefore a control-flow and verification design problem, not a procurement problem.

Angle

Argue that cost observability, per-workflow token attribution, and retry/step limits are production infrastructure, and that unit-price metrics are actively misleading when consumption grows faster than price declines.

Source video ↗

Verification is the product: designing agents around the cheapest possible check

Because an agent only pays off when success probability exceeds the ratio of verification time to manual task time, verification is the cost-determining subsystem of any agent architecture. Teams that treat human review as an informal afterthought will deploy agents that lose money on tasks a person would have finished faster.

Angle

Derive concrete design consequences: output schemas chosen for cheap checkability, deterministic assertions and static analysis co-deployed with generation, and bounded human review surfaced as an explicit line item.

Source video ↗

Rebuild the process or inherit the waste: why agent insertion fails

Inserting agents into legacy workflows inherits the original design assumptions — human latency, handoffs, batch steps — as new waste. Structural gains come from end-to-end redesign of a few high-value processes rebuilt from scratch, piloted as lighthouses rather than spread as shallow insertion.

Angle

Contrast shallow tool insertion against end-to-end redesign of 2-3 lighthouse processes, and connect redesign to proprietary context ownership as the reason the result is defensible rather than replicable.

Source video ↗

When generation outpaces verification, throughput is capped by review

Autonomous coding agents produce far more output than humans, which shifts the bottleneck from generation to human review capacity. Any high-volume generation agent deployed without automated verification and test harnesses will increase token spend without increasing delivered throughput.

Angle

Frame the generation/verification asymmetry as the same ROI threshold seen from the delivery side, and prescribe co-deployed automated verification as a release requirement rather than a follow-up project.

Project ideas

4

Agent ROI Gate

gatehouse

A deployment gate that scores candidate tasks on measured generation time, verification time, and manual baseline time will predict which tasks clear P(success) > verify/do, and tasks above the threshold will show measurable productivity lift while those below will not.

Proof of concept

Instrument 20-30 candidate tasks across coding, research, and transactional workflows; log manual time, agent time, success rate, and verification time; rank tasks by the computed ratio and run a controlled A/B on the top and bottom ranked tasks.

Measurement

Predicted-vs-actual productivity delta (agent time + verification time vs manual time), false-positive deployment rate, and correlation between the pre-computed ratio and observed lift.

Source video ↗

Variance Governor Runtime

new

Enforcing per-task spend ceilings, retry budgets, and step/loop caps at runtime will cut the spread between p50 and p95 task cost on identical tasks substantially while keeping task success rate within a small tolerance of ungoverned execution.

Proof of concept

Run a fixed task suite with identical inputs across an ungoverned agent and a governed agent with configurable loop bounds, retry caps, and hard per-task spend ceilings; log per-task token cost distribution and success rate.

Measurement

Ratio of p95 to p50 task cost, share of tasks hitting the ceiling, change in success rate, and reduction in budget-overrun incidents at the workflow level.

Source video ↗

Refinement Budget Benchmark

beyond-evals

Loop topologies that separate generation from bounded refinement and minimize context re-sent per pass will reduce refinement-token share materially versus an unbounded emergent self-repair loop, without reducing end-to-end task success.

Proof of concept

Implement three topologies on the same task set — single-shot with strong output contracts, bounded self-repair (fixed pass count), and critic-verifier loop — with token accounting that splits generation, refinement, and re-transmitted context.

Measurement

Refinement-token share of total spend per topology, total tokens per successful task, and task success rate; test whether refinement share drops below the ~60% baseline.

Source video ↗

Lighthouse Outcome Attribution Harness

movement-lab

Rebuilding 2-3 high-value processes end-to-end with agentic workflows and instrumenting outcome-level metrics will show materially larger EBIT-linked impact than agent insertion into the same processes, and the gap will be invisible under adoption/usage metrics alone.

Proof of concept

Select two lighthouse processes; redesign one end-to-end and instrument the other with shallow insertion; deploy shared outcome instrumentation (cost-to-serve, cycle time, error/rework rate) alongside usage telemetry.

Measurement

Outcome metric delta between redesign and insertion arms versus usage/adoption metric delta between the same arms, quantifying the usage-to-value gap.

Architectural implications

5

Verification is treated as the cost-determining step in agent economics rather than an add-on.

Before

Agents are designed around generation quality; human review is an informal afterthought.

After

Verification is a first-class subsystem: cheap automated checkers, assertions, and bounded human review surfaced as an explicit cost in the agent's design.

Consequence

Task selection, output schemas, and tool interfaces are chosen to minimize verification time, making cheaply checkable outputs a design requirement rather than a nicety.

Source video ↗

Cost is a distribution rather than a per-task constant in agentic workflows.

Before

Cost planning assumes a deterministic unit cost per task and average-based budgeting.

After

Runtime enforces retry caps, step limits, and per-task spend ceilings, with cost variance instrumented per workflow.

Consequence

Budget overruns surface at the workflow level and can be contained by architectural controls (loop bounds, context pruning) rather than by policy memos.

Source video ↗

The refinement loop dominates token spend, so loop topology is the primary cost lever.

Before

Iterative self-repair is an emergent property of the agent loop, unconstrained and unmeasured.

After

Refinement passes are explicitly bounded, and context re-sent per pass is minimized or cached.

Consequence

Cost control shifts from model selection to control-flow design, and the separation between generation and refinement becomes an explicit architectural boundary.

Tool-call protocol choice (MCP vs direct API) is treated as an architectural cost/variance question.

Before

Tool integration is chosen on convenience and compatibility alone.

After

Protocol and topology choices (single agent vs multi-agent swarm, MCP vs direct API) are evaluated against their token overhead and path variance.

Consequence

Tool integration becomes an economics decision, and multi-agent designs must justify their added token and coordination cost.

Proprietary context is named as the defensible asset, not the model.

Before

After

Context, data ownership, and firm-boundary decisions (build/buy/partner) determine where differentiation resides.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Falling per-token price vs. exploding token consumption

Benefit

Cheaper frontier inference enables more ambitious agentic workflows and coding assistants.

Cost or risk

Total spend rises faster than unit price falls, producing budget overruns for most organizations.

Source video ↗

Agent autonomy vs. verification overhead

Benefit

Agents execute tasks without continuous human involvement.

Cost or risk

The agent only pays off if success probability exceeds the verification-to-task time ratio; low-probability tasks impose review costs that can exceed manual execution.

Source video ↗

Generation throughput vs. review capacity

Benefit

Coding agents produce code far faster than humans.

Cost or risk

Generated volume outpaces human review, and the extra iteration drives token consumption upward.

Source video ↗

Broad adoption vs. realized value

Benefit

Near-universal adoption of AI tooling spreads familiarity and usage.

Cost or risk

Adoption without workflow redesign yields little measurable EBIT impact, so investment concentrates without return.

Probabilistic agent paths vs. predictable unit economics

Benefit

Agents can handle variable, open-ended tasks without brittle hand-coded branches.

Cost or risk

Cost per identical task can swing up to 30x, undermining budgeting and capacity planning.
