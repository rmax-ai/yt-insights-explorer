Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Nouha Dziri
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Optimizing multi-agent coding teams for cost, performance, and efficiency at inference time.
  • Purpose: To provide a systematic framework and empirical findings on how to design, scale, and optimize multi-agent coding teams for maximum reward and minimum inference cost. Nouha Dziri from Cohere presents research on optimizing multi-agent coding teams. Exploring inference-time compute scaling through multi-agent collaboration rather than just thinking longer or sampling more, the talk details how structuring agent teams (lead and worker models), allocating tasks across software engineering stages (planning, implementation, verification), and mixing model capabilities (strong leads with cheap planners or workers) can achieve frontier coding performance at 70% lower cost.

Topic Map

Scaling Inference-Time Compute

  • Explanation: Discusses traditional methods of scaling inference-time compute (think longer via longer chains of reasoning, sample more via parallel independent attempts) and introduces a new axis: working as a team where agents split tasks and coordinate.
  • Key claims:
    • Traditional scaling methods (thinking longer or sampling more) lead to context window bloat and failure on long tasks.
    • Working as a team introduces a new axis for scaling inference-time compute.
  • Examples:
    • Single long-running agents exhausting context windows with build logs, stack traces, and diffs.
  • Terminology:
    • inference-time compute
    • context window bloat
    • chain of reasoning
  • Why it matters: Context window bloat makes single long-running agents inefficient on complex, multi-hour coding tasks, necessitating decomposed multi-agent teams.

Multi-Agent Team Design as a Compute-Allocation Problem

  • Explanation: Framing multi-agent team design mathematically around four variables: stage of software engineering, worker model, number of workers, and lead model, measured by efficiency (reward per added dollar).
  • Key claims:
    • Team design can be formulated as a compute-allocation problem to maximize reward per added dollar.
    • Efficiency is defined as the reward difference between team and solo agent divided by cost.
  • Examples:
    • Hierarchical harness with a lead agent distributing subtasks to workers and consolidating results.
  • Terminology:
    • compute-allocation problem
    • efficiency metric
    • team configuration
    • hierarchical harness
  • Why it matters: Allows systematic optimization of agent teams balancing performance gains against skyrocketing token costs.

Stage-Specific Allocation and The Planning Bottleneck

  • Explanation: Investigating which stage of software engineering (Planning, Implementation, Verification) benefits most from extra agents. Results show planning is the primary bottleneck and most efficient place to spend compute.
  • Key claims:
    • Discovery and planning are the primary bottlenecks in reverse-engineering binaries.
    • Planning-only delegation matches full PIV (Planning, Implementation, Verification) performance at half the cost.
    • Scaling workers in implementation or verification stages yields little to no performance gain while increasing cost.
  • Examples:
    • ProgramBench benchmark where agents rebuild programs from binaries and documentation.
  • Terminology:
    • ProgramBench
    • bottleneck
    • planning-only delegation
    • PIV
  • Why it matters: Directs resources precisely where they generate the highest ROI, avoiding wasteful spending on implementation workers.

Heterogeneous Model Teams: Cheap Workers and Strong Leads

  • Explanation: Testing whether cheaper model variants (Luna, Terra, Sol) can be used as workers or leads. Shows that a strong lead with cheap planners/workers achieves near-frontier results at 70% lower cost.
  • Key claims:
    • The lead sets the ceiling on performance and workers set the price.
    • A strong lead (Sol) combined with cheap planners (Luna) achieves similar performance to all-Sol teams at 60% less cost.
    • A strong lead with cheap workers (fixed size) achieves 70% lower cost compared to all-stage worker teams.
  • Examples:
    • Using GPT-5.6 Sol as lead with Luna as planners/workers.
  • Terminology:
    • heterogeneous team
    • Luna
    • Terra
    • Sol
    • scaling law
  • Why it matters: Enables enterprise deployment of advanced coding agents at a fraction of the cost.

Agent Communication Dynamics

  • Explanation: Analyzing how agents communicate. Findings show 84% of messages go from workers to the lead, with fewer than 1 worker-to-worker message per task, indicating hierarchical structure even when peer communication is enabled.
  • Key claims:
    • Agents predominantly communicate up to the lead rather than laterally between peers.
    • Peer messaging in harnesses did not raise reward and often introduced noise or malformed messages.
  • Examples:
    • Message frequency charts across models like Claude Opus, Gemini, and GPT variants.
  • Terminology:
    • peer messaging
    • worker-to-worker communication
    • status report
  • Why it matters: Simplifies harness design by showing complex peer-to-peer communication protocols are unnecessary and often counterproductive.

Key Points

Teams are a new axis of inference-time scaling

  • Explanation: Decomposing tasks across multiple agents yields up to ~10 points of reward improvement over single agents.
  • Evidence: ProgramBench evaluation showing performance jumps with multi-agent harnesses.
  • Practical implication: Allows tackling complex coding tasks beyond single-agent context limits.

Planning is the bottleneck in reverse engineering

  • Explanation: Adding workers to the planning stage yields 1.6x more reward per dollar than implementation or verification.
  • Evidence: Efficiency ratios across P, I, and V stages on ProgramBench.
  • Practical implication: Allocate extra agent budget to exploration and planning rather than code writing.

The lead sets the ceiling, workers set the price

  • Explanation: Strong capability is required at the leadership level for coordination, while cheaper models suffice for worker subtasks.
  • Evidence: Comparative charts of Luna, Terra, and Sol model combinations.
  • Practical implication: Optimize inference budget by deploying expensive frontier models only as leads.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Hierarchical Multi-Agent Coding Harness

  • How it works: A lead agent receives the task, decomposes it into subtasks, delegates them to worker agents working in fresh contexts, and consolidates the compact results.
  • Components:
    • Lead agent
    • Worker agents
    • Delegate tool
    • Shared workspace
  • When to use: For complex coding tasks, reverse engineering, and multi-file repository navigation.

Examples & Case Studies

Agents on ProgramBench attempting to rebuild binaries like dsq or ffmpeg from documentation.

  • Illustrates: Discovery bottleneck where understanding binary interface and edge cases requires parallel exploration.
  • Lesson: Parallelizing discovery through planning workers drastically improves code reconstruction quality.

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Delegate tasks to worker agents starting with fresh contexts.
    • Focus multi-agent scaling on the planning and exploration stage.
    • Use strong models for leads and cheaper models for workers.
  • Strategic:
    • Treat agent team design as a compute-allocation optimization problem.
    • Avoid complex peer-to-peer communication protocols; hierarchical reporting to the lead is more efficient.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • How should thousands of agents be organized in self-organizing vs hierarchical structures?
    • How can multi-agent security and auditing be enforced as error propagation grows?

Core insights

6
[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltymoderate evidence

Compute should be concentrated in the planning/discovery stage rather than implementation or verification: planning-only delegation matches full Planning→Implementation→Verification performance at half the cost, and extra planning workers return ~1.6x more reward per dollar than extra implementation or verification workers.

Why it matters

It inverts the intuitive instinct to parallelize code writing. Teams that scale implementation workers pay more for flat reward; teams that parallelize exploration of an unknown interface (binary, undocumented repo) get the gain. Architecturally, this says the expensive, wide fan-out belongs upstream of code generation.

Generalization

In any agentic task with an unknown-spec discovery phase (reverse engineering, legacy migration, unfamiliar codebase onboarding), the bottleneck is information acquisition, not artifact production; allocate parallel agents to reduce uncertainty before generating artifacts.

[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltymoderate evidence

Heterogeneous teams beat homogeneous ones: model capability at the lead position determines the performance ceiling, while worker model choice determines price, so a frontier lead with cheap workers/planners reaches near-frontier reward at ~60-70% lower cost.

Why it matters

This gives a concrete, deployable cost lever: spend the frontier-model budget only on the orchestrator that decomposes and consolidates, and route subtask execution to cheaper tiers. It reframes model selection from a single global choice into a per-role allocation decision.

Generalization

In hierarchical agent systems, put capability where context is synthesized and decisions are made (decomposition, consolidation, judging), and cheap capacity where work is parallel and independently verifiable.

[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltymoderate evidence

Generalization

When models are given both hierarchical and lateral communication channels, they route status upward; adding unneeded channels mostly adds protocol surface area and failure modes rather than capability.

[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Multi-agent decomposition is a distinct axis of inference-time scaling from thinking longer or sampling more, and it exists specifically because single long-running agents suffer context window bloat from build logs, stack traces, and diffs, which causes failure on long tasks.

Why it matters

It names the failure mechanism (context saturation by verbose tool output) rather than treating multi-agent as a generic improvement. The design implication is context hygiene: workers operate in fresh contexts and return compact results, so the lead's window holds decisions, not artifacts.

Generalization

Any long-horizon agent whose tool outputs are high-volume/low-information (logs, traces, diffs) will degrade by context dilution; splitting into fresh-context workers with summarized returns is a context-budget management technique, not just a parallelism technique.

[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Team design can be formalized as a compute-allocation problem over four variables — software-engineering stage, worker model, worker count, and lead model — with efficiency defined as the reward delta between team and solo agent divided by cost.

Why it matters

It turns multi-agent configuration from taste into a measurable optimization objective with an explicit denominator. Teams get evaluated on marginal reward per added dollar, which correctly penalizes configurations that buy small gains at large cost even when absolute reward is higher.

Generalization

Any agentic system with configurable fan-out and per-role model tiers should be tuned against a marginal-efficiency metric (delta-reward per delta-cost), not raw benchmark score, because raw score monotonically rewards spending.

Deep dives

5

Stage-aware compute allocation: is planning always the bottleneck?

Research question

Does the planning-bottleneck result (planning-only delegation matching full PIV at half the cost, ~1.6x reward-per-dollar advantage for planning workers) survive outside binary reverse engineering — on repo-level feature work, bug fixing against test suites, and legacy migrations where the spec is partially known?

Why

If planning dominates only when discovery cost is unusually high relative to implementation, then the headline cost claim is a special case, and harnesses that hard-code planning-heavy fan-out will misallocate budget on tasks where the difficulty lives in verification. Establishing the boundary condition is prerequisite to shipping a stage-aware scheduler as a default.

Source video ↗

The lead-ceiling / worker-price frontier and its degradation floor

Research question

How far can worker capability be degraded before a frontier lead can no longer compensate through consolidation, and does weak-worker output ever drag the team below the lead's solo-agent performance?

Why

The 'lead sets the ceiling, workers set the price' claim is the entire economic case for heterogeneous teams, but it reports a single point (Sol lead + Luna planners) rather than a surface. Without knowing the floor and the shape of the curve, teams cannot set a worker tier with any confidence, and a budget cut that crosses the floor turns a 60% saving into a regression.

Source video ↗

Context isolation versus intra-thread compaction as the real mechanism behind multi-agent gains

Research question

How much of the measured ~10-point team gain is attributable to fresh worker contexts (context-budget management) rather than to parallel exploration per se, and can an equivalent gain be recovered in a single agent by structurally evicting verbose tool output?

Why

If the benefit is mostly context hygiene, then multi-agent orchestration is an expensive way to buy a context-management feature, and a single-agent harness with tool-output offloading might capture most of the gain at far lower coordination overhead. If the benefit survives control for context, decomposition is genuinely doing something else. Separating these determines whether the complexity of a lead/worker harness is justified.

Source video ↗

Emergent hierarchy versus harness-induced hierarchy

Research question

Is the observed near-total upward message flow (84% worker-to-lead, under one worker-to-worker message per task) a property of the models, or an artifact of the delegate tool and prompt structure — and would a protocol actively rewarding lateral coordination change both topology and reward?

Why

The engineering conclusion 'do not build peer-to-peer channels' currently rests on observational data from one harness. If the topology is harness-induced, then that conclusion is circular: the harness never made lateral coordination cheap or legible. Distinguishing model preference from harness bias decides whether peer protocols are dead ends or just under-designed.

Source video ↗

Marginal-efficiency evaluation as the default agent benchmark metric

Research question

Does scoring agent configurations on marginal reward per added dollar (rather than absolute reward) change which architectures win, and what does the reward-versus-cost frontier look like for the same task family?

Why

Raw benchmark scores monotonically reward spending, so leaderboards systematically select for expensive configurations. A cost-normalized metric is the only way to make the planning-versus-scaling tradeoff legible across teams, and it converts a single number into a curve that reveals saturation points where additional workers stop paying.

Article ideas

4

Your Parallel Code-Writing Workers Are Burning Budget for Nothing

Parallel agent fan-out pays only where uncertainty is being reduced, not where artifacts are being produced: the empirical ordering — planning workers return ~1.6x the reward per dollar of implementation or verification workers, and planning-only delegation matches full planning-implementation-verification at half the cost — implies that any harness that scales uniformly across stages, or that scales the code-writing stage because it looks like 'the work,' is structurally over-spending. The argument is that agent teams should be budgeted like information acquisition, with wide fan-out upstream of code generation and narrow execution downstream.

Angle

Reframe the planning-vs-implementation cost result as a general rule about where uncertainty lives, illustrated with the reverse-engineering case, then show the failure mode of stage-blind fan-out and what a stage-aware budget looks like in a harness config.

Source video ↗

The Lead Sets the Ceiling, Workers Set the Price

Model selection in agent systems should stop being a single global choice and become a per-role allocation: capability belongs where context is synthesized and decisions are made (decomposition, consolidation, judging), and cheap capacity belongs where work is parallel and independently checkable. The concrete claim — a frontier lead with cheap planners matches all-frontier teams at ~60% lower cost, and with fixed-size cheap workers at ~70% lower cost — is only safe if you accept the asymmetric risk: under-provisioning the lead caps the whole team regardless of worker count, so the saving is contingent on getting one role exactly right.

Angle

Cost engineering as role-level allocation, with the asymmetric-risk framing made explicit: workers are a price dial, the lead is a ceiling dial, and the two dials do not trade off symmetrically.

Source video ↗

Stop Building Peer-to-Peer Agent Protocols

Lateral coordination machinery in agent harnesses is mostly protocol surface area for a behavior models do not exhibit: 84% of messages route worker-to-lead with fewer than one worker-to-worker message per task, and enabling peer messaging did not raise reward while introducing noise and malformed messages. The stronger argument is architectural rather than empirical — the lead-as-hub topology is cheaper to build, easier to observe because all state flows through one consolidation point, and aligned with what agents actually do, so the burden of proof sits with anyone adding a shared blackboard or lateral consensus layer.

Angle

Contrarian engineering piece against the multi-agent framework orthodoxy, with the honest caveat that the result may be harness-induced and an explicit test that would falsify it.

Source video ↗

Benchmark Agents on Reward per Dollar, Not Reward

Raw benchmark scores monotonically reward spending, which means agent leaderboards systematically select for expensive configurations and hide the fact that most of the measured gain sits on the cheap end of the curve. Defining efficiency as the reward difference between a team and a solo agent divided by cost converts a score into a frontier, exposes saturation points where extra workers stop paying, and forces the honest comparison most teams skip: the same dollars spent on a stronger single model versus on decomposition.

Angle

Evaluation-practice critique with a concrete alternative metric and the argument that any config with configurable fan-out should be reported as a curve, not a point.

Project ideas

4

Stage-Budget Sweeper

beyond-evals

On repo-level SWE tasks with partially known specs (test-suite-driven bug fixing, feature work in a large existing repo), planning-only delegation retains at least 90% of full planning-implementation-verification reward at no more than 60% of the cost — but the reward-per-dollar advantage of planning workers falls below 1.3x, versus the ~1.6x reported on binary reverse engineering.

Proof of concept

A harness with per-stage worker count and per-stage model tier as explicit config, run over a fixed task suite in three families: binary reconstruction, test-driven bug fixing, and feature addition in a large repo. Sweep planning-only versus full PIV, and sweep worker count per stage.

Measurement

Reward-per-dollar curves per stage per task family; the ratio of planning-worker to implementation-worker marginal efficiency in each family; the cost at which full PIV first beats planning-only.

Source video ↗

Role-Tier Router with a Lead Floor

gatehouse

A frontier lead with the cheapest viable worker tier retains at least 95% of all-frontier team reward at 40% or less of the cost, and there exists a measurable worker-capability floor below which team reward drops below the lead's solo-agent reward — i.e., weak workers actively subtract value rather than merely failing to add it.

Proof of concept

Build role-level model configuration into the harness (separate lead and worker/planner model settings, per-role token accounting) and sweep a 2D grid of lead capability against worker capability on a fixed task set, fitting reward and cost surfaces.

Measurement

Reward and cost per grid cell; the location and sharpness of the reward cliff as worker tier degrades; the crossover point where team reward falls below lead-solo reward; per-role token spend share.

Source video ↗

Consolidation Fidelity Instrument

new

Information loss at the worker-to-lead consolidation boundary predicts final task failure better than worker output quality does, once worker quality is above a modest threshold — meaning the lead's consolidation step, not worker generation, becomes the dominant failure mode as team size grows.

Proof of concept

Instrument the worker-to-lead boundary to capture what each worker produced versus what the lead retained; perturb consolidation by capping summary length and by injecting controlled omissions in worker returns, then measure downstream reward sensitivity.

Measurement

Correlation between per-task retained-information ratio and final reward; reward degradation curve as summary compression increases; fraction of failures attributable to consolidation loss versus worker error under manual error analysis.

Source video ↗

Lateral Channel A/B

new

The observed near-absence of worker-to-worker messaging is harness-induced rather than model-induced: a harness that gives workers a typed, schema-validated lateral channel and explicitly rewards using it will produce materially more lateral traffic and equal-or-higher reward, whereas free-form peer chat reproduces the reported noise and malformed messages at no reward gain.

Proof of concept

A/B three harnesses on the same task suite: standard lead-hub with no lateral channel, free-form peer messaging, and typed peer messaging with schema validation and a reward/instruction that makes lateral coordination cheap and legible. Log full message topology.

Measurement

Message topology distribution (worker-to-lead versus worker-to-worker share); reward; rate of malformed or dropped messages; delta in reward per dollar between the three harnesses.

Architectural implications

4

The harness topology that emerges is lead-as-hub with fresh-context workers, not a peer network.

Before

Build a general multi-agent framework with peer-to-peer messaging, shared workspaces, and lateral negotiation protocols.

After

Build a lead orchestrator with a delegate tool and worker subprocesses that return compact summaries; do not implement worker-to-worker channels.

Consequence

Less protocol code, fewer malformed-message failure modes, and easier observability since all state flows through one consolidation point.

Source video ↗

Model tiering becomes a per-role runtime concern rather than a global configuration.

Before

Pick one model for the whole agent run.

After

Parameterize the harness with separate lead model and worker/planner model settings, defaulting to frontier lead + cheap workers.

Consequence

The harness needs routing/config plumbing at the role level, and cost accounting must be tracked per role to validate the efficiency metric.

Source video ↗

Planning is where fan-out is bought; implementation is where it is wasted.

Before

Scale all pipeline stages uniformly, or scale the code-writing stage because that is 'the work'.

After

Budget parallel exploration agents for discovery/planning, and keep implementation and verification worker counts small or fixed.

Consequence

Stage-aware scheduling: the harness must support different worker counts and models per stage, which is a scheduling problem rather than a single global fan-out parameter.

Source video ↗

Context management is achieved structurally by worker context isolation rather than by summarization inside one long thread.

Before

Rely on compaction/summarization within one agent's growing context to survive long tasks.

After

Push verbose tool output into short-lived worker contexts and pass only compact consolidated results back to the lead.

Consequence

Logs, traces, and diffs stop competing for lead context, extending viable task length without changing the underlying context window.
