Summary

Overview

  • Speaker: Sebastian Fox
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Evaluating and detecting silent failures in production clinical AI notes (ambient scribes)
  • Purpose: To explain why ambient scribe AI notes fail silently in clinical settings, why traditional evaluation methods fall short, and how a continuous context-engineering evaluation loop can catch high-stakes errors. Sebastian Fox, a medical doctor turned founder of Composo, discusses the hidden dangers of production AI-generated clinical notes (ambient scribes). Drawing from real-world studies, he highlights that ~1 in 20 notes carry severe errors capable of causing significant harm, yet they often look completely fine ("silent failures"). He argues that static upfront rubrics and fine-tuning weights are insufficient because "taste" (what matters in a specific context) is tacit, contextual, and moving. Instead, he proposes a three-step continuous loop: Discover failure modes from real outputs, Capture expert judgment, and Calibrate every output against that growing record.

Topic Map

The Problem: Silent Failures in Clinical AI

  • Explanation: Ambient scribes generate clinical notes that look completely fine on the surface but contain critical omissions or hallucinations, leading to severe medical risks.
  • Key claims:
    • Around 1 in 20 real AI-generated notes carry an error severe enough to cause significant harm if uncaught.
    • Nearly 1 in 5 had an important omission, and >1 in 10 had a hallucination.
    • The dangerous failures are the ones that look completely fine.
  • Examples:
    • A 72-year-old with tension headache missing a mention of jaw pain on chewing (giant cell arteritis, risking blindness).
    • A 20-year-old with a sore throat given chest pain, suspected angina, un-taken diabetes medications, and a non-existent hospital address, leading to unnecessary diabetes screening.
  • Terminology:
    • ambient scribes
    • silent failures
    • giant cell arteritis
    • hallucination
    • omission
  • Why it matters: In healthcare and high-stakes domains, being confidently wrong can be a matter of life and death, yet adoption of AI scribes is scaling rapidly without adverse event tracking.

Why Existing Evaluation Methods Fail

  • Explanation: Models are extraordinarily capable at generating fluent text and getting facts right, but they lack "taste" — the ability to determine what matters in a specific context.
  • Key claims:
    • LLMs add, change, or omit information during transcription and generation.
    • Blatant errors are easy to catch; subtle over-interpretations, harmless rephrasings, and meaningful flips are hard.
    • Taste is tacit, contextual, and moving (guidelines change, doctors disagree).
  • Examples:
    • Humalog vs Humulin insulin confusion.
    • Hyperthyroidism vs hypothyroidism.
    • Dropped 'no' changing 'no evidence of cancer' to 'evidence of cancer'.
    • Two patients with blood in urine: one coming from France (irrelevant), one from Lake Malawi (schistosomiasis diagnosis).
  • Terminology:
    • taste
    • tacit
    • contextual
    • moving
    • transcription layer
  • Why it matters: Relying on off-the-shelf judges or static rubrics only catches 60-70% of errors because they lack domain-specific taste.

The Solution: A Continuous Evaluation Loop

  • Explanation: Instead of static upfront rubrics or static fine-tuned weights, build a continuous three-step loop: Discover, Capture, Calibrate.
  • Key claims:
    • Evaluation is asymmetrical: verification is easier than generation, but knowing what matters requires contextual taste.
    • Baking rubrics into weights goes stale and cannot explain why.
    • The easiest place to start is having experts leave free-form comments on real outputs.
  • Examples:
    • Comparing off-the-shelf judge (60%), engineered judge (70%), and calibrated expert-loop judge (85%+ agreement with peak human expert).
    • Using memories of judged cases, expert corrections, and relevant docs/guidelines assembled per output.
  • Terminology:
    • context engineering
    • failure-mode ontology
    • judging agent
    • calibration
  • Why it matters: Evaluation isn't something you have; it's something you do continuously as failure modes evolve.

Key Points

Scale of AI Scribe Adoption vs. Lack of Tracking

  • Explanation: Ambient scribes are in about a third of US practices and growing rapidly, yet their errors are barely tracked and rarely reported as formal incidents.
  • Evidence: ~1 in 20 real AI notes contain severe errors in production datasets.
  • Practical implication: Healthcare and other high-stakes domains are flying blind regarding silent AI failures.

The Three Ways Models Fail on Transcripts

  • Explanation: Even with perfect transcripts, models add, change, or omit information.
  • Evidence: Examples include misheard drug names (Humalog/Humulin), flipped negation ('no evidence' to 'evidence'), and missed critical clinical context.
  • Practical implication: Perfect transcription does not equal correct medical documentation.

Why Weights and Upfront Rubrics Fail for Evaluation

  • Explanation: Upfront rubrics miss unforeseen failure modes, and fine-tuning model weights goes stale and lacks explainability.
  • Evidence: Static benchmarks and prompts capture only superficial correctness rather than contextual importance.
  • Practical implication: Evaluators must be dynamic and driven by real-world expert feedback.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

The Three-Step Evaluation Loop

  • How it works: A continuous loop that discovers failures from real outputs, captures expert judgment, and calibrates every output against the accumulated record.
  • Components:
    • 1 - Discover: Find failure modes from real outputs (build failure-mode ontology from data, not guesswork).
    • 2 - Capture: Capture expert judgment on real outputs (free-form comments, reasons, corrections).
    • 3 - Calibrate: Calibrate every output against retrieved memories of similar judged cases, expert corrections, and guidelines.
  • When to use: When building AI evaluation systems for high-stakes domains where standard rubrics and static prompts fail to catch subtle errors.

Examples & Case Studies

Actionable Takeaways

  • Immediate:
    • Stop relying solely on static upfront rubrics and fine-tuned weights for evaluation.
    • Start capturing free-form expert comments on real production outputs.
    • Build a failure-mode ontology derived from real data rather than whiteboard guesses.
  • Strategic:
    • Treat evaluation as a continuous process ('something you do'), not a static asset ('something you have').
    • Employ context engineering by retrieving relevant memories of past judged cases, expert corrections, and guidelines for each evaluation.
    • Recognize that verification is asymmetrical to generation and requires specialized judging agents.
  • Questions to investigate:
    • How do we scale expert feedback collection without overwhelming clinicians?
    • What is the optimal retrieval mechanism for surfacing similar historical judged cases?
    • How can this evaluation loop be adapted to non-medical high-stakes domains like legal contracts or financial advice?

Compressed Summary

  • Ambient scribe AI notes suffer from silent failures where ~1 in 20 notes contain severe, potentially harmful errors.
  • Models fail by adding, changing, or omitting critical information even when transcription is accurate.
  • Taste (knowing what matters) is tacit, contextual, and moving, making static rubrics and fine-tuned weights inadequate.
  • A three-step continuous loop (Discover, Capture, Calibrate) using context engineering outperforms static evaluation.
  • Keywords: clinical ai, ambient scribes, evaluation, context engineering, failure modes

Core insights

6
[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltystrong evidence

Judge quality is primarily a context-assembly problem, not a model-selection problem: an off-the-shelf judge reached ~60% agreement with a peak human expert, an engineered judge ~70%, and a 'calibrated expert-loop' judge that retrieves memories of previously judged cases, expert corrections, and relevant guidelines per output reached 85%+ agreement.

Why it matters

It reframes the evaluator as a retrieval-and-context-engineering subsystem with a persistent, growing case memory, rather than a prompt or a stronger model. The largest accuracy gain came from curating and retrieving judgment history per output, which is an operational data problem rather than a modeling problem.

Generalization

Any LLM-as-judge pipeline in a domain with expert-defined correctness should treat the judge as a context-assembled agent over a curated memory of past adjudications, and expect the biggest gains to come from that memory layer.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

'Taste' — determining what matters in a specific clinical context — is tacit, contextual, and moving (guidelines change, doctors disagree), so it cannot be fully specified in an upfront rubric nor baked into fine-tuned weights. Any evaluation system built as a static artifact goes stale; it must be a continuously executed loop (Discover → Capture → Calibrate).

Why it matters

This is an argument for treating evaluation as an operational service with a runtime and a feedback ingestion path, not a deploy-time configuration file. It also explains why static benchmarks systematically overstate reliability in high-stakes domains.

Generalization

Domains where the ground truth is expert tacit judgment (medicine, law, finance, security triage) should architect evaluation as a living system whose specification evolves from production data, with the rubric as an output of the loop rather than its input.

[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Identical structural omissions can carry radically different clinical meaning depending on context: two notes both reading '...a recent holiday to...' — France (irrelevant) versus Lake Malawi (schistosomiasis) — require opposite judgments. Relevance, not presence/absence of facts, is the unit of evaluation.

Why it matters

It explains mechanically why generic rubrics and fact-checking-style judges fail: they score on content presence rather than on contextual salience. It argues for per-output retrieval of domain context (patient-specific, epidemiological, guideline) as a precondition for correct scoring.

Generalization

For any evaluator over free-text generation, relevance judgments must be grounded in retrieved case-specific context; boolean or checklist scoring will systematically miss the highest-stakes errors.

[!]Failure Modemedium noveltystrong evidence

Clinical AI scribe failures are silent and distributed across a large tail: ~1 in 20 production notes carry an error severe enough to cause significant harm, nearly 1 in 5 contain an important omission, and >1 in 10 contain a hallucination — yet the dangerous ones read as completely fine, while blatant errors are the ones reviewers catch. Notably these failures occur even with perfect transcripts: the model adds, changes, or omits information at the interpretation layer.

Why it matters

It separates the transcription layer from the documentation/interpretation layer as distinct failure domains, so transcript-accuracy metrics give false assurance. It also sets a base rate: a 5% severe-harm rate means small-sample spot checks and aggregate quality scores are useless for detection.

Generalization

High-stakes generative systems need continuous sampling of a large output population plus salience-aware review, since the failures that matter are rare, plausible-looking, and invisible to fluency or factuality heuristics.

Source video ↗
[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

The cheapest bootstrap for an evaluation system is unstructured free-form expert comments on real production outputs, and the failure taxonomy should be derived bottom-up from those outputs rather than hypothesized in advance. This flips the usual order: ontology emerges from data, and the rubric is a compiled artifact of accumulated judgments.

Why it matters

It gives a concrete low-friction starting point that doesn't require expert time on rubric design (a task experts are bad at) and avoids inventing failure modes that never occur, while ensuring the taxonomy covers what production actually emits.

Generalization

Failure-mode ontologies for any agentic system should be mined from production traces with lightweight human annotations, then versioned, rather than authored up-front from anticipated risks.

Source video ↗
[~]Tradeoffmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Fine-tuning evaluation criteria into model weights trades away two things at once: freshness (weights go stale as guidelines and expert consensus shift) and explainability (the model cannot articulate why an output is wrong, which is required if the eval is to drive correction or review).

Why it matters

It draws a boundary: implicit, weight-encoded judgment is acceptable for generation, but evaluation in auditable domains needs explicit, inspectable rationale. That favors inference-time context injection over weight updates for the judging role.

Generalization

Separate systems that must justify decisions (audit, review, compliance) from systems that merely produce artifacts; keep the former's criteria in retrievable, versionable context rather than in parameters.

Deep dives

4

Memory curation policies for calibrated judges

Research question

As a judge's case memory grows to thousands of accumulated adjudications and expert corrections, what retrieval policy (recency decay, expert-authority weighting, contradiction detection, periodic re-audit) keeps agreement with expert consensus stable rather than drifting toward its own prior judgments?

Why

The 60% → 70% → 85%+ progression is attributed to retrieving memories of judged cases and corrections, but an unmanaged memory store is a self-reinforcing loop: the judge retrieves its own past verdicts as precedent. Without an explicit curation policy, the component that produced the largest accuracy gain becomes the largest silent-failure surface, in a domain where errors are already invisible.

Source video ↗

The inter-rater ceiling problem in judge benchmarking

Research question

When expert clinicians disagree with each other on production notes, what is the pairwise human agreement ceiling, and does an 85% agreement figure against a single 'peak' expert sit near that ceiling or well below it?

Why

Every claim about judge quality in this talk is denominated in agreement with one annotator. If experts agree with each other at 80%, a judge at 85% is at or above human parity and further modeling work is misallocated; if experts agree at 95%, the residual 15% is a large untouched error mass. Without the ceiling, the headline number cannot be interpreted, and neither can the 60-70% baseline it is compared to.

Source video ↗

Salience-grounded evaluation: relevance as the scoring unit

Research question

Does injecting retrieved patient-specific and epidemiological context into an evaluator measurably shift its salience judgments toward expert consensus on structurally identical passages whose clinical meaning differs?

Why

The Lake Malawi versus France example is a minimal pair that isolates the variable: same surface content, opposite correct verdicts. It converts an abstract claim about 'taste' into a testable design constraint — that the evaluator must retrieve case-specific context before scoring — and explains mechanically why checklist and fact-presence judges miss the highest-stakes errors rather than merely underperforming on average.

Source video ↗

From judge agreement to measured harm reduction

Research question

Does raising judge agreement from 60% to 85%+ against an expert reviewer increase the number of harm-severe errors detected per unit of expert review, and specifically improve recall in the rare severe-error stratum where the base rate is ~1 in 20?

Why

Agreement on sampled outputs is a proxy metric; the operational claim being made is that the loop catches dangerous notes. Aggregate agreement can improve while severe-stratum recall stays flat, because severe errors are rare and plausibility-correlated. Instrumenting detection-per-review-hour and severe-stratum recall turns a quality score into a claim that can be falsified and that a clinical operator can act on.

Article ideas

4

Your LLM Judge Is Not Weak, It Is Starving for Context

The 60% → 70% → 85%+ progression from off-the-shelf to engineered to calibrated judges is not a model-selection story; the largest single gain came from per-output retrieval of prior adjudications and expert corrections, which means judge quality is an operational data problem and teams that respond by upgrading the base model are optimizing the wrong variable.

Angle

Decompose the three judge configurations by what changed architecturally rather than which model was used, then argue that every team building LLM-as-judge should budget for a case-memory store before it budgets for a better model.

Source video ↗

Evaluation Is a Runtime, Not a Rubric

Because taste is tacit, contextual and moving, any evaluation system shipped as a static artifact — an up-front rubric, a fine-tuned judge — is stale on arrival; evaluation has to be an executed loop whose rubric is an output compiled from real failures and expert comments, with a runtime, a feedback ingestion path, and an owner.

Angle

Contrast deploy-time evaluation configuration with runtime evaluation, and show that the artifact ordering flip (ontology emerges from production outputs, rubric compiles from judgments) is what makes the system stay correct as guidelines shift.

Source video ↗

Perfect Transcripts, Broken Notes: The Layer Nobody Instrumented

The 1-in-20 severe-harm rate persists even when transcription is flawless, because the model adds, changes and omits information at the interpretation layer — so transcript-accuracy dashboards are giving false assurance, and the documentation stage needs its own error budget and its own salience-aware instrumentation.

Angle

Separate the pipeline into transcription and documentation as explicit architectural seams, and use the dropped-'no' and Humalog/Humulin cases to show why fluency and factuality heuristics cannot see interpretation-layer failures.

Source video ↗

You Cannot Fine-Tune Taste Into a Judge in an Auditable Domain

Encoding evaluation criteria in weights buys inference-time cheapness at the cost of freshness and explainability simultaneously, and in domains where a judgment must be justified to a reviewer, regulator or clinician, a judge that cannot say why it was wrong cannot drive correction — so criteria belong in retrievable, versioned context rather than in parameters.

Angle

Frame it as a boundary between generation systems (implicit judgment acceptable) and decision-justifying systems (explicit rationale required), and show that this boundary, not accuracy, decides the architecture.

Project ideas

4

Case-Memory Judge Harness

beyond-evals

On a held-out set of production outputs containing known harm-severe errors, a judge that retrieves curated prior adjudications and expert corrections per output achieves materially higher agreement with expert reviewers than an identically-prompted judge with no memory, and the gain concentrates in the subtle-error stratum (rephrasings, meaningful flips, contextual over-interpretation) rather than in blatant errors.

Proof of concept

Build three configurations over the same model: (a) generic rubric prompt, (b) hand-engineered detailed rubric, (c) rubric plus top-k retrieval over a growing store of judged cases, expert corrections and guidelines. Stratify the eval set by error subtlety and run all three against the same reviewers.

Measurement

Agreement with expert reviewers per configuration, reported overall and stratified by subtle-versus-blatant errors; the hypothesis fails if (c) does not beat (b) on the subtle stratum by a margin larger than the reviewer-to-reviewer disagreement band.

Source video ↗

Bottom-Up Failure Ontology Miner

gatehouse

A failure taxonomy mined by clustering free-form expert comments on real production outputs covers the failure modes that actually cause severe errors better than an up-front taxonomy authored from anticipated risks, leaving fewer severe errors unclassified on held-out outputs.

Proof of concept

Take a batch of production outputs with lightweight free-form expert comments, cluster comments into an ontology, then classify a disjoint held-out set of adjudicated errors under both the mined and the up-front taxonomies. Report the fraction of mined categories absent from the up-front list.

Measurement

Severe-error classification coverage and unclassified-error rate on held-out outputs under each taxonomy; success requires the mined ontology to reduce the unclassified severe-error rate, and the measurement also quantifies how many data-derived modes were invisible to up-front design.

Source video ↗

Expert-Time Budget Allocator

new

A small expert seed corpus plus targeted human audit restricted to low-confidence or high-salience outputs preserves severe-error recall while consuming substantially less expert time than uniform review, and recall degrades predictably once the audit allocation drops below a measurable threshold.

Proof of concept

Simulate a review queue: seed the judge with a fixed expert-annotated corpus, have it score an output stream with confidence, and route only the flagged subset to reviewers. Sweep the audit allocation fraction and plot severe-error recall against expert hours consumed.

Measurement

Severe-error recall per expert review hour across allocation fractions, compared against uniform review; the hypothesis fails if recall collapses before expert time is materially reduced, which would indicate the loop cannot port to domains with sparse experts.

Source video ↗

Severe-Stratum Drift Monitor

beyond-evals

An unmanaged case memory degrades: over successive self-feeding iterations, judge agreement with fresh expert adjudications on newly arriving outputs declines relative to a memory-pruned and recency-weighted variant, with the decline first appearing in the rare severe-error stratum.

Proof of concept

Run the calibrated judge over a streamed output population in two arms — unmanaged accumulating memory versus a policy with recency decay, expert-authority weighting and periodic re-audit against expert ground truth — and sample fresh expert adjudications at each interval.

Measurement

Agreement with fresh expert adjudications over iterations for both arms, plus severe-stratum recall; the hypothesis is supported if the unmanaged arm degrades measurably while the managed arm stays flat within the inter-rater band.

Architectural implications

3

The judging agent's competence comes from per-output context assembly (similar judged cases, expert corrections, guidelines), not from a fixed prompt or a stronger base model.

Before

Evaluation configured as a static prompt/rubric, or a fine-tuned judge model, deployed once and re-run unchanged.

After

Evaluation as a runtime that, for each generated output, retrieves relevant prior adjudications and domain documents and composes them into the judging context.

Consequence

Introduces a case-memory store, a retrieval/ranking layer, and versioned judgment records as first-class infrastructure; evaluation cost and latency become per-output inference plus retrieval, and memory curation becomes an ongoing operational owner.

Source video ↗

Rubrics are an output of the loop (compiled from real failures and expert comments) rather than an input authored in advance.

Before

Subject-matter experts author failure taxonomy and scoring rubric before seeing production outputs.

After

Production outputs are reviewed with free-form comments; failure modes are mined and clustered into an ontology that generates the rubric and the judge's criteria.

Consequence

Shifts expert time from speculative rubric design to concrete adjudication, and makes the failure taxonomy a versioned, data-derived artifact that can be audited against its evidence.

Source video ↗

Perfect transcription is explicitly not the quality boundary; errors accumulate in the interpretation/documentation layer.

Before

Pipeline quality attributed primarily to the ASR/transcription stage; transcript accuracy used as the reliability proxy.

After

The generation stage treated as its own failure surface with its own error budget (additions, changes, omissions, context-driven over-interpretation) requiring separate instrumentation.

Consequence

Transcript-accuracy dashboards must be supplemented with salience-aware evaluation on the generated artifact, and the boundary between transcription and documentation becomes an explicit architectural seam.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

5

Calibrated judge agreement ceiling

Benefit

Retrieval-augmented, expert-loop judging reaches 85%+ agreement with a peak human expert, far above off-the-shelf and engineered judges.

Cost or risk

Residual 15% disagreement against a single 'peak' expert remains, and the benchmark itself is one expert's judgment in a domain where 'doctors disagree' — so the judge can still silently miss the class of errors that matters most.

Source video ↗

Expert-in-the-loop data capture

Benefit

Free-form comments on real outputs are the lowest-friction, highest-signal way to start the loop and avoid inventing nonexistent failure modes.

Cost or risk

Requires scarce expert time continuously, produces unstructured output that needs clustering into an ontology, and makes the eval throughput bounded by human review capacity.

Source video ↗

Weight-encoded versus context-encoded criteria

Benefit

Baking rubrics into weights makes judgment cheap and low-latency at inference.

Cost or risk

The judgment goes stale as guidelines change and provides no rationale, so errors cannot be explained or acted on in a review workflow.

Source video ↗

Relying on off-the-shelf judges

Benefit

Immediate deployment of an evaluation signal with no domain data collection or expert involvement.

Cost or risk

Catches only 60-70% of errors because the judge lacks domain-specific taste, giving false assurance at exactly the base rate (1 in 20 severe) where unseen misses matter most.

Rapid adoption without adverse-event tracking

Benefit

Ambient scribes are already in about a third of US practices, delivering documentation leverage at scale.

Cost or risk

Severe errors are barely tracked and rarely reported as formal incidents, so the deployment population is a blind spot rather than a measurement surface.

Open questions

4

How does a calibrated, memory-retrieval judge avoid drifting or overfitting to its own accumulated judgments as that memory grows and evolves?

Why unresolved

The summary describes accumulating judged cases, corrections, and guidelines into memory and retrieving per output, but gives no mechanism for pruning, weighting recency, detecting contradictory precedents, or re-auditing memory against expert ground truth.

Research direction

Build retrieval policies with explicit decay/authority weighting over case memory; measure judge agreement under memory ablation and injection of stale or contradictory precedents.

What is the human agreement ceiling that a calibrated judge can be measured against, when experts themselves disagree?

Why unresolved

The benchmark reported is agreement with a single 'peak human expert', while the summary states that doctors disagree and taste is moving — the inter-rater ceiling is not established, so 85% may be near-ceiling or far from it.

Research direction

Measure pairwise expert agreement on the same production outputs to establish an inter-rater ceiling, then express judge performance relative to that ceiling rather than to one annotator.

Source video ↗

Do judge-agreement gains (60% → 85%) actually translate into reduced harm in production, and how would that be measured?

Why unresolved

The evidence presented is agreement with an expert reviewer on sampled outputs; no outcome data linking judge agreement to caught-and-prevented severe errors or to clinical outcomes is described.

Research direction

Instrument the production loop to track severe errors detected per unit review, recall at the severe-error stratum, and downstream incident rates before and after introducing the calibrated judge.

Source video ↗

How does the Discover-Capture-Calibrate loop port to high-stakes domains without a dense supply of expert reviewers?

Why unresolved

The loop's core input is scarce expert adjudication; the summary does not address domains where experts are unavailable, expensive, or where the relevant taste is distributed across many practitioners.

Research direction

Test whether a small expert seed corpus can bootstrap the judge and then self-label with targeted human audit only on low-confidence or high-salience outputs, measuring how much expert time can be removed before recall degrades.
