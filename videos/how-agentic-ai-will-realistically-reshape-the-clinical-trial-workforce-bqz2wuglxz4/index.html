Overview

  • Speaker: Krishna Cheriath, VP, head of clinical research digital data and AI, Thermo Fisher Scientific
  • Channel: Applied Clinical Trials
  • Main topic: The integration and workforce impact of agentic AI in clinical trials
  • Purpose: Provide realistic insights into how agentic AI will transform clinical trial execution, workforce structures, and operational roles over the next few years. Krishna Cheriath, VP and head of clinical research digital data and AI at Thermo Fisher Scientific, discusses how agentic AI is poised to realistically reshape clinical trial design and execution. He outlines a four-level framework for AI augmentation (AL0 to AL4) and explains how clinical trial roles will evolve from human-centric to AI-augmented over the next few years.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

AI Augmentation Levels (AL0 - AL4)

  • How it works: Classifies the division of labor between humans and AI across four distinct operational tiers.
  • Components:
    • AL0: Current state where work is predominantly done by humans with tools and assistance.
    • AL1: Combination of human and AI assistance where human verifies and validates every output.
    • AL2: Majority of work done by AI, with human validating outputs and acting as verifier/certifier.
    • AL3: AI does the bulk of work while humans perform auditing, sampling, and quality control.
    • AL4: Fully autonomous AI agents executing tasks independently.
  • When to use: Evaluating clinical trial tasks, operational workflows, and automation readiness.

Core insights

5
[O]Mental Modelmedium noveltystrong evidence

The AL0-AL4 framework is organized around one axis: who verifies the output and at what granularity. AL1 = human validates every output, AL2 = human certifies/validates AI output, AL3 = human performs auditing, sampling, and quality control, AL4 = no human. The level is a property of the workflow's verification topology, not of the model's capability.

Why it matters

Gives engineers a capability-independent way to classify and design agent workflows: instead of asking 'is the model good enough', ask 'where in the pipeline does verification live and at what sampling rate'. That reframes autonomy as an oversight-architecture decision, which is directly implementable as different runtime topologies (inline gate vs. out-of-band auditor).

Generalization

Any agentic system operating under audit or safety constraints can be positioned on a verifier-granularity axis (per-output gate -> certification -> statistical sampling -> none), independent of the underlying model's raw capability.

[M]Mechanismmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Human oversight degrades predictably in granularity as agent volume rises: 100% verification is replaced by sampling and auditing. The binding constraint is verification bandwidth, not model quality.

Why it matters

Implies that scaling agent throughput forces a separate QA subsystem (sampling policy, statistical acceptance criteria, audit artifacts) rather than simply adding more human reviewers. The pipeline must emit machine-consumable evidence so that sampled outputs can be audited after the fact.

Generalization

Any human-in-the-loop system under volume pressure converges on statistical sampling; this requires the runtime to persist auditable per-step provenance, otherwise sampling is impossible to perform retrospectively.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

The AL levels are applied at the task/workflow level, while the workforce claim is made at the role level ('all clinical trial roles will be AI-augmented'). Roles decompose into many tasks, each of which may sit at a different AL level, so role-level autonomy is a derived aggregate rather than a single setting.

Why it matters

Forces explicit task decomposition before any autonomy assignment; a system or org that assigns a single autonomy level per role/agent will misplace verification effort. The correct unit of analysis is the task boundary inside the workflow.

Generalization

Autonomy configuration in agentic systems should be attached to task nodes in a workflow graph, not to agents or job titles; agent-level autonomy is an emergent summary of its task mix.

Task-level autonomy topology for agentic clinical workflows

Research question

How should clinical trial roles be decomposed into task nodes, and how should each node be assigned an AL level, verifier, and audit artifact schema based on its verification topology?

Why

Role-level autonomy claims obscure the fact that roles decompose into tasks with different verification needs. Assigning one autonomy setting per role or agent misplaces human review effort and makes governance imprecise. A task-node map is the minimum unit for implementable autonomy policy.

Statistical sampling for AL3 oversight

Research question

What sampling rates and statistical acceptance criteria bound residual defect probability when AI produces the bulk of clinical trial work?

Why

As agent volume rises, 100% verification is replaced by sampling and auditing. That shift turns oversight into a statistical QA problem requiring provenance, defect-rate assumptions, sampling plans, and acceptance thresholds. Without this, AL3 is an aspiration rather than an auditable operating mode.

Architectural implications

3

Verification responsibility moves out of the inline execution path as autonomy increases.

Before

Human reviewer sits inside the pipeline as a blocking per-output gate (AL1).

After

Human or human-defined policy runs as an out-of-band auditing and sampling function over a stream of AI-produced artifacts (AL3).

Consequence

The runtime must emit structured, retained artifacts and provenance for every unit of work so that sampling can occur after execution; non-blocking QA changes latency and cost profiles and requires a sampling policy with acceptance thresholds.

Source video ↗

Autonomy is assigned per task node rather than per agent or per role.

Before

A single autonomy setting or model configuration governs an entire agent or job function.

After

Each task node in the workflow carries its own AL level, verifier, and guardrail set.

Consequence

Orchestration layers need per-node policy metadata and routing; evaluation must be reported per task node, not per agent.

Source video ↗

Agent fleet management gains an HR-like control plane.

Before

Agents are deployed as static configurations with fixed prompts, tools, and permissions.

After

Agents occupy tiers with defined supervision levels and explicit progression criteria, with tier-appropriate guardrails.

Consequence

Requires measurement of agent reliability per tier as a promotion gate, plus the ability to demote or quarantine an agent after regression — i.e., continuous evaluation wired into deployment rather than one-time validation.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

3

Sampling-based oversight vs. residual defect risk

Benefit

Human effort scales sublinearly with AI output volume, enabling throughput gains.

Cost or risk

Switching from 100% verification to sampling means defects that fall outside the sample go undetected; the guarantee becomes statistical, not absolute, which is a weaker safety posture for patient-facing data.

Source video ↗

Autonomous execution vs. regulatory compliance and patient trust

Benefit

Higher AL levels accelerate scientific progression and reduce cycle time.

Cost or risk

Clinical trials operate on patient-centric data, so autonomy must be constrained by governance, security, and compliance; the acceleration benefit is capped by the auditability of each automated step.

Source video ↗

Workforce analogy applied to agents

Benefit

A familiar mental model (entry/mid/expert) gives leaders a concrete way to plan agent rollout and guardrails.

Cost or risk

The analogy imports assumptions from human career development that may not hold for software artifacts (e.g., that capability increases monotonically with tenure, or that tiers are stable over time), risking misplaced trust or over-engineering of agent 'progression'.
