Overview

  • Speaker: Sam Green & Aaron Linsky
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Building 'The Vault' data platform at Bridgewater Associates to turn unstructured data into a first-class asset for humans and AI agents.
  • Purpose: To explain the technical design, product engineering takeaways, and strategic impact of building an enterprise RAG and unstructured data platform at scale for investment professionals and AI agents. Sam Green and Aaron Linsky from Bridgewater Associates discuss 'The Vault,' an enterprise data platform storing over 100 million documents from 135 external sources plus internal communications, research, and transcripts. They explore the evolution from structured time-series models to language models with unstructured content, emphasizing that humans and AI agents have very different needs regarding recall, precision, filtering, entitlements, and scale. They share real-world engineering hurdles—such as search precision, prefiltering vs. postfiltering vs. native filtering (TurboPuffer)—and how they moved users from email workflows to the Investor Workbench where AI acts as a proactive participant.

Core insights

7
[M]Mechanismhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Vector search filtering is a three-way architectural choice, not two: postfiltering (fast, low recall), prefiltering (100% recall, slow), and native filtering where the index searches only the nearest clusters that contain metadata matches — yielding fast speed at roughly 95% recall.

Why it matters

Filtering placement determines both latency and recall for every retrieval call; teams that only know pre/post filtering are forced into an unnecessary speed-vs-recall tradeoff, and the recall loss from postfiltering silently corrupts agent reasoning (the agent never sees the globally relevant doc that failed the metadata filter).

Generalization

In any ANN index with hard constraints (tenant, date, entitlement, entity), the correct boundary is pushing predicate evaluation into index traversal rather than filtering in the application layer — evaluate vendors on native filter support, not just raw QPS.

[E]Empirical Resultmedium noveltymoderate evidence

Retrieval quality was reconstructed as a compound stack, not a single lever: precision@10 started around 60% and reached 90–95% only after fixing ingestion issues and then adding reciprocal rank fusion, rerankers, query expansion, and agentic search.

Why it matters

Suggests that ingestion/data-quality defects are a first-order precision problem that reranking cannot fix — and that hybrid fusion plus reranking plus query expansion plus agentic iteration is cumulative rather than substitutable. An ablation is the only way to know which component is paying for itself.

Generalization

For any RAG system, instrument precision@k as a regression metric and treat pipeline stages (ingestion, fusion, rerank, expansion, agentic loop) as separately measurable contributors; expect ingestion hygiene to dominate before any model-level tuning helps.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltymoderate evidence

Humans and agents are two consumer classes with inverted retrieval objectives: humans are precision-sensitive, low-volume (~300 words/min) readers; agents are recall-sensitive, high-volume (30,000+ words/min) consumers that need performant bulk access at scale.

Why it matters

This reframes the platform requirement: instead of one ranked list, you need two access paths with different SLOs — a top-k, high-precision surface for humans and a high-recall, high-throughput, filterable surface for agents. Optimizing one metric for both consumers yields a system that is mediocre for each.

Generalization

Design retrieval APIs with an explicit consumer profile (precision-first vs recall-first, interactive vs batch) and expose depth/filter/throughput parameters rather than a single default. Applies to agent harnesses, memory stores, and tool design generally.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Unstructured content was made retrievable only after being enriched with ontology metadata (country, concept, market, time period) at ingestion, so that filtered retrieval can be expressed in domain terms rather than in embedding similarity alone.

Why it matters

Native/structured filtering presupposes the metadata exists and is consistent; the metadata schema layer, not the vector store, is the actual gating dependency for both precision and entitlement enforcement. Query examples in the talk are compound (as-of date + source + currencies), which is impossible to serve well from embeddings alone.

Generalization

Treat metadata extraction/ontology alignment as a first-class ingestion stage with its own quality metrics; a retrieval system's filter expressiveness is bounded by the entity schema it populates at write time.

[A]Architecturemedium noveltyweak evidence

Entitlements are treated as one of the five core search dimensions alongside recall, precision, filtering, and scale — i.e., permission-aware retrieval is a retrieval-time property, not an application-layer check.

Why it matters

If entitlements are not part of index traversal, you either leak restricted content into context windows or post-filter away your recall. Making permissions a filter dimension forces them into the same native-filtering machinery, which changes index partitioning and query planning decisions.

Generalization

In multi-tenant or compliance-constrained agent systems, permission predicates should be compiled into the same query plan as semantic search, and evaluated as a hard constraint during collection selection.

[>]Predictionmedium noveltyweak evidence

The stated end-state is to move agents from reactive query-answerers to proactive participants: a workbench that continuously tracks what matters to a given investor and surfaces insights unprompted, including feeding systematic forecasting and signal generation.

Why it matters

Shifts the agent runtime from request/response to a persistent per-consumer interest model plus background evaluation loop — which introduces entirely different engineering concerns (state per user, scheduling, deduplication of alerts, cost of continuous retrieval) than an on-demand RAG endpoint.

Generalization

Proactive agents require an explicit, inspectable 'what this user cares about' model as an architectural component; without it, proactivity degrades into notification noise, and the evaluation target becomes precision of interruption rather than answer quality.

[P]Practicemedium noveltymoderate evidence

Adoption was engineered by mapping existing user habits onto new primitives — subscribed broker lists became saved searches, email folders became saved collections — because dropping a superior tool onto a desk does not produce use.

Why it matters

For internal agent products, the migration path is a technical design constraint: the new system must expose primitives isomorphic to the old workflow (saved queries, collections, subscriptions) or usage never materializes regardless of retrieval quality.

Generalization

Before launching an internal agent tool, inventory the incumbent workflow artifacts (folders, subscriptions, saved queries, spreadsheets) and ship 1:1 equivalents plus an import path; measure activation as migration of existing artifacts, not feature usage.

Filter selectivity regimes for native vector filtering

Research question

How does native metadata filtering recall degrade as predicate selectivity ranges from 0.001% to 50%, and where does it beat prefiltering or postfiltering on p99 latency?

Why

A single claimed ~95% recall number is insufficient for architecture decisions; if native filtering degrades on rare-entity or narrow-date predicates, teams may silently lose the documents agents most need.

Architectural implications

4

Metadata filtering moves inside the vector index rather than being applied after retrieval or by brute-force pre-scanning candidates.

Before

Retrieve top-k vectors globally, then discard results failing metadata predicates in the application layer (or force a full scan under the predicate).

After

Push predicates into index traversal so the search descends only into clusters containing qualifying records, preserving recall and latency simultaneously.

Consequence

Vector store selection becomes a capability decision (native filtering support) rather than a price/QPS decision; the application layer shrinks to query construction and fusion.

Source video ↗

Ingestion becomes a quality-critical subsystem rather than a plumbing concern.

Before

Treat chunking/parsing/metadata tagging as an upstream data-engineering detail; attribute poor search results to the embedding model or the LLM.

After

Fix ingestion issues first, then layer reciprocal rank fusion, rerankers, query expansion, and agentic search on top.

Consequence

Budget and headcount shift toward parsing, dedup, and metadata extraction, with precision@k measured continuously across the pipeline.

Source video ↗

The retrieval surface splits into a human-facing precision path and an agent-facing recall/throughput path over one shared index.

Before

A single search endpoint with one default depth and ranking tuned to human reading speed.

After

Parameterized retrieval: shallow, heavily reranked, high-signal results for humans; deep, filterable, high-throughput access for agents consuming orders of magnitude more text.

Consequence

Ranking models, caching, and latency SLOs must be specified per consumer class; observability needs to separate agent-driven and human-driven traffic.

Source video ↗

The agent runtime gains a persistent per-user interest model and a background loop for proactive surfacing.

Before

Agent is invoked by a user prompt and terminates after producing a response.

After

Agent continuously evaluates new ingested documents against a tracked model of each investor's priorities and emits unsolicited insights into a workbench.

Consequence

New requirements appear: state management per user, scheduling and cost control for continuous retrieval, dedup/novelty detection for surfaced items, and evaluation of interruption quality.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Postfiltering vs prefiltering vs native filtering

Benefit

Postfiltering is the cheapest to implement and fastest per query; prefiltering guarantees 100% recall; native filtering is claimed to achieve both fast speed and ~95% recall.

Cost or risk

Postfiltering silently loses globally relevant documents that fail metadata predicates; prefiltering can be very slow at corpus scale; native filtering depends on index-level support and accepts an approximate (~95%) rather than exact recall ceiling.

Source video ↗

Serving humans and agents from one platform

Benefit

A single tool meeting both consumer classes produces the greatest impact per the speakers, avoiding duplicated pipelines and divergent indexes.

Cost or risk

The two consumer classes have conflicting requirements (precision vs recall, interactive vs bulk throughput), so shared defaults will under-serve at least one of them.

Source video ↗

Build the platform in-house vs vendor adoption

Benefit

Bridgewater gained the breadth and modeling control needed to turn unstructured data into a differentiator.

Cost or risk

Building and maintaining ingestion, ontology, filtering, and search quality in-house is a large, ongoing engineering commitment before any user value appears.

Source video ↗

Preserving legacy user workflows

Benefit

Mapping email folders and subscribed broker lists to saved collections and saved searches drives adoption of the new platform.

Cost or risk

Designing around incumbent habits constrains the new product's primitives and can carry forward concepts that do not fit agent-first interaction.
