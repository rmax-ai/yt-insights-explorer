Overview

  • Speaker: Rashi Agrawal
  • Channel: AI Engineer
  • Main topic: Guardrails and decisioning frameworks for building member-facing health AI systems
  • Purpose: To provide practitioners with concrete architectural patterns and decision-making frameworks for deploying member-facing healthcare AI safely and compliantly. Rashi Agrawal, Head of AI and ML at Hinge Health, discusses the critical guardrails and architectural principles needed to build safe, member-facing healthcare AI. Drawing on real-world industry incidents like AI chatbots giving dangerous medical advice, Agrawal presents a three-layer architectural approach and a five-rule decisioning framework to ensure safety, reliability, and human trust in healthcare AI deployments.

The Three Non-Negotiable Foundations

  • Key claims:

Code Layer vs. Model Layer (Layer 1 and Layer 2)

  • Explanation: Detailed breakdown of enforcing policy via architecture and placing must-not-fail behaviors in the code layer above prompts.
  • Key claims:
    • A model is not a guardrail, and a system prompt is not code

    • Every turn goes through the code layer first before reaching the model

    • Emergency escalation routing for self-harm or suicidal ideation directly to 911 or 988 in code

Safety as a Continuous Evaluation Layer (Layer 3)

  • Explanation: Why pre-launch checklists are insufficient and how production automated judges, member feedback, and sampled traces maintain safety over time.
  • Key claims:

The Decisioning Framework (5 Rules for Stakeholder Disagreements)

  • Explanation: A structured framework for resolving launch disagreements across clinical, legal, compliance, product, and engineering stakeholders.
  • Key claims:
    • Worst case wins: Severity is set by the worst plausible outcome, not the average
    • Severity does not equal capacity: A bug's severity is determined by harm, not team capacity
    • Asymmetric default: When in doubt, default to the safer mistake
    • Revealed tolerance beats stated tolerance: Align with actual accepted production behavior
    • Humans are the constraint: Always design for the human in the loop
  • Examples:
    • Comparing safety bugs (always hold and fix) versus polished bugs (ship)
    • Evaluating clinical risk vs velocity risk prior to launch
  • Terminology:
    • worst case
    • asymmetric default
    • revealed tolerance
    • human in the loop
  • Why it matters: Resolves organizational deadlock when five different stakeholders see five different risks during product launches.

Key Points

Constraint is the Architecture

  • Explanation: Safety failures stem from architectural design choices made before model deployment, not just model failures.
  • Evidence: ECRI ranking AI chatbot misuse as the top health tech hazard for 2026.
  • Practical implication: Architect systems so that PHI is stripped at ingestion and environments are strictly isolated.

Must-Not-Fail Belongs Above the Prompt

  • Explanation: Probabilistic models are unreliable for deterministic business logic and critical safety checks.
  • Evidence: Code layer runs first on every turn; models never get a vote on high-stakes calls.
  • Practical implication: Implement emergency routing and authentication checks in deterministic code rather than system prompts.

Safety is Continuous, Not a Gate

  • Explanation: Pre-launch evaluation checklists are necessary but insufficient for production AI systems.
  • Evidence: New model versions and prompt changes introduce new failures over time.
  • Practical implication: Deploy continuous automated judges, live member feedback monitoring, and 100% sampled traces.

Worst Case Wins in Healthcare Decisions

  • Explanation: When evaluating bug severity, the worst plausible outcome sets the standard, not the average case.
  • Evidence: A bug causing serious harm to 0.1% of users is much worse than a minor annoyance to 100%.
  • Practical implication: Never compromise on safety bugs for velocity; treat fast-follows as committed debt.

Frameworks, Models & Processes

Three-Layer Architecture for Member-Facing Health AI

  • How it works: Combines policy-enforcing architecture, deterministic code layers for high-stakes logic, and continuous safety evaluation layers in production.
  • Components:
    • Layer 1: Pipeline sanitization and environment isolation
    • Layer 2: Code layer running above prompts for deterministic safety rules
    • Layer 3: Continuous evaluation layer with automated judges, member feedback, and sampled traces
  • When to use: When building member-facing healthcare AI or any high-stakes regulated AI application.

The Five-Rule Decisioning Framework

  • How it works: A decision-making model to resolve launch blockages when cross-functional stakeholders disagree on risk.
  • Components:
    • Rule 1: Worst case wins
    • Rule 2: Severity does not equal capacity
    • Rule 3: Asymmetric default (safety first)
    • Rule 4: Revealed tolerance over stated tolerance
    • Rule 5: Humans are the constraint
  • When to use: When deciding whether to ship, delay, or fix features when clinical, legal, compliance, product, and engineering stakeholders disagree.

Deep dives

5

A classification rubric for must-not-fail decisions

Research question

Can every decision point in an agent loop be classified as deterministic-mandatory or probabilistic-permitted using only two axes — reversibility of the action and severity of the plausible worst outcome — and does that rubric reproduce expert judgment on real incident data?

Why

The talk asserts must-not-fail rules belong in code but never says how to recognize one. Without a rubric, teams either over-constrain (hard-coding everything, losing model flexibility) or under-constrain (trusting prompts for irreversible actions). A falsifiable rubric turns 'constraint as architecture' from a slogan into an auditable checklist applied to every branch point before a single token is generated.

Source video ↗

Human review capacity as the binding constraint on continuous evaluation

Research question

What reviewer-to-traced-conversation ratio and triage SLA are required before a continuous eval layer changes outcomes rather than accumulating unread signals, and how does that ratio scale with judge count?

Why

Automated judges scale detection linearly and cheaply; remediation does not. If the eval layer's signal volume exceeds review throughput, safety silently degrades while dashboards look green — the failure mode is invisible precisely because every individual component is working. Deriving staffing curves converts an operational afterthought into an architectural input that caps how many judges are worth running.

Source video ↗

Judge reliability on escalation errors: false negatives against clinical review

Research question

What is the judge-vs-clinician agreement rate on sampled high-stakes traces, and specifically what fraction of escalation failures (under-triage of emergencies) do automated judges miss?

Why

A continuous eval layer is only as trustworthy as its worst false-negative class. Judges checking 'safety' and 'escalation correctness' may agree with clinicians on tone and general accuracy while systematically missing the exact tail cases that make the severity standard necessary. Measuring the miss rate — not aggregate agreement — determines whether judges can be load-bearing or must stay advisory.

Source video ↗

The measured cost of worst-case severity gating

Research question

How many release-days does worst-case severity gating cost per quarter, and how many severe incidents does it prevent relative to an aggregate-quality release standard?

Why

The rule is stated prescriptively with illustrative comparisons but no cost data, so it survives partly because the velocity loss is unmetered. If blocked-release duration and hold-time are never tracked, 'never compromise on safety bugs for velocity' becomes an unfalsifiable commitment that erodes quietly under delivery pressure the first time it is expensive.

Source video ↗

Costs of pushing compliance to data-flow seams

Research question

What capability loss results from stripping PHI at the pipeline boundary and hard-isolating production from non-production, and can that loss be quantified in debugging time and model quality?

Why

Boundary enforcement bounds regulatory exposure structurally, but the price is paid later and less visibly: fewer debuggable traces, less representative training data, and slower reproduction of production failures. Naming and measuring that price is what keeps the boundary decision an engineering tradeoff rather than an unquestioned rule.

Article ideas

4

Your Model Is Not a Guardrail: Why Safety Has to Be Code That Runs Before the Prompt

Any safety control expressed as a system prompt is probabilistic by construction and therefore not a control at all; must-not-fail behaviors such as crisis escalation, identity verification, and intent routing must be deterministic branches that execute before model invocation and can short-circuit it entirely.

Angle

Contrast the two architectures with the concrete failure case — a model under-triaging a life-threatening emergency — and show that the fix is a code branch, not a better prompt or a stronger model.

Source video ↗

The Eval Dashboard Nobody Reads: Human Review Is the Real Safety Bottleneck

Continuous automated judging does not make a system safer when signal volume outruns remediation capacity; safety architecture must treat reviewer headcount and triage SLAs as design inputs and cap judge coverage to what the team can actually act on.

Angle

Invert the usual framing: instead of asking how much to monitor, ask how much the team can respond to, and design detection backwards from that number.

Source video ↗

Worst Case Wins: Release Gating for Tail Risk Instead of Average Quality

Aggregate quality metrics systematically average away the harms that matter; release decisions should be driven by the severity distribution's tail, with a separate non-negotiable path where any plausible severe outcome holds the release regardless of mean score or velocity cost.

Angle

Make the counterintuitive comparison concrete — 0.1% serious harm versus 100% annoyance — then argue that the standard only holds if its velocity cost is measured rather than left implicit.

Source video ↗

Constraint Is Architecture: Enforce Policy at Seams, Not at the Model

Most AI safety failures are decided at design time — at the pipeline boundary where PHI is stripped and at the environment seam where production is isolated — because those are the only points where enforcement is deterministic, cheap, and auditable regardless of model behavior.

Angle

Trace a single data record from ingress to inference and mark every point where policy could be enforced, showing that only the seam-level checks survive model changes.

Project ideas

4

Must-Not-Fail Router

gatehouse

A deterministic pre-model gate that classifies each turn against a reversibility/harm rubric and short-circuits high-stakes cases to fixed handlers reduces catastrophic misroutes on a red-team suite to zero, at the cost of a bounded latency overhead and a measurable false-escalation rate.

Proof of concept

Build a thin pre-model layer that intercepts every request before LLM invocation, routes self-harm, emergency, identity, and intent cases to hard-coded handlers, and passes everything else through. Run it against a prompt-injection and jailbreak corpus plus a set of emergency under-triage scenarios, comparing gated versus prompt-only configurations.

Measurement

Catastrophic misroute rate (target: 0 on the adversarial suite), p95 added latency in milliseconds, false-escalation rate versus baseline, and injection success rate against emergency and auth branches.

Source video ↗

Escalation Judge Certification Harness

beyond-evals

Automated judges calibrated on aggregate safety agreement will miss a materially higher fraction of escalation failures than they do of general accuracy errors, so judge false-negative rate on escalation must be measured separately before judges are treated as load-bearing.

Proof of concept

Assemble a sampled set of high-stakes traces with clinician labels, score them with several judges across accuracy, safety, and escalation dimensions, and decompose agreement by error class, isolating under-triage misses.

Measurement

Judge-vs-clinician agreement overall and per error class, escalation false-negative rate, and the gap between aggregate agreement and escalation-specific agreement.

Source video ↗

Triage Capacity Planner

new

Signal-to-action latency, not detection volume, predicts whether a continuous eval layer prevents incidents; beyond a threshold judge-to-reviewer ratio, additional judges add review backlog with no reduction in time-to-remediation.

Proof of concept

Instrument an eval pipeline end-to-end with timestamps from signal creation through reviewer action, then simulate increasing judge coverage per 10k sampled traces and measure backlog growth and time-to-action.

Measurement

Signals generated per 10k traces, reviewer throughput, median and p95 signal-to-action latency, and unattended-signal backlog as judge coverage scales.

Source video ↗

Decision-Point Reversibility Audit

movement-lab

Applying a two-axis rubric (reversibility, worst-case severity) to every branch point in an existing agent yields a defensible split between deterministic and probabilistic decisions, and identifies branches currently delegated to prompts that expert reviewers agree must be code.

Proof of concept

Enumerate all decision points in a running agent, score each axis independently, have reviewers classify the same points blind, and compare the rubric's assignments against expert judgment and against the current implementation.

Measurement

Classification agreement with expert reviewers, count of must-not-fail decisions currently enforced only by prompt or model behavior, and remediation effort per reclassified branch.

Architectural implications

3

Safety-critical decisions are moved out of the prompt and into a deterministic pre-model layer.

Before

System prompts and model instructions are trusted to enforce escalation, authentication, and routing rules.

After

Every turn passes through deterministic code that handles intent routing, identity verification, and crisis escalation (e.g., routing self-harm signals to 911/988) before the model is called.

Consequence

Prompt injection and probabilistic drift can no longer override hard policy, but the code layer becomes a critical-path component requiring its own testing, latency budget, and change control.

Source video ↗

Evaluation is promoted from a pre-launch checklist to a continuously running production subsystem.

Before

A launch gate reviews quality once; subsequent model or prompt changes ship with limited regression coverage.

After

Scheduled automated judges run every few hours over live traffic on clinical accuracy, safety, and escalation correctness, alongside per-message feedback and 100% sampling of high-stakes cases.

Consequence

New failure modes introduced by version changes are caught in production trends, but the team must staff human reviewers to read and act on the resulting signals.

Source video ↗

Compliance boundaries are enforced at data-flow seams rather than at the model.

Before

PHI handling and environment separation are treated as policy or downstream filtering concerns.

After

PHI is stripped at the pipeline boundary before data reaches the data lake, and production/non-production environments are hard-isolated.

Consequence

Regulatory exposure is bounded structurally and independently of model behavior, at the cost of reduced data richness for training and debugging.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Continuous judging vs. human review capacity

Benefit

Automated judges on live traffic catch post-launch regressions introduced by model or prompt changes.

Cost or risk

Signal volume can outrun the team's ability to read and act, making the eval layer performative.

Source video ↗

Deterministic gating vs. model flexibility

Benefit

Hard-coded routing and escalation make must-not-fail behaviors non-negotiable and injection-resistant.

Cost or risk

Every high-stakes case must be expressible as a deterministic rule; unusual cases may be misrouted by code that cannot reason about context.

Source video ↗

Worst-case severity standard vs. delivery velocity

Benefit

Tail-risk harm is never averaged away and safety bugs block release.

Cost or risk

Release velocity degrades whenever any low-probability severe outcome exists, and fast-follows become committed debt.

Reliance on probabilistic triage for consumer health queries

Benefit

Frontier models give broad, conversational coverage of health questions used by tens of millions of people.

Cost or risk

Under-triaging life-threatening emergencies is a documented, measurable failure mode of the same generative behavior that makes the system useful.
