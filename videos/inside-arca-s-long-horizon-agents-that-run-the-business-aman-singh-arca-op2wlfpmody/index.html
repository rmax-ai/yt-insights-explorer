Overview

  • Speaker: Aman Singh

  • Main topic: Building and operating long-horizon AI agents that run financial services at Arca
  • Purpose: To share engineering insights and architectural patterns for deploying autonomous long-horizon AI agents in financial services. Aman Singh, Lead AI Engineer at Arca, explains how Arca operates as an AI-native financial services firm where AI agents run the business rather than just assist it. He details the difference between short-running tasks and long-horizon work spanning days, weeks, or months, and outlines the core primitives—signals, operational context, harnesses, and people integration—used to build reliable, scalable agent-driven financial operations.

Key Points

Arca is not a SaaS company

  • Explanation: Arca does not sell software to other businesses; Arca is the financial services firm itself running operations using applied AI.
  • Evidence: We're an applied AI company that happens to be running a services business in this industry.
  • Practical implication: Companies building AI-native operations can capture service margins rather than just software subscription revenue.

Signals close context gaps

  • Explanation: Background agents continuously monitor emails, phone calls, documents, meetings, custodian feeds, and market data to keep agents updated.
  • Evidence: Every email, call, document and event, from inside the firm and out.
  • Practical implication: Autonomous agents require real-time ingestion of multi-modal firm data to maintain situational awareness.

Agents prompt people, not people prompt agents

  • Explanation: Instead of humans prompting an LLM chat interface, agents execute 90% of the work and pull humans in only when approval or human execution is required.
  • Evidence: Agents prompt people more than people prompt agents.
  • Practical implication: Inverts the standard co-pilot interaction model into an autonomous workflow model.

[O]Mental Modelhigh noveltystrong evidence

Long-horizon work is distinct from long-running work: it is a stop-start workflow spanning days with many actors, decision points, and handoffs, not continuous token generation. This implies the core engineering problem is durable state and re-entry, not long context or long inference sessions.

Why it matters

Most agent runtimes are optimized for a single continuous loop. If real enterprise work is stop-start over days, the runtime's primary job becomes persisting intent, waiting on external events, and resuming correctly — a workflow/durability problem, not an inference problem.

Generalization

Any agent task that spans multiple days or crosses organizational boundaries should be modeled as a durable, resumable workflow with explicit decision points rather than a long prompt chain.

[A]Architecturehigh noveltymoderate evidence

The interaction model is inverted: agents do ~90% of the work and prompt humans at specific approval/execution points, rather than humans driving a chat interface. The human becomes a step in the agent's workflow, not the workflow's operator.

Why it matters

This relocates human-in-the-loop from the front-end entry point to an approval/notification primitive inside the orchestrator, which changes how you design UX, permissions, audit trails, and blocking vs non-blocking handoffs.

Generalization

For autonomous systems, treat human input as an async, addressable service the agent calls (request + wait + resume), not as the initiating client of the system.

Research question

What runtime abstractions—checkpointed plan state, event-sourced memory, idempotent resumption, and signal-triggered wakeups—are minimally required for multi-day agent workflows, and how do they compare with existing durable workflow engines?

Why

If enterprise work is stop-start over days, the central engineering problem is state durability and correct re-entry, not longer context or longer inference sessions. This determines the runtime architecture, failure recovery model, and auditability of autonomous operations.

Source video ↗

Human approval as an asynchronous, addressable service in the agent loop

Research question

How should human approval be modeled as an async service with routing, ownership, escalation, timeout behavior, and audit records when agents, not humans, initiate most interactions?

Why

Moving human-in-the-loop from a chat front end to a callee inside the orchestrator changes UX, permissions, audit trails, and blocking semantics. Without this inversion, teams will design approval flows around the wrong control flow.

Source video ↗

Signal provenance, deduplication, and conflict resolution for in-flight plans

Research question

How can multimodal organizational exhaust—emails, calls, documents, meeting notes, CRM remarks, custodian feeds, and market moves—be converted into durable signals with provenance, recency weighting, and explicit plan-revision records without corrupting active agent plans?

Why

Signals are a correctness-critical control channel, not just an ingestion feature. Naive signal appends can mutate multi-day plans with stale, misparsed, or contradictory context, producing confident execution of wrong work.

Source video ↗

Checkpoint-level evaluation of multi-day agent plans

Research question

How can multi-day agent workflows be evaluated before completion, when failure may only surface days later at a handoff, using replayable scenarios, injected signals, mocked external systems, and checkpoint-level plan-correctness scoring?

Why

Final-output evals are insufficient for long-horizon agents because the dangerous errors are plan deviations that only become visible after several days and multiple handoffs. Evaluation must move inside the workflow, not stay at the answer boundary.

Source video ↗

Harness/sandbox separation and autonomy thresholds for irreversible actions

Research question

How should the harness—tools, guardrails, persistent memory—be separated from sandboxed execution so that autonomy versus escalation thresholds can be classified by reversibility and blast radius, especially for financial actions like asset liquidation and transfers?

Why

Splitting policy/context from execution gives a fault boundary for irreversible side effects and a place to enforce guardrails independently of model reasoning. The policy for when an agent acts autonomously versus escalates remains an open design problem.

Teams building enterprise agents are optimizing the wrong axis when they treat multi-day work as a longer inference session; stop-start workflows with actors, handoffs, and external waits require durable execution primitives—checkpoints, signal wakeups, and idempotent resume—and architectures without them will fail in production regardless of model quality.

Your Agents Should Prompt You: The HITL Inversion That Breaks Chat-First Design

If agents do most of the work and call humans only for approval or execution, then human-in-the-loop must be redesigned as an asynchronous approval service with routing, escalation, timeout handling, and audit records; keeping chat as the primary human interface creates reliability and compliance ceilings.

Angle

Show how control flow, permissions, and UX requirements change when humans are callees rather than callers in the agent workflow.

CRM Notes Are Control Inputs: The Hidden Correctness Problem in Signal-Driven Agents

Ingesting organizational exhaust as signals is not a convenience feature but a correctness-critical control channel; without provenance, deduplication, recency weighting, and explicit plan-revision records, agents will confidently execute stale or contradictory plans.

Angle

Trace how an offhand CRM note or missed email can mutate a multi-day agent plan, then propose a signal-to-plan attribution and conflict-resolution design.

Stop Evaluating Final Answers for Multi-Day Agents

Single-turn and final-output evaluations are structurally inadequate for long-horizon agents because the critical failures occur as plan deviations days before completion; checkpoint-level plan-correctness scoring with injected signals and mocked external systems should become the minimum viable evaluation method.

Angle

Argue for replayable multi-day workflow benchmarks that score the plan state at each checkpoint, not just the final answer.

new

Architectural implications

4

Durable execution replaces continuous inference as the runtime core for multi-day tasks.

Before

Agent loop holds state in context and runs until completion.

After

Workflow engine externalizes plan, memory, and pending decision points; agents sleep and are woken by signals.

Consequence

Reliability requirements shift to state persistence, idempotent resumption, and replay — the same problems as distributed workflow systems.

Source video ↗

Human-in-the-loop becomes an async approval service rather than a chat front end.

Before

Human opens chat, describes task, reviews output.

After

Agent initiates a request with full context to a specific person and blocks on a decision; human responds asynchronously.

Consequence

Needs routing/ownership logic, escalation policies, and an audit record of who approved what and when.

Source video ↗

A signal ingestion layer sits upstream of all agents.

Before

Agents act only on explicit prompts.

After

Every email, call, document, meeting note, custodian feed, and market move becomes an event that can create or revise agent work.

Consequence

Ingestion, deduplication, and signal-to-plan attribution become critical infrastructure; noisy signals become a correctness risk.

Source video ↗

Operational context must be formalized before agents can use it.

Before

Institutional knowledge lives in people and ad hoc processes.

After

Firm knowledge and client history are structured so agents can retrieve and act on them.

Consequence

Workflow standardization becomes a prerequisite engineering task, not a documentation chore.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

3

Autonomy vs irreversible financial error

Benefit

Agents can execute multi-step money movement (liquidate assets, settle, transfer) without waiting on a human.

Cost or risk

Hallucination or edge-case errors in irreversible actions like asset liquidation; sandboxing reduces but does not eliminate real-world effect risk.

Source video ↗

Latency vs approval guardrails

Benefit

Removing humans from the critical path compresses days to minutes.

Cost or risk

Fewer human checkpoints means guardrails must be encoded in the harness, including for cross-department task routing.

Source video ↗

Broad signal ingestion vs state noise

Benefit

Agents watch the world via signals so humans don't have to babysit workflows.

Cost or risk

Unstructured human inputs (e.g., an offhand remark logged in a CRM) mutate agent plans, introducing stale or misparsed context that propagates across days.
