[E]Empirical Resulthigh noveltymoderate evidence

Frontier model cost to complete a single robot subtask fell from $2.36 (July) to $0.38 (September), an 84% drop in two months, i.e. roughly a 3-week halving period. The unit of measurement is cost per completed subtask, not per token or per FLOP.

Why it matters

Tracking cost-per-completed-task rather than cost-per-token is the metric that determines whether an agentic robotics or automation loop is economically viable. At a 3-week halving rate, a subtask that costs dollars today is effectively free within a year, which invalidates any architecture whose design is constrained by inference cost of the current model generation.

Generalization

For any agent system, instrument cost-per-successful-subtask (including retries and failures) as a first-class curve, not a static budget assumption. Decisions about model tiering, retry budgets, and verification depth should be revisited on the cadence of that curve, not annually.

Generalization

Default architecture should be one generalist model plus domain-specific tooling and context; add a specialist only when a measured comparison on both success rate and cost-per-task justifies it.

Decomposing the 3-week cost halving: price, token efficiency, or pass-rate

Research question

If cost-per-successful-subtask is held at a frozen task suite and frozen success threshold, how much of the observed 84% drop (July to September) is attributable to model price changes, to tokens/actions per episode, and to pass-rate improvement?

Why

The headline halving rate is the single number that justifies or invalidates retry-heavy, verification-heavy agent architectures. If most of the drop comes from pass-rate rather than price, the correct engineering response is better verification, not cheaper batching; if it comes from price, the response is dynamic retry budgets. Without the decomposition the number is unusable for architecture decisions.

Alignment as orchestration-layer mechanism design

Research question

Can differing scoring, selection, and elimination rules in a competitive multi-agent system be shown to produce measurably different adversarial versus prosocial equilibria, holding the base model and prompts constant?

Why

If alignment behavior is dominated by the selection mechanism rather than the per-agent prompt, then guardrail-only designs are structurally insufficient and the reward/ranking layer becomes a first-class system component requiring its own evaluation. This is the difference between reviewing individual agent outputs and reviewing the tournament the agents are playing in.

Attributable intermediate artifacts in long-horizon code agents

Research question

Does inserting a machine-generated, human-reviewable technical specification between codebase ingestion and code generation reduce rerun cost and localize defect attribution, compared with a direct context-in/code-out pipeline?

Why

A spec boundary converts an opaque generate-and-pray loop into a pipeline where failure localizes to spec generation versus implementation and reruns start from the spec rather than re-ingesting the codebase. It also makes the claimed 80% autonomy figure auditable, since review effort at the spec layer and the diff layer can be measured separately.

The generalist-versus-specialist crossover on cost-per-success

Research question

At what task volume and difficulty does a generalist frontier model beat a fine-tuned specialist on cost-per-successful-task, and does the crossover observed on a public benchmark tier hold in a matched off-benchmark deployment?

Why

The answer determines whether specialization effort is positive or negative work. If the crossover is stable and cost-normalized, per-task fine-tunes are a maintenance liability; if the specialist wins in the high-volume narrow tail, the harness must support routing to specialists rather than a single model.

A Benchmark Without a Price Tag Is Not a Deployment Decision

Leaderboards that rank models on success rate alone cannot answer the only operational question teams have — which model to run — and the shift toward cost-normalized comparisons on named task tiers makes a whole class of 'we picked the best model' decisions retroactively unjustifiable.

Angle

Take the reported 84% cost drop and show that a pure success-rate ranking would have produced the same model choice before and after the cost collapse, despite the deployment economics having inverted.

Alignment Is a Scoring Bug, Not a Prompt Bug

If agents and humans are both trained to win, then alignment failures emerge from the selection mechanism — who is scored on what, and what happens to losers — and system prompts and content filters cannot fix an objective that is embedded in a tournament.

Angle

Pair the claim that humanity lacks alignment among itself with the historical prize examples to argue that outcome-defined rewards shift where misalignment can occur rather than removing it, and that orchestration-layer scoring must itself be evaluated.

Source video ↗

When Inference Becomes Free, Retries Become the Failure Mode

A three-week halving curve destroys the implicit budget that currently bounds agent loops; the constraint on agent design migrates from spend to latency, tool-call storms, and unbounded self-verification, which means cost-based guardrails are the wrong control surface going forward.

Angle

Argue that near-zero marginal cost per subtask permits aggressive retry and multi-sample strategies but removes the economic signal that kept runaway loops in check, so new boundedness must come from explicit step budgets and latency budgets.

Put a Reviewable Artifact in the Middle of Your Code Agent

The most valuable structural choice in a long-horizon code agent is not the model but the placement of a generated specification between ingestion and generation, because it converts an unattributable failure into a localizable one and makes the human's residual share of the work measurable rather than rhetorical.

Angle

Subtask Cost Curve Harness

beyond-evals

Measuring cost-per-successful-subtask (including retries and failures) on a frozen task suite produces a curve steep enough that a routing and retry policy tuned at one point in time becomes measurably suboptimal within one model release cycle.

Proof of concept

Build a harness that runs a fixed task suite against successive model versions, logs success/failure, retries, tokens/actions per episode, and computes cost-per-successful-subtask with confidence intervals; replay historical runs to reconstruct the curve and re-tune a routing threshold at each point.

Measurement

Change in cost-per-successful-subtask across model versions at constant task suite and constant success threshold, decomposed into price, action-count, and pass-rate components; measured regret of policies tuned at t versus re-tuned at t+1.

Source video ↗

Incentive Sandbox for Multi-Agent Systems

gatehouse

Holding base model and prompts constant, changing only the scoring and selection rules of a competitive multi-agent setup will produce measurably different rates of adversarial behavior against a held-out evaluation rubric.

Proof of concept

Implement a small multi-agent task (e.g. resource allocation under a shared budget) with a pluggable scoring/selection mechanism: winner-take-all versus proportional reward versus reward-with-penalty-for-rubric-violations. Run identical seeds across mechanisms and log inter-agent actions classified as cooperative or adversarial.

Measurement

Rate of rubric-violating actions and outcome quality per mechanism, with the base model and system prompts held fixed and only the selection rules varied.

Source video ↗

Spec-Boundary Code Agent

movement-lab

Inserting a machine-generated, human-reviewable specification between codebase ingestion and code generation reduces the cost of a failed change (rerun cost) and improves defect attribution versus a direct context-in/code-out pipeline.

Proof of concept

Build a two-stage pipeline: stage one ingests a repository and emits a reviewable spec plus acceptance tests; stage two generates and pre-compiles code against that spec. Force a class of induced failure (ambiguous requirement) and compare recovery cost between spec-first and direct pipelines.

Measurement

Rerun cost per failed change, tokens/context re-ingested per recovery, fraction of failures correctly attributed to spec versus implementation, and human review minutes at each layer.

Source video ↗

Crossover Bench: Generalist vs Specialist at Constant Cost Axis

beyond-evals

On narrow high-volume task tiers a fine-tuned specialist retains a cost-per-success advantage over a generalist frontier model, even where the generalist wins on raw success rate at high difficulty.

Proof of concept

Select one narrow, high-volume task tier and one broad, high-difficulty tier; evaluate a generalist frontier model and a fine-tuned specialist on both, scoring success rate, cost per successful episode, latency, and tail failure rate on the same harness.

Measurement

Cost-per-success ratio and success-rate delta between generalist and specialist per tier, plus tail failure rate, to locate the task volume threshold at which routing to a specialist is justified.

Tradeoffs and failure modes

4

Generalist versus specialized models

Benefit

Generalist models transfer across domains and reduce the number of models, pipelines, and eval suites to maintain.

Cost or risk

A specialist may still win on cost or latency for narrow, high-volume tasks; migrating off a specialist on the strength of a single leaderboard risks regressions in the tail.

Source video ↗

Collapsing inference cost removes the implicit budget on retries

Benefit

Near-zero marginal cost per subtask permits aggressive retry, self-verification, and multi-sample strategies.

Cost or risk

The economic signal that previously bounded runaway loops disappears; unbounded retries, tool-call storms, and latency amplification become the dominant failure mode instead of spend.

Source video ↗

Human review of the residual 20% in autonomous SDLC

Benefit

Human judgment applied where automation is weakest acts as a correctness gate on high-risk changes.

Cost or risk

The human becomes the throughput bottleneck and the architecture's effective ceiling; the '80% autonomous' figure says nothing about review time per change.

Source video ↗

Incentive-based alignment

Benefit

Outcome-defined rewards can attract solutions and behavior outside the designer's prescribed path.

Cost or risk

Reward-driven agents optimize the proxy rather than the intent, and the surrounding competitive incentives (geopolitical, corporate) can dominate any locally well-designed reward.
